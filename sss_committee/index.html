Maricopa Community Colleges District · AI Resource Committee

Student Support and Success

A district-wide process improvement initiative across all 10 colleges
A committee initiative to identify where AI can streamline student service operations and free staff time for direct student support. Co-chair role since spring 2026.
Context

The Maricopa Community Colleges District serves more than 140,000 students annually across 10 colleges. Each college has built its own student services infrastructure over decades — advising, counseling, basic needs, tutoring, financial aid, career services, and dozens of cohort-specific programs. The services exist. The questions worth asking are whether students can find them, whether staff have the time and tools to deliver them well, and whether the district's AI investment can make both of those better.

AIRC Domain 5 is the committee chartered to answer those questions, develop a roadmap, and recommend where the district should invest in AI for student-facing and staff-facing systems. The work is just beginning.

The Questions Driving This Work

Services exist. The challenge is connection.

Two related problems shape this committee's charge.

First, students often don't recognize their situation as something a service was built for. A student behind because of ADHD may not think to ask Disability Resources. A student behind because of childcare may not think of Basic Needs. A student struggling with research and writing may not know the library or writing center can help. Most students assume they need to push through alone, and the help that could have changed their semester sits unused.

Second, the staff who run those services would benefit from AI tools that streamline their day-to-day work, but many don't know what's possible or where to start. Caseloads are full and the time to investigate new tools doesn't exist. Both sides of this gap have to be addressed for AI investment to actually improve student success.

Through structured fieldwork in 2026–2027, the questions worth answering include:

  • How do students discover the help that exists in ways that match their actual situations, rather than the names the services happen to use?
  • What kinds of AI tools would actually help the staff running these services, and what support do they need to describe what they could use?
  • District-licensed systems coexist with campus-specific tools and local workflows. How does this combined environment actually function across the 10 colleges?
  • Each department holds its own data on usage and outcomes. What would a shared baseline look like, and how could it be built without compromising department ownership?
  • AI investment without a shared understanding of the underlying systems risks duplicating effort, building point solutions that don't scale, or undermining staff trust. What sequencing avoids those risks?
The Approach

A three-phase methodology, scoped across 2026–2027.

The committee operates on the principle that good recommendations require good evidence. Discovery and diagnosis come before solutioning. Each phase produces artifacts that inform the next.

Phase 1
Discovery
Summer 2026

District-wide inventory of tools and services. Draft crosswalk of service functions across all 10 colleges, verified through campus representatives. Baseline data collection coordinated through department managers, with data ownership remaining at the department level.

Phase 2
Diagnosis
Fall 2026

Parallel usability studies and staff workflow audits at sample campuses. Test student accounts walk through the full lifecycle from application to workforce, documenting friction points from the student's perspective. The goal is to learn how staff and students actually experience the systems, not to assume.

Phase 3
Recommendations
Winter 2026 / Spring 2027

Prioritized roadmap of AI opportunities — features in tools already licensed, new builds where they would create real impact, and the staff support needed for adoption to succeed.

Why this sequencing matters

Most AI initiatives in higher education start with solutions and look for problems. This one starts with the work itself — what student services actually do, what students actually experience, and where the friction actually lives. Recommendations are stronger and adoption is more durable when they come out of evidence rather than assumption.

Intended Outcomes

What success would look like at the district level.

A verified service crosswalk and tool inventory that lets the district build once and deploy in ways that preserve local identity.
Baseline metrics across all 10 colleges on usage, referral sources, and workload, owned by the departments that generate them.
A prioritized AI roadmap sequenced by impact and feasibility, grounded in fieldwork rather than feature lists.
A repeatable methodology other AIRC domains could adopt for their own discovery and diagnosis work.
More student contact, not less. Every recommendation evaluated against whether it expands or reduces human-to-human support.
Staff trust in the process, built by listening to what staff describe before recommending what they should adopt.
