# GCC AI Practitioners, Community of Practice Launch Kit

Written Aug 3, 2026. Everything you need to launch the CoP: a mission statement, the group&rsquo;s identity, the meeting cadence, the meet-and-greet agenda, the goals-meeting agenda, and a ready-to-send launch email. Edit freely.

**Goal.** Stand up a faculty and staff community of practice for the people already building and using AI at GCC, so good practice spreads by example, and so the group produces real artifacts (a publishable piece on authentic assessment, shared builds, a conference session) rather than just meeting.

**Audience.** GCC faculty and staff who are already practitioners with AI: building tools, using it in teaching or student support, experimenting. Not a training series for people who have never touched it.

**Process.** Two meetings a month, alternating on campus and online, each with a focused topic and a show-and-tell. Members share what they are doing, collaborate on real projects, attend conferences and learning together, and publish. Light structure, practitioner-led.

---

## The group in one line
We are the campus&rsquo;s AI practitioners: the people already putting AI to work in teaching, student support, and design. We meet to learn from each other, build together, and lead the campus by example, not by mandate.

**Important framing (hold this):** this is a group of practitioners and innovators, not a training program for non-users, and not a committee that tells people what to do. We share insight, collaborate on projects, and are the innovative force on campus. Nobody is being trained or corrected.

## Mission statement (pick one, or blend)
- **Option A (full):** The GCC AI Practitioners is a community of practice for faculty and staff already building and using AI in their work. We meet to learn from one another, collaborate on real projects, present our work, and act as the campus&rsquo;s innovative force in applying AI to teaching, student support, and design, so effective, responsible practice spreads by example.
- **Option B (short):** A community of practice for GCC faculty and staff already putting AI to work, meeting to share what we are doing, build together, and lead the campus by example.

## Cadence and norms
- **Two meetings per month.** Alternate on campus and online so everyone can join.
- Each meeting has **one focused topic** and a **show-and-tell** slot so someone shares what they are trying.
- High-level sharing, not step-by-step tutorials. We are here to spark ideas and collaborate, not to run workshops.
- Notes and links live in one shared place (decide where at the first meeting).

---

## Meeting 1: meet and greet (about 50 minutes)
The first meeting is only about meeting each other and shaping the group. No goal-setting yet.

1. **Welcome and why we are here** (5 min). This is a practitioner community, not a training. We share, build, and lead by example.
2. **Around the room** (20 min). Each person: who you are, what you are already doing with AI, and one thing you would love this group to help you with.
3. **The shape of the group** (10 min). Practitioners sharing and building, cadence of two meetings a month, on-campus and online, show-and-tell each time, notes in one place.
4. **A teaser for next time** (10 min). Name a few things we *could* work on together (see the candidate goals below) so people come to meeting two ready to prioritize. Do not decide yet.
5. **Logistics and close** (5 min). Set the recurring meeting time, the communication channel, and the date of meeting two.

## Meeting 2: set our goals (about 50 minutes)
1. **Recap and norms** (5 min).
2. **Put the candidate goals on the table** (30 min). Discuss each, then prioritize. Candidate goals to seed the conversation:
   - **Advance student and employee AI literacy on campus.** This is an ARC (district AI Resource Center) goal that nobody on our campus is currently rolling out. We are positioned to pilot it.
   - **Collaborative research on authentic assessment in the age of AI**, aimed at producing a publishable piece (article, conference paper, or open white paper once there is data). Proposed as the group&rsquo;s year topic.
   - **Present together at a conference.** Submit a panel or session as a group.
   - **Collaborate on the real AI builds members are already making**, sharing and improving each other&rsquo;s tools.
   - **A running show-and-tell and a shared knowledge base**, so what any of us learns is captured for all of us.
3. **Pick one or two primary goals for the year**, plus a first concrete step for each and who convenes it.
4. **Assign light structure** (who convenes, where notes live) and close.

**Scope rule (so the group produces, not just meets):** aim to end the year with at least one publishable piece plus two or three collaborative builds or pilots. Every meeting topic should ladder up to one of the chosen goals.

## Write the boundary before meeting one

## The operating outline

### First meeting, 60 minutes

- **5 min.** Why this exists and what it is not. Read the three boundary sentences.
- **20 min.** Three members demo something real, 5 to 7 minutes each. **Recruit these three in advance.** Do not ask for volunteers in the room.
- **20 min.** Open discussion on one contextualizing question. Every Learner’s: “What issues would this solve in your area?”
- **10 min.** Co-write the vision statement live in the shared doc. Every Learner recommends this specifically as a launch activity, not a pre-written thing you present.
- **5 min.** Name the first artifact. Ask who wants a low-stakes role: note taker, resource curator, next month’s demo.

Steal Cleveland State’s invitation line, it does more work than a mission statement: *“a space to talk about AI, get some clarity, exchange ideas, or even just vent.”*

### First 90 days

- **Fix the slot and never move it.** Same day of month, same hour, hybrid. Monthly at 60 minutes is the overwhelming default across every verified CoP.
- **Stand up two things only.** A capture form and a place to talk. Nothing else. See the software section.
- **Rotate the host from month two.** This is the burnout answer, and it is also how you find out who your college liaisons are.
- **Publish a structured recap after every session.** Highest-leverage habit on this list. It is the durable artifact, the recruiting pitch, and your FEP evidence, all from one hour of writing. Columbia’s template: What’s New, Why It Matters, Session Highlights, Key Discussion Points, Resources, Takeaway.
- **By meeting three, name the artifact the group is building.** Every Learner is firm that this is required, not optional. The obvious candidate for you is a shared no-PII AI use-case inventory across colleges, which also feeds the ARC work you already owe.
- **Set the 6 to 12 month success definition.** Arizona’s is refreshingly small and worth copying: “3 identified use cases.”

### First year

- **Months 4 to 6.** Split into subgroups once whole-group discussion gets too broad to be useful. For you the cut is student-facing services versus classroom.
- **Month 6.** Quarterly review, three questions only. What is working? What is not? What should we add or drop?
- **Months 7 to 9.** Recruit college liaisons on the ASU hub-and-spoke model. One person per college relaying local activity into the monthly meeting.
- **Month 10.** One public showcase. An Open Space unconference is cheaper than a symposium and fits the practitioner ethos better.
- **Month 12.** The sustaining decision, forced by evaluation, three options only: continue as is, evolve to a new purpose, or disperse. Publish an annual report.

### Track four things, not ten

Only two sources in the whole verified set name metrics. Harvard: attendance, artifact contributions, reported changes in practice, pilot outcomes, reviewed quarterly. Arizona: adoption and awareness, comfort-level survey movement, count of identified use cases. Everything else publishes nothing at all.

## Software, and the argument against most of it

You asked to explore something beyond Google files. Here is the honest answer, and it is not the one the feature lists point to.

**You do not have a software problem yet.** You have maybe fifteen busy people who will show up occasionally and say what they tried. The failure mode is not that your wiki lacks backlinks. It is that nobody writes anything down. Every platform below assumes people will voluntarily go somewhere and type, which is the one thing busy faculty reliably do not do.

### The recommendation

**A report-out form, feeding a sheet, feeding a public page you design yourself, plus whatever meeting or channel already exists.**

1. **Capture.** One form, Microsoft or Google depending on what the district actually licenses. Confirm with the help desk first, Maricopa’s public tech page suggests M365 is the safer institutional bet. Same six fields every time: what I used AI for, which tool, what course or department, what worked, what broke, would I do it again, link to the artifact. Two minutes to fill. Anonymous by default, which also keeps you clean against the ARC no-student-data line.
2. **Store.** The sheet it feeds is the queryable archive. Filter by tool, by department, by worked or did not work. A row can be sorted. A paragraph cannot.
3. **Publish.** This is where you have an advantage nobody else in the research had: you already own a GitHub Pages site and you build clean HTML. The accumulated pattern library becomes a page you design, at a URL with no login, that looks like your work instead of like a Google Site. No vendor, so no procurement conversation. If you would rather not hand-build it, a Google Site published Public is the zero-effort version.
4. **Discuss.** In the room, at the meeting. If you want a channel between meetings, use Viva Engage if people already live in Teams, or a Google Group if they do not. Both already licensed, neither needs a new account. The POD Network, the leading educational-development association in the country, runs its national open discussion on a free Google Group. That is not a fluke.

The form is the only component that changes behavior. It turns “I’ll write that up sometime” into a two-minute obligation attached to a meeting.

### The brainstorming board question

**Yes, but time-boxed to one session, then closed.** A standing board is a second place to lose things.

- **Apply for Miro Education now.** Free forever, 100 members, colleges qualify, and visitors can edit a public board with **no sign-in**. Approval takes up to 10 days, so start the application before you need it.
- **If the session is sooner than that, use Excalidraw.** Zero login for anyone, works immediately, end-to-end encrypted.
- **Google Jamboard is dead.** App went view-only Oct 2024, files permanently deleted Dec 2024. Google’s own replacements are FigJam, Lucidspark, and Miro.

### If you outgrow it

**Notion, free workspace, published as a public site**, if the report-outs need real structure and you want it to look good without hand-writing HTML. Free plan publishes unlimited public sites and readers need no account. The catch: the free education plan is **one member only**, so either you are the sole editor or it is $10 per member per month.

**Discourse free plan**, but only past roughly forty active people generating real async discussion. $0, unlimited members, public and search-indexed, durable archive. An empty forum is worse than no forum.

### Ruled out, with reasons

- **Slack free** keeps only 90 days of history. That is fatal when the archive is the entire point.
- **Microsoft Loop** cannot be shared publicly at all. External viewers need a guest account in the tenant.
- **Microsoft Teams free “Communities”** explicitly bars work and school accounts. Anyone who says “we have Teams, we’ll just make a community” is assuming something Microsoft contradicts.
- **Coda no longer exists.** Rebranded Superhuman Docs July 2026, education discount paused.
- **Circle, Mighty Networks, Higher Logic, Hivebrite.** Sales-quoted contract products built for associations with paid community staff. Not viable for something you are starting yourself.
- **Canvas.** Two reasons. Instructure had a confirmed breach in 2026, and separately Canvas reads as coursework and obligation, which is the wrong feeling for a voluntary group.
- **Discord.** You run one for students and it works there, but it reads as a student space to faculty and has no institutional admin control or export.

## Operating detail: the form, the roles, the folder

### The eight form fields

Kept short on purpose. Three minutes to fill.

Name and college or department · what you tried (tool name) · use case (dropdown: teaching, assessment, advising or student support, content or design, operations) · who it served (students, staff, faculty) · what happened, two sentences · would you recommend it (yes, no, yes with caveats) · a link or file others can reuse · what you are stuck on. Plus one checkbox: may we share this district-wide.

Put the no-PII line **on the form itself**, not in the invite email, and say it again at meeting one.

You already run this exact pattern twice. `airc-sss/Create_Barrier_Log_Form.gs` builds a Form plus a linked responses sheet in a couple of minutes, and `airc-sss/run-log-form-setup.md` documents the prefill technique. This is not new tooling. It is the third instance of something that works.

### Roles, small and named out loud

- **Convener and co-convener.** Two people, not one. If the group only runs when you run it, it is not a community of practice, it is your side project. Name the co-convener at meeting one.
- **Librarian, rotating each semester.** Owns the sheet and the folder, checks that show-and-tells got logged, keeps the folder from becoming a junk drawer.
- **Show-and-tell presenter, rotating, booked two meetings ahead.** Booked ahead is the part that matters. “Anyone want to share?” produces silence.
- **ARC liaison.** Carries the quarterly digest up and district context back. Given Domain 5, that is likely you, and it should be written down rather than assumed.

### The entry ticket

The show-and-tell presenter submits the form **before** they present. Their submission is the entry ticket, and the presentation is them talking through a row that already exists. Everyone else can submit any time, and the form stays open between meetings, so the CoP still produces in a month when the meeting gets eaten by finals or a district deadline.

Once a quarter, the conveners spend forty-five minutes turning the sheet into a one-page digest: what people are using, what is working, what three people are stuck on, one thing worth piloting. That page goes to the ARC and is the raw material for the publishable piece the launch kit asks for. **Nine months of digests is a paper. Nine months of meeting notes is not.**

### The folder, four subfolders, nothing else

- `01-Charter-and-Goals`, the charter, the chosen goals, the meeting schedule.
- `02-Intake`, the form and the responses sheet. This is the important one.
- `03-Meetings`, one dated agenda-and-notes doc per meeting. Not one endless doc.
- `04-Artifacts`, the digests, slides, prompts, builds, and drafts people will share.

**The folder lives on a Maricopa account or a shared drive, never a personal Gmail.** The kickoff form went out from a personal account. Fine for a scheduling poll, not for the group's archive. Move it before there is anything in it worth losing, with two owners, checked once a semester by the librarian.

What does not go in: student PII or anything pulled from a system of record (that is a Domain 1 governance question and the answer is no), real-time chat, and anything needing a tool the district has not licensed.

### First three moves, this week

1. **Build the form.** Copy the pattern from `Create_Barrier_Log_Form.gs`, cut it to the eight fields, run it, grab the responder link. About thirty minutes.
2. **Stand up the folder on the Maricopa account** with the four subfolders, move the existing notes doc into `03-Meetings`, and name the librarian.
3. **Seed the sheet before anyone sees it.** You and the co-convener each enter three real entries. An empty form is intimidating and an empty sheet reads as a dead project. Then send the link with the meeting-one invite and book the first two presenters **by name**, not by open call.

## What could go wrong

1. **The platform becomes the project.** Near certainty if you pick anything paid. Three meetings on tool selection, permissions, and taxonomy, and the CoP has produced a workspace instead of a practice. Pick the form this week and let the archive’s shape emerge from what people submit.
2. **You become the bottleneck.** Every design here has a curator. If you are the only one turning submissions into the published page, the CoP stops when you get busy. Decide now whether an ugly-but-public raw sheet is acceptable, so it survives a month when you do not curate.
3. **The form gets filled twice and then never again.** It needs a forcing function. Everyone who presents fills it before they talk, and the last five minutes of every meeting is spent filling it.
4. **A free tier moves under you.** Coda’s education discount paused. Slack’s free history dropped to 90 days. Instructure killed Free-for-Teacher after a breach. The form plus sheet plus your own page is the only design here where nobody can change the terms.
5. **Someone escalates it.** The moment it looks successful there will be pressure to redo it properly on a district platform with a contract and a security review. A year of report-outs in a portable sheet is what gives you leverage in that conversation instead of starting over.
6. **The archive accumulates without becoming reusable.** A hundred rows of “I tried Claude for rubrics, it was fine” is not a knowledge base. Force specificity in the fields: the actual prompt, the actual artifact, the actual failure.
7. **The form gets four responses.** The most likely failure of all. Countered by making submission the entry ticket for presenting, by seeding it, and by never asking for anything the person has not already done.
8. **Twice a month decays into once a semester.** Probable in a district this size on this calendar. Designed around: the form runs whether or not the meeting happens, so a skipped month costs a conversation, not the record.
9. **The group drifts into a training series.** People will ask for step-by-step help, because they always do. The launch kit is emphatic that this is not that. Say no twice and it holds. Say yes once and the practitioners stop coming.
10. **Scope collision with the ARC.** The CoP is a GCC campus group. Domain 5 is district work. When they overlap, the CoP contributes evidence and the ARC makes decisions. Write that line down now, while it is uncontroversial.

## Where the published advice does not fit Maricopa

**Everything verified is single-campus except two, and neither maps cleanly.** ASU’s hub-and-spoke is closest, and it is one university with one provost, one IT organization, one identity system. NCCCS is a state system, but North Carolina’s colleges have far less autonomy than Maricopa’s ten. Nothing in the literature addresses ten colleges with their own teaching centers, their own deans, and a district office that already tried the committee approach and let it go stale.

**The playbooks assume a sponsor with resources.** Every Learner ties sustainability to funding and “sustained resource allocation.” Colorado State’s guide budgets a shared meal at every meeting. You have five hours a week of paid ARC time and no budget line. The advice about sponsorship and mini-grants is not actionable.

**Cadence advice assumes people can attend.** A fixed monthly hour works at one campus. Across ten colleges on different schedules it will exclude someone every time. NCCCS solved this by being fully virtual, which is a real tradeoff none of the sources discuss.

**Almost nobody publishes what happened next.** Cadence and format are well documented. Outcomes are not. The one voice describing actual behavior change is Pima’s CIO, writing 23 Aug 2026: *“Instead of focusing primarily on concerns about cheating, faculty began discussing how AI could support teaching while also encouraging us to rethink assessment.”* His advice was to start small, solve real problems, and build from there.

**One asset you have that none of these sources do.** You are already inside the governance body. Every CoP in the verified set had to build a path to leadership from scratch. You have the reverse problem: building a path away from it, credibly enough that practitioners believe the room is theirs. The three boundary sentences are not decoration. They are the design constraint.

## The agenda

### 1. Why this exists, and what it is not (5 min)

Open with the invitation line, not a mission statement:

> This is a space to talk about AI, get some clarity, exchange ideas, or even just vent.

Then read these three sentences out loud, word for word. They go into the charter as written.

1. **This is not a training program. Bring something you are already doing.**
2. **This is not a governance body. It does not set policy and it does not oversee anyone&rsquo;s operational decisions.**
3. **It does surface recommendations upward to the ARC when the group agrees there is something worth saying.**

Then say the part behind sentence three, once, plainly: you co-chair the Student Support and Success domain of the district ARC, so claiming this group is fully independent of that would not hold up. What that means in practice is a one-page digest going up once a quarter, with the group seeing it first. The room stays the practitioners&rsquo; room.

No slides. No round of introductions here, the demos do that work.

### 2. Three demos (20 min, 5 to 7 minutes each)

Three people, recruited in advance, showing something real they already made or already use. Michelle introduces each in one sentence and keeps time.

Format for each presenter, say this to them ahead of time:
- What you were trying to do.
- What you actually did, on screen.
- What broke, or what surprised you.
- What you would want help with.

Rules in the room: no step-by-step tutorials, no tool evaluations, no apologizing for the thing being rough. Questions held to the discussion block unless someone is stuck on a fact.

Michelle keeps a visible timer. Cut at seven minutes even if it is good, because running long here is what eats the co-writing block, which is the part that only happens if it is protected.

### 3. Open discussion, one question (20 min)

The question, on screen, unchanged for the whole block:

> **What issues would this solve in your area?**

One question, not a list. If the room goes quiet, the follow-ups are: what did you just see that you could use tomorrow, and what are you already doing that nobody in this room knows about.

The note taker captures in the shared doc under three headings only: **problems named**, **things people are already doing**, **what people said they are stuck on**. Not a transcript. Stuck-on items are the ones that matter, they become future meeting topics and the first artifact.

Michelle facilitates, does not answer. If someone asks for step-by-step help with a tool, say the thing directly: that is a workshop, and this is not that, but two people here have done it and can meet you after. Saying no twice makes it hold.

### 4. Co-write the vision statement, live (10 min)

Shared doc on the screen, everyone can see the cursor. Do not present a pre-written statement. Do not send it out afterward for comment.

How to run it:
- Michelle types what people say, verbatim, as a list. No editing yet.
- At about six minutes, read the list back and ask which two or three phrases are doing the real work.
- Draft one or two sentences from those phrases in the room. Read it out loud once.
- Good enough is the target, not final. It goes into the charter with today&rsquo;s date on it and gets revisited at the six-month review.

Prompt if the room stalls: finish the sentence &ldquo;A year from now, this group will have &hellip;&rdquo;

### 5. First artifact and low-stakes roles (5 min)

**Name the candidate artifact.** The obvious one, and say it as a proposal, not a decision: a shared no-PII inventory of how people at GCC are actually using AI, built from the intake form, published as a page anyone can read without a login. It is the thing the form already produces, so it costs the group nothing extra, and the quarterly digest drawn from it is what goes to the ARC. Confirm or replace it by meeting three.

**Ask for four roles by name in the room.** Small, specific, time-bound.

| Role | What it costs | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Co-convener | One planning conversation a month. Not a co-teacher, an insurance policy so this is not one person&rsquo;s side project. |  |
| Note taker, today | The shared doc for this meeting only. |  |
| Librarian, this semester | Owns the folder and the responses sheet, checks that demos got logged. Rotates next semester. |  |
| Next month&rsquo;s demo, two slots | Five to seven minutes, booked now, two meetings ahead. |  |

**Close with the fixed slot.** Same day of month, same hour, hybrid, next two dates announced now. It does not move.

**Last thirty seconds:** everyone opens the form and submits one row before they leave the room. This is the forcing function. It happens at the end of every meeting from here on.

## What people leave with

- The three boundary sentences, so nobody is confused about whether they are being trained, governed, or reported on.
- Three things they saw working, made by people they can find on campus.
- The form link, and a row of their own already in the sheet.
- A vision statement in their own words, dated today.
- The next two meeting dates.
- For four of them, a small named role.

## The two failure modes to watch in this meeting

1. **The demos run long and the co-writing block gets cut.** Then the group leaves with nothing it made together, and the vision statement becomes something Michelle writes alone and presents, which is the version that does not stick. Cut demos at seven minutes.
2. **Someone asks the group to become a training series.** People will, because they always do. The answer is no, said kindly, twice. Say yes once and the practitioners stop coming.
