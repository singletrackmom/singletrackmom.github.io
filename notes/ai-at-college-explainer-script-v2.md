# AI at College: Know the Rules — script v2 (revised for student clarity + faculty buy-in)

Two-host explainer (Nia and Sam) · ~5 min · plain, spoken, phone-first. Names are just the two voices, edit freely.

## What changed and why (for you and for faculty who'll assign it)
Reviewed through three lenses: a pro-AI faculty view, an anti-AI English PhD, and a real student. The changes that matter:
- **Fixed an unsafe line.** The old "being upfront keeps you safe even in classes that ban it" is false, disclosure is not permission. It's now explicit: in a no-AI class, the only safe move is not using it. (This is the biggest liability fix and the biggest reason a skeptical professor will trust the video.)
- **Gave the "no" equal weight.** The old "it's a craft" + "don't waste your tuition" was thin and guilt-trippy. Now the no is an intellectual case (writing is thinking; the struggle is the learning; the professor grades your growth, not the paper). Cut the money guilt-trip.
- **Added the one mental model:** use AI on the *thinking and studying*, not to produce the graded artifact.
- **Answered the fears students actually have:** "I already used it and didn't know it was banned," false accusation by unreliable AI detectors (keep your drafts), "is Grammarly/Translate/spellcheck AI?", "what if my professor doesn't reply?"
- **Made the good uses concrete and leveling-up** (plain-language explanations, Spanish-first, study plans around work, feedback on your own draft, getting past the blank page) and the workforce point specific (nursing, business, IT, design, trades) with the "human judgment still matters" hedge.
- **Added a hidden-risks beat** (made-up facts, bias, privacy) and an "allowed isn't always wise" beat.
- **Plainer words + on-screen definitions** for syllabus, cheating/honest, "cite," craft, and where the syllabus lives (Canvas).

Production notes: burn-in captions; release a Spanish version or Spanish captions; when a key term is spoken, show it on screen with a one-line plain meaning; use on-screen chapter titles so a distracted viewer can find their place; big text for a vertical phone.

## Scene 1 · The flip
**Nia:**  Ever used AI on your homework, then thought, wait, is this cheating?  
**Sam:**  You're not alone. Here's the part that surprises new students.  
**Nia:**  In some college classes, your professor WANTS you to use AI.  
**Sam:**  In others, it's a hard no. Same college, different classes.  
**Nia:**  In the next few minutes you'll know how to tell which is which, and how to stay out of trouble.  

## Scene 2 · Rule one: the rules change by class
**Nia:**  Rule one. The AI rules change from class to class.  
**Sam:**  Your math class might ban it. Your business class might require it.  
**Nia:**  Your English class might let you brainstorm with AI, but not write the essay for you.  
**Sam:**  None of them are wrong. They're teaching different things.  
**Nia:**  So never assume. What's fine in one class can flip in the next.  

## Scene 3 · Why a class says no
**Nia:**  So why would a class ever say no? It's not because your professor is scared of the future.  
**Sam:**  It's because in a lot of classes, the writing IS the thinking. You don't figure out what you believe and then write it down. You figure it out BY writing.  
**Nia:**  That stuck feeling, wrestling an idea into words, that struggle is your brain building the idea. Hand it to AI and you skip the exact thing you came to college to grow.  
**Sam:**  Same in art, in math, in code. The messy first try, the redo, that's where the learning lives.  
**Nia:**  A paper AI wrote can look great and teach you nothing. Your professor isn't grading the paper. They're growing you.  
**Sam:**  So a "no" isn't your teacher being behind. It's them protecting the part that's yours.  

## Scene 4 · The one idea that clears up most of the confusion
**Nia:**  Here's the shortcut that answers most questions. Ask: what is this assignment actually measuring?  
**Sam:**  If it's measuring whether YOU can write it, code it, or draw it, then that final thing has to be yours.  
**Nia:**  But the stuff around it, understanding the reading, planning your time, quizzing yourself, getting unstuck, that's usually fair game, unless your teacher says otherwise.  
**Sam:**  Use AI on the learning. Not on the answer they're grading.  
**Nia:**  Put it another way: AI can be your editor, not your author. It can help sharpen what's yours. It can't be the one whose ideas get graded.  

## Scene 5 · Rule two: find the rule, then ask
**Nia:**  Rule two. Find the rule for each class.  
**Sam:**  Open the class in Canvas and find the first document your teacher posted, usually called the syllabus. That's the class rules page. [on-screen: syllabus = the class rules page in Canvas]  
**Nia:**  Use the search and type "AI." See what it says: can you use it, when, how much.  
**Sam:**  Can't find it, or it's confusing? Message your instructor before you use AI. One line: "Hi Professor, is it okay to use AI for this assignment?"  
**Nia:**  What if they don't answer right away? Don't guess. Wait to hear back, ask in class, or email again. Waiting is safer than guessing.  
**Sam:**  And teachers respect you asking first. It shows you're trying to do it right.  

## Scene 6 · Already worried?
**Nia:**  Maybe you already used AI, and now you're not sure it was allowed.  
**Sam:**  Take a breath. You're not in trouble for asking. Message your teacher, be honest about what you did, and ask what to do next. Asking is the safe move.  
**Nia:**  And a worry we hear a lot: what if you write something yourself and a computer "AI detector" wrongly says it's AI?  
**Sam:**  Those detectors aren't reliable. They flag real student writing, especially from students who learned English as a second language.  
**Nia:**  So protect yourself: keep your drafts, your notes, your version history. If you can show how you built it, you can show it's yours.  

## Scene 7 · What even counts as AI?
**Nia:**  Quick one, because it's confusing. What actually counts as AI?  
**Sam:**  These are usually fine, unless your teacher says otherwise: spellcheck, Grammarly for grammar, Google Translate to understand an assignment.  
**Nia:**  The risky one is having AI write the whole thing for you, or make the project you're being graded on.  
**Sam:**  When in doubt, treat it like anything else: check the rule, or ask.  
**Nia:**  Two quick safety notes. Don't paste anything private into AI, no ID numbers, no health info, no one else's info. What you type may not stay private.  
**Sam:**  And when you pick a tool, choose ones that respect your privacy and are clear about how they work.  

## Scene 8 · Rule three: some classes teach AI on purpose
**Nia:**  Rule three. Some classes teach you AI on purpose.  
**Sam:**  Because a lot of real jobs use it now. Nurses use AI tools to check drug interactions and write up notes. Someone in business drafts an email or cleans up a spreadsheet. IT folks find the bug in their code.  
**Nia:**  Designers spin out ten rough directions before lunch, then do the real design themselves. Even in the trades, you'll troubleshoot with an AI assistant.  
**Sam:**  The job still needs a human who knows what "good" looks like and can catch when the tool's wrong. That judgment is what these classes are really teaching.  
**Nia:**  So a class that teaches AI isn't cheating. It's getting you ready for a real job.  

## Scene 9 · Rule four: your 2 a.m. study buddy
**Nia:**  Rule four. Even when you can't use AI ON the assignment, it can still help you learn.  
**Sam:**  Think of it like a study group that's awake at two in the morning.  
**Nia:**  Stuck on a reading that's all jargon? Paste a paragraph and ask it to explain it like you're new to this. That's what a tutor does.  
**Sam:**  English isn't your first language? Ask it to explain the idea in Spanish first, then in plain English. You still do the work in English, but now you actually get it.  
**Nia:**  Working two jobs? Tell it your free hours and your due dates, and ask for a study plan that fits real life.  
**Sam:**  Wrote a draft yourself and want a second read? Ask what's unclear or where your point is thin. You wrote it, you're just getting feedback.  
**Nia:**  Want to think harder, not less? Ask it to argue the other side, poke holes in your point, or flag the bias in something you're reading, then YOU decide what holds up.  
**Sam:**  Big project? Have it outline your own ideas or shrink a long reading to the main points, but always read the real source yourself and check the details before you trust them.  
**Nia:**  It can even help you format your APA or MLA citations, or catch accidental plagiarism before you turn work in.  
**Sam:**  The blank page is where a lot of us quit. AI's best trick is getting you past the blank page so you can do the real work.  
**Sam:**  One line keeps this safe: even a great study use can cross the line if the class says no AI, period. When your instructor bans it, the ban wins.  

## Scene 10 · Allowed isn't always wise
**Nia:**  One more thing, even when AI is allowed.  
**Sam:**  Ask yourself a second question: is this helping me learn, or just helping me finish?  
**Nia:**  Finishing feels good tonight. Learning is what's still there at the final, and in the job after.  

## Scene 11 · The rule under all the rules
**Nia:**  So here's the rule underneath all the others. When in doubt, ask first, and be honest.  
**Sam:**  If a class allows AI, say how you used it, one line: "I used AI to brainstorm ideas," or "to check my grammar." [on-screen: show the exact sentence]  
**Nia:**  But hear this clearly. If a class bans AI, telling your professor afterward does NOT make it okay. Being honest is good, but it isn't a free pass.  
**Sam:**  The only thing that keeps you safe in a no-AI class is not using it.  
**Nia:**  And whatever the class, check any fact AI gives you against a real source. It can make things up and sound totally sure. Your name is on the work, so you own it.  

## Scene 12 · Close
**Nia:**  That's it. Find the rule for each class. Ask when you're not sure.  
**Sam:**  Use AI to learn, not to fake it.  
**Nia:**  And if you remember nothing else, remember this: [slow, big, on-screen]  
**Sam:**  Ask first. Be honest. Put your name on your own work.  
**Nia:**  You've got this. See you in class.
