The Design Studio

Experiential learning

Real clients, real briefs, real deadlines. A course I taught for a decade, where students took live work from campus partners and shipped it, from a 90-foot mural to motion, print, publications, and an app. It outgrew the course, won a grant, and now runs as a paid studio.

Goal

Put students in front of actual clients and actual deadlines, so they graduate with professional work rather than class assignments. Every project on this page was made inside the course.

The design of it is the point. In an ordinary assignment the instructor is the audience, so the student is really performing for a grade. Here a client is waiting, the work goes out into the world, and the student has to defend the choices to someone who did not assign them. The assessment is the professional task. There is no way to fake it, no way to paste it, and nothing a model can hand back that a client will accept.

Audience

Students first, and not only design students. The studio is a multidisciplinary group, film, photography, animation, and design, because a real client brief rarely stays inside one discipline. Campus partners second: the offices, programs, and committees who need this work and have no creative staff of their own. Both are real, and neither is a simulation of the other.

Process

Students work in teams, and one student on each team serves as the project manager. That student writes the design brief and the timeline, interfaces directly with the client, and keeps the team on track. Not me. The brief names the client, the goal, the approach, and what the client actually needs; the timeline carries the proof cycles, the client approval gates, and the delivery date. Teams run in Basecamp, Slack, Miro, and Discord.

While I taught it, my job was to decide which projects the studio took on and in what order, and to hold the standard. Theirs was to run it. A student who has written a brief, held a timeline, and told a client that a date is slipping has done something no assignment can simulate.

The boards below are the working evidence, not a reconstruction. Both were built by student teams while the work was live.

So they leave with two things, not one. They leave with the work, and they leave having practiced real project management methodology: scoping against a client’s actual need, sequencing a build, running a proof and approval cycle, tracking a team, and delivering on a date they committed to in writing. Designers are rarely taught any of it, and it is the thing that decides who gets to lead.

Which is why I tell them to put it on their resume as an internship, because that is what it is. They had a client, a brief, a deadline, and a deliverable that shipped. The only difference between this and an internship is that it happened to be worth credit.

The model outgrew the course. We wrote a grant, it came through, and the class no longer runs, because it did not need to. The studio now employs four paid students each semester, drawn from film, photography, animation, and design, doing client work for a wage instead of for credit. Students who want the experience without the job take an internship placed with a client instead.

A film faculty member runs it now. Three of us advise, one from animation, one from photography, and me from design. I do not run the studio, and that is the point. The work students made inside the course is what made the case for funding it, and the thing I built outlasted my running it.

Selected client work

Nobody knew the free-textbook filter existed

Client · Open Educational Resources Committee

Open Maricopa, formerly Maricopa Millions, is a district program created by district colleagues, not by me. It funds faculty to build their own course materials instead of assigning a textbook, and it has saved students more than $36 million. Students see it as a filter in course search, and that is the problem: a filter nobody notices saves nobody anything, which makes this an adoption problem rather than an engineering one. The college’s OER committee, on which I serve, brought it to the studio as a brief with one hard constraint. The piece had to play on Gaucho TV and the screens in the student union, and those screens have no sound. It had to be understood by someone walking past, with the audio off, who was not planning to pay attention. That constraint is what made it a design problem: no voiceover to lean on, so the motion, the characters, and the interface had to carry all of it.

Produced by Digital Media Arts students under faculty direction, from brief through script, storyboard, animation, and delivery. The filters are live in Maricopa Find a Class.

A day in the life, told by the people living it

Client · Digital Media Arts recruitment

A prospective student does not want a brochure, and the only people who can honestly describe the life are the ones living it, so the students made the recruitment piece themselves. It opens on an alarm clock and follows a Digital Media Arts student through an ordinary day, across campus, into class, into the work. It is live action with hand-drawn animation laid over it, which is harder than either one alone, and it is both narrated and captioned, so it holds up with the sound off and for a viewer who needs the text. Nobody required that. It is the same instinct every brief here demands: decide who you are actually talking to, then meet them where they are.

Produced by Digital Media Arts students under faculty direction.
