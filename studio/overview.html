The Design Studio

Experiential learning

Real clients, real briefs, real deadlines. A course I taught for a decade, where students took live work from campus partners and shipped it, from a 90-foot mural to motion, print, publications, and an app.

Goal

Put students in front of actual clients and actual deadlines, so they graduate with professional work rather than class assignments. Every project on this page was made inside the course.

The design of it is the point. In an ordinary assignment the instructor is the audience, so the student is really performing for a grade. Here a client is waiting, the work goes out into the world, and the student has to defend the choices to someone who did not assign them. The assessment is the professional task. There is no way to fake it, no way to paste it, and nothing a model can hand back that a client will accept.

Audience

Students first: design students who need to have done the work before anyone will hire them for it. Campus partners second: the offices, programs, and committees who need design help and have no design staff of their own. Both are real, and neither is a simulation of the other.

Process

Students work in teams, and one student on each team serves as the project manager. That student writes the design brief and the timeline, interfaces directly with the client, and keeps the team on track. Not me. The brief names the client, the goal, the approach, and what the client actually needs; the timeline carries the proof cycles, the client approval gates, and the delivery date. Teams run in Basecamp, Slack, Miro, and Discord.

My job is to decide which projects the studio takes on and in what order, and to hold the standard. Theirs is to run it. A student who has written a brief, held a timeline, and told a client that a date is slipping has done something no assignment can simulate.

So they leave with two things, not one. They leave with the work, and they leave having practiced real project management methodology: scoping against a client’s actual need, sequencing a build, running a proof and approval cycle, tracking a team, and delivering on a date they committed to in writing. Designers are rarely taught any of it, and it is the thing that decides who gets to lead.

Which is why I tell them to put it on their resume as an internship, because that is what it is. They had a client, a brief, a deadline, and a deliverable that shipped. The only difference between this and an internship is that it happened to be worth credit.

The model outgrew the course. We wrote a grant, it came through, and the class no longer runs. Students now either work for the studio and are paid, or they take an internship placed with a client. The work made in the course is what made that case.

Selected client work

Nobody knew the free-textbook filter existed

Client · Open Educational Resources Committee

Open Maricopa, formerly Maricopa Millions, is a district program, and not one I built. Colleagues elsewhere in the district created it and have taken it to conferences around the world. It pays faculty a stipend to develop their own course materials instead of assigning a textbook, and it puts the result in front of students as a filter in course search: Zero Textbook Cost, Low Textbook Cost, and Open Educational Resources. It set out to save students about a million dollars. Between 2013 and 2023 it saved them more than $36 million. That is somebody else’s achievement, and it is worth saying plainly, because it is the reason this brief mattered.

The feature works. It is also buried in a filter menu, so a student who does not know it is there pays for a book they never needed. The savings are real and the awareness is not.

That is not an engineering problem, it is an adoption problem, and the fix is not more software. The college’s OER committee, which I serve on, brought it to the studio as a client brief: make students aware of a thing that already exists, in a form students will actually watch.

The brief carried one hard constraint, and it is the constraint that made this a design problem. The piece had to run on the web, on the screens in the student union, and on Gaucho TV, the displays scattered around campus. Those screens have no sound. So it had to be understood by someone walking past it, with the audio off, who was not planning to pay attention. No voiceover to lean on, no narration to explain the interface, and no second chance. Everything had to be carried by the motion, the characters, and the interface itself. The dollar with the sunglasses is not decoration, it is the thing that stops a student mid-walk and shows them, without a word, where to click and what they stop paying for.

Produced by Digital Media Arts students under faculty direction, from client brief through script, storyboard, animation, and delivery. The filters are live today in Maricopa Find a Class. Savings figure from the Open Maricopa program.

A day in the life, told by the people living it

Client · Digital Media Arts recruitment

A prospective student does not want a brochure. They want to know what their life would actually be like, and the only people who can answer that honestly are the ones already living it. So the students made the recruitment piece themselves. It opens on an alarm clock going off and follows a Digital Media Arts student through an ordinary day: across campus, into class, into the work.

It is live action with hand-drawn animation laid over it, which is harder than either one alone, and it is narrated and captioned, so it works with the sound on or off and for a viewer who needs the text. Nobody made them do that. The same instinct shows up in every project here: figure out who you are actually talking to, then meet them where they are.

Produced by Digital Media Arts students under faculty direction.
