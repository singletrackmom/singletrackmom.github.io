Round 1 · Core

1Walk me through your background and why this role.
I’ve spent more than 20 years in higher education widening access for working learners, and I want to do that next on a team already committed to the future of education, instead of spending my energy convincing my campus to keep up. My background is unusual in a useful way: I’m a designer and UX person with a master’s in instructional technology focused on personal learning environments and adult online learning, but I’ve also been an ed-tech product manager and the director of instructional technology for a college in the Maricopa district, one of the largest in the country. For the past year I’ve gone deep on AI so I could teach it responsibly, and that’s pulled me into district-level AI strategy. WGU is where I’d love to do this work with rigor and real support behind it. Mission first, “why WGU” up front, credentials second.
2What attracts you to competency-based education, and where does your experience translate?
What draws me to CBE is that it measures what a student can actually do, not time in a seat, and that’s already how I teach. In my classroom students demonstrate mastery through portfolios and real projects rather than tests, aligned to competencies our industry advisors help define. I’ll be honest that I haven’t taught inside WGU’s specific model, with rolling starts, flat-rate terms, and the Program Mentor and Evaluator structure, so I’d be designing within CBE for the first time. But the core, competency over seat time, authentic assessment, and meeting working adults where they are, is the work I’ve been doing for years. I’m eager to bring that instinct into a model built entirely around it. Nails CBE mechanics, then the honest “eager to design within CBE” bridge.
3How do you think about rigor, and the limits of your own expertise?
The risk is real, AI makes it easy to build something and overclaim, so I’m careful about what I am and am not. My expertise is learning design and the student experience; that’s the domain I own. When I build, I start the way I make my beginning design students start, with a requirements document: who’s the audience, what’s the goal, what problem are we solving. The tools I make are prototypes to test an idea, not products I’d call proven, my campus bot and Render are in testing, not production. And I know where my expertise ends: for production engineering, security, psychometrics, and rigorous efficacy studies, I partner with experts, which is exactly what WGU Labs is for. I’d rather pilot, measure, and find out something already exists than ship and overclaim. Names where you defer to experts, disarms the vibe-coding concern.
4A promising AI capability lands tomorrow. How do you decide whether and how to bring it in at scale?
My first question is always, what problem are we solving, is this real or is it the shiny new thing. I learned that the hard way as an instructional technologist: we once took “free” SharePoint because of a Microsoft contract instead of defining the problem first, and spent two years forcing the wrong tool to build an intranet. So even if a capability looks exciting, I’d write a clear requirements document tied to a student outcome, then run a small pilot with a power user who’s genuinely on board, never with skeptics. I’d test whether it actually moves the outcome, expand to a second pilot, and only scale once it’s proven. That test-before-you-scale discipline is exactly how WGU Labs already works. Problem-first, pilot with power users, WGU Labs tie-in.
5Where should there always be a human in the loop, and how do you protect data and trust?
I can’t think of many places where there shouldn’t be a human in the loop. I start every class modeling responsible use and teaching critical thinking, because many students were told AI is cheating, so I reframe it: you’re here to learn to think, so use it, but interrogate what it gives you. Same with my agents, I always review and iterate the output rather than trust it blindly. On data, my rule is simple: no student PII goes into AI. My tools collect a first name at most, I work from sandboxed copies of courses, and nothing touches live student data. If security built a vetted, FERPA-compliant path, I’d be open to it, but I’d never improvise that myself. With working adults, trust is the whole game, and you earn it by collecting the minimum. Crisp two-part answer; names FERPA and the no-PII rule.
6How do you assess real competency when AI can produce the work?
I assess what students can do by scaffolding the process, not just grading the final artifact. Every project starts with thumbnail sketches, a design brief, and storyboards, so I watch the thinking develop. AI is welcome as a thinking partner or to polish writing, but not to make the art, because the point is to learn the craft, and a finished-looking AI image with no process behind it is a conversation. Because everything is staged and personal, like a goals statement that has to be theirs, AI shortcuts don’t get a student through. That’s authentic, competency-based assessment, and it matters more than ever: when AI can fake the output, you assess the process and what the student can actually produce. Names it “authentic, competency-based assessment” — Tommy’s exact theme.
7Pitch an AI idea for your first 90 days.
I’d want to validate whether there’s a need for a portable career-launch tool, because I’ve built one and the early signal is promising. It’s called Render. In my capstone, students pull everything from the semester into it, aligned to one real reach job they choose on day one: job search, resumes, mock interviews, a skills-gap analysis, and a personal learning plan with a schedule to close the gaps. They graduate owning it, no PII, it lives on their machine and keeps working after the course ends, and faculty in other disciplines have already asked for a version. For WGU’s working adults, a take-with-you, continued-learning plan tied to real goals fits CBE well. But I’d start in discovery: confirm the need and check whether something already exists, then pilot and measure before building. Confident pitch, but opens and closes in discovery mode.
8How would you think about belonging and persistence in a self-paced model?
This is a question I care about, because connected, supported students persist, and self-paced models are where that’s hardest. On my campus I attack it two ways: a bot that connects students to support services they don’t always know to ask for, and a Discord that ties faculty, tutoring, and students together informally, where students help each other and we run critiques. That peer layer is what sets our async courses apart. WGU already has real strengths here, the mentor model and early-alert notifications, so I wouldn’t reinvent it. I’d start by measuring it: do students actually feel supported and connected, and where are the gaps. Then I’d test lightweight, always-on ways to add peer connection without breaking self-pacing. Human-in-the-loop, measured against persistence, piloted before scaling. Credits WGU’s strengths, then proposes measure-first.

Round 2 · Behavioral, PM & Closing

9Tell me about a time you turned a new technology into something people actually used.
Early in my career I had a graphic design job where on day one someone handed me HTML and said learn it, and a coworker and I built one of the first Fortune 100 company websites. The web didn’t exist as a medium when I trained, so I carried what I knew about design and how people read into a brand-new tool. I think about AI exactly the same way. This past year I learned it by building with it, then brought it into my classes so students get literate and responsible with it, and built Render so the whole job-search process lives in one place they own. The throughline is always the same: learn the new medium on its own terms, carry the durable design principles into it, and make something people actually use. The HTML story, framed as “carry principles into the new medium.”
10Tell me about aligning people who didn’t report to you.
Most of my influence has nothing to do with authority. I co-chair the Student Support and Success domain of our district AI committee, working across all 10 colleges, none of whom report to me, so it’s all shared purpose and listening. On my own campus I’ve gotten faculty to try AI not by mandating it but by starting an informal community of practice and a Discord where early adopters share what works, and letting curiosity spread. As a product manager at an ed-tech company I worked from requirements engineers had built and had to align design, engineering, and stakeholders without owning any of them. The pattern is always the same: anchor on the student outcome everyone already cares about, make it easy and low-risk to say yes, and let the wins do the convincing. Influence without authority; the ARC domain + CoP + PM experience.
11Tell me about managing a complex project from initiation to completion.
The clearest one is leading our campus through an LMS change. I led the LMS team during the Blackboard era and contributed to the evaluation and RFP that ended with our district moving to Canvas. That meant defining requirements with faculty, evaluating vendors against those requirements, helping manage the migration for a very large number of users, and then supporting people through the change. It’s exactly the discipline I’d bring to AI decisions at WGU: define the problem and the requirements first, evaluate against them, pilot, then roll out with support, rather than buying or building first and forcing it to fit later. LMS RFP → Canvas: a clean start-to-finish with sign-offs.
12Describe a project that stalled or failed. What did you learn?
An early version of my campus support bot taught me a real lesson. When students asked about courses, the AI would confidently invent course names and numbers that didn’t exist, because it was pulling from training data instead of our actual catalog. It looked polished and was wrong, which is the worst combination in a student-facing tool. So I pulled it back, added guardrails so it only draws from verified information, and made “never trust the model for specifics” a rule. The lesson was that fluency isn’t accuracy, and anything student-facing has to be grounded and tested before it goes live. That’s a big part of why I’m so careful now about the line between a prototype and a production tool. Honest failure (AI hallucination) with a responsible-AI lesson.
13How do you measure whether a program-experience change actually worked?
I try to measure the outcome, not the activity. With our lowest-success foundation course, the redesign isn’t “done” because we shipped it, it’s measured by whether the success rate actually moves, which we check against the retention research it’s grounded in. For something like student connection, I’d use support-and-belonging surveys and persistence data, not how many features we added. In Render there’s a funnel diagnosis that reads a student’s real activity to find where their search is stalling. The principle is the same one I’d bring to WGU: judge a change by learning, retention, and engagement, not by what got built. Outcome over activity; KPIs tied to retention and engagement.
14A faculty group resists an AI rollout. What do you do?
I’ve lived this, so I’d start with empathy, not a mandate. A lot of faculty are anxious about AI, around cheating, around being replaced, around extra work, and those concerns are legitimate. I’d never roll out to skeptics first. I’d start with willing power users, make the value obvious with their own students, and create a low-pressure community of practice where people share what’s working instead of being trained at. I’d also be honest about the guardrails, no student PII, human in the loop, AI as a partner and not a replacement. Adoption follows trust and visible wins, not pressure. On my campus that informal, voluntary approach has done far more than any top-down push could. Empathy + power users first + community of practice.
15What does a Product Owner own, and how do you prioritize when everything’s urgent?
I’ll be upfront that my PSPO certification is in progress, I’m finishing it this summer, so I’m speaking from product experience plus what I’ve studied. A Product Owner owns the value of the product and the ordered backlog, deciding what gets built next and, just as importantly, saying no to protect the goal. When everything feels urgent, I prioritize by student impact: what moves the outcome for the most learners, tested in small increments. Ship a thin slice, check whether it actually works, then scale, rather than committing big to something unproven. That’s the same evidence-first rhythm WGU Labs uses, and it’s how I’d run AI pilots here. Honest “PSPO in progress,” then value + ordered backlog + impact.
16What questions do you have for us?
A few I’d genuinely want to ask: What does success look like for this role in the first 6 to 12 months? How does the Program Experience Futures team work day to day with WGU Labs and the academic programs? How are AI pilots scoped, governed, and tested for efficacy today? How do you balance moving fast on AI with responsible-AI guardrails at this scale? And practically, what’s the remote or in-office expectation for this specific role? I’d also love to know what the first program-experience problem is that you’d want this person to take on. Pick 3 or 4; the efficacy and remote ones are the highest value.
