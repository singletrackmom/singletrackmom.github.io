⭐ The pilot question

Planning and evaluating pilots is in the job description, so he will ask when I ran a pilot and my process. Say the process first, then one story in STAR.

My pilot process, one line:

“I define what I am trying to move before I build, test it small with real users against a baseline, and fix it from what actually happens instead of what I hoped. I do not scale anything to everyone until the evidence says it is really helping, and if the data says the approach is wrong I rework it rather than force it.”

On “killing” a prototype: He is not going to ask whether I would kill a prototype, and I do not have to say I would. I love my prototypes and they need fixing, not killing. The rigor he is listening for is that I do not roll something out to everyone just because the demo looked good, I test it, fix it, and follow the evidence, reworking the approach if the data says so. That is the honest version of the same discipline.

Option A · Render usability pilot (recommended)

S Render is a career-launch tool I designed and coded myself, a personal learning environment a student builds across a capstone and keeps. I had a working prototype but no evidence it worked for students rather than for me.

T Before it went near a graded course, I needed to know where real students got stuck. So the task was a structured usability pilot, not a demo.

A I recruited students, gave them real tasks, and watched, no rescuing. I logged every hesitation, scored the friction by severity, and rebuilt the worst offenders. Then set up a supervised Fall pilot with Career Services with a defined outcome to measure, not “did they like it.”

R The pilot changed the product: the flow that made sense to me confused students, and I cut it. It is honestly still a prototype heading into that Fall pilot, and that is the point. I test before I scale.

Guardrail: do not claim production or a success percentage I cannot verify.

Option B · District Barriers method pilot (scale + rigor)

S A UX study across all ten Maricopa colleges and roughly 140,000 students. That big, it fails if the instrument is wrong.

T So before fieldwork I piloted the method itself, a small run to prove the walkthrough protocol and scoring held before scaling to ten colleges.

A Twelve persona-driven walkthroughs, each barrier scored on a Nielsen severity rating and confirmed by a second rater. I watched whether the protocol produced consistent, decision-useful findings.

R The pilot surfaced its first catastrophic-severity finding and proved the instrument. I earned the right to scale because I tested small first.

Option C · Course rebuild pilot (teaching)

S Commissioned to rebuild my program’s highest-enrollment, lowest-success course, the one students were withdrawing from.

T Rather than redesign on instinct, I treated the rebuild as a pilot: change deliberately, teach it, watch persistence.

A Reworked it module by module from retention research, wove in student-support touchpoints tied to an institutional learning outcome, taught it, watched engagement and completion through Canvas analytics.

If he pushes “how did you know it worked?” “I set the metric before I built. For a learning pilot that is persistence and completion, not satisfaction. The hard questions (assessment validity)

My position in three sentences:

1. When a model can produce the artifact, the artifact stops being evidence of who produced it. Construct validity, not academic integrity.

2. Detection is not the answer. Detectors are unreliable and do nothing about the student who used AI well, who is the one we want to graduate.

3. Change what the assessment collects. A model produces a finished thing, not a trail of having thought. Collect the trail: the commitment before knowing, the alternative rejected, the revision under a real consequence, the decision defended live.

1 · Scale (the one that can kill me)

“Your model is one designer with one SME. We have 175,000 students. How does this scale?”

Honest: “It does not scale as a bespoke design engagement, and I won’t pretend otherwise. What scales is the pattern, not the instance. The pairing produces a reusable template: what the scenario has to do, what the rubric collects, what a defensible response looks like. The expensive part is the first one in a discipline; after that it is authoring, not designing. And the evaluator’s job may get easier, reasoning a student can defend is easier to score than an artifact where you’re quietly wondering who wrote it.” Then: “How you get from one to a thousand at your scale is a question I’d want to work on, and I don’t have the answer today.” Turn it back: “How are your evaluators handling the ones they suspect now? What does that cost you?”

2 · Isn’t a defense just another artifact?

“Nothing stops them mechanically. But the questions are generated from what they specifically said, about a scenario they specifically saw, and they’re follow-ups. A student who didn’t do the thinking can’t survive three rounds of ‘why not the other option?’ with a model in the loop, because the model doesn’t know what they chose. It gets slower than just doing the work.” The better answer: “Stop trying to make it impossible. Design the AI use in and grade the disclosure, ask the student to delineate what the model produced from what they produced, and grade the delineation. That works on the student who used AI well.”

3 · Prove it works

“I don’t have that evidence and I won’t claim it. I have a diagnostic in testing and a design not yet built. The first question is only whether it reads as credible to the faculty who teach the subject. Whether it measures better is an empirical claim I haven’t tested, and I’d test it properly before scaling. That’s my whole no-scale-until-proven position, and I won’t abandon it for my own idea.”

4 · Why isn’t proctoring enough?

“Proctoring protects recall and can’t protect the thing you actually certify. You can’t proctor a business analysis into being authentic. And the moment you proctor everything you’ve become a testing center and given up the thing that makes WGU worth more than one.”

5 · The surveillance question

“Against it. Render stores a first name in the browser, CopaMigo stores nothing, the Quality Suite reads a package with no student data by design. Process evidence doesn’t require surveillance, it requires asking for the process as a deliverable. ‘Submit the working file, the version you rejected, and one paragraph on what the critique changed.’ The student hands you the trail.”

6 · You’re community college faculty

Don’t get defensive, don’t apologize twice. “I haven’t run assessment at 175,000 students and won’t pretend. I have run a study across ten colleges and ~140,000 students, the scale question in another domain, and I hold courses across every discipline to a national standard as a lead reviewer, which is assessment-validity work under another name. What community college gives me is proximity: the frightened faculty walk into my office and I watch what actually happens.”

7 · What did your own tool find?

If it flagged a lot, say so and say what I changed, the credibility is in turning the instrument on myself first. If I haven’t run the full pass, say so and why (Canvas isn’t exporting my learning outcomes, which is itself a finding). Do not invent a number.

8 · What would falsify this?

“If students produce equally strong reasoning trails with a model in the loop and without one, the trail isn’t measuring what I think. If evaluators can’t score reasoning reliably, inter-rater reliability collapses and it’s worse than what it replaced. If it takes so much longer that only students with time can pass, I’ve built an equity problem and should stop.” A futures person respects a falsification condition more than a vision statement.

9 · The actual future (5 years)

Give a validity answer, not a technology one. “Content becomes abundant and nearly free, so the scarce thing is judgment. The artifact stops being the unit of assessment and the trail becomes the unit. Assessment stops being an event at the end and becomes continuous evidence along the way, which CBE is already better positioned for. The institutions that survive are the ones that can still credibly say their credential means the person can do the thing. Validity is about to be the whole competitive advantage.”

10 · Why us?

“Because you have the most to lose and gain. Every institution has this problem; most can hide behind seat time. You can’t, your degree is a claim about competence backed by an assessment and nothing else. You have to solve this first, and properly. It’s the most interesting version of the problem, and the one where getting it right actually matters.”

Futurist / director questions

Reframe, ground in shipped work, land a thesis.

1 · Where is learning headed, and what can an institution do a model company never will?

Content is collapsing to nearly free and infinite, so the scarce thing is judgment and a human who actually became capable. A model company optimizes for capability and engagement. An institution optimizes for what they structurally can’t: whether a real person crossed a threshold and can be trusted by an employer to have done it. Our edge is trust, credentialing of genuine competence, human formation, and a mission pointed at the people the market ignores.

2 · Assessment when the model can do the task

Grading the artifact means grading the machine. Move assessment onto the judgment: can you direct the tool, critique what it gives you, catch it when it’s confidently wrong, make the call it can’t. That’s the competency worth certifying. CBE has an unfair advantage if it shifts from “can you produce X” to “can you make the decisions a professional makes under real conditions.”

3 · Human-in-the-loop at scale

It breaks only if a human touches every interaction, that’s the luxury version. Redefine it: a human present at the moments that decide whether a learner stays, the doubt, the barrier, the quiet “maybe I should quit.” AI absorbs the infinite-scale work; deploy the human surgically. CopaMigo is this made concrete. Scale the machine; concentrate the human.

4 · Rigor vs hype, how do I know a pilot works before you scale it?

I define the outcome before I build and refuse to scale on enthusiasm. Every pilot starts with the number I’m moving and a baseline. Small pilot, measured delta, hard lines. And the tell: I won’t scale a pilot that demoed brilliantly but didn’t move the number, I’ll rework it until it does. Anyone can ship a demo; the strategist says “this one didn’t work” and means it.

5 · Student-owned agent vs accreditation and data

Separate the layers. The learner’s personal environment (tools, sources, agent they keep) and the institutional record of demonstrated competency are different data, and I don’t let them collapse. Render is built this way, no student data by design; the credential lives with the institution, the agent with the student. Certify the competency, don’t own the student’s whole cognitive life.

6 · Biggest way AI makes education worse

Cognitive offloading that looks exactly like learning, excellent work, nothing learned, invisible because the artifact looks like success. Engineer against it: make the thinking visible and assess that, scaffold AI to provoke reasoning not hand over answers, measure whether the human got more capable. After McLuhan: every medium reshapes how we think. If we’re not deliberate, this one teaches learned helplessness beautifully.

7 · Build, buy, or ignore?

Start from the journey and the number I’m moving, never the tool. Filter: does this touch a barrier I’ve measured? Build only what’s core and where owning the pedagogy and data matters (Render). Buy the commodity infrastructure. Ignore the feature masquerading as a strategy and the tool that demos gorgeously but can’t show an outcome.

8 · Does AI widen the gap or close it?

Both, and left alone it widens, the resourced learner gets a superpower, the struggling one gets a fluent wrong answer they can’t challenge. Closing it is a design choice: build for the bottom of the distribution first. CopaMigo aims exactly there. Build for the median and AI will happily widen the gap for you.

9 · A belief I had to abandon

That a search result counts as evidence. In the journey study, four early findings fell apart once I opened the actual pages. Now a search result can claim only so much, an opened page can claim more, and I applied that rule back across the whole study.

10 · If AI personalizes the whole path, what’s the institution for? The faculty?

The institution stops delivering content, that role is over. It becomes the thing that certifies a human grew capable, and the community that holds them while they do. The faculty member is less lecturer, more mentor and designer, the person who says “you can do this” at the moment a learner is ready to walk away. Content scales infinitely; belief and belonging don’t. The machine handles the path; the human is why anyone stays on it.

Bonus · What does the future of AI in education look like?

Agentic and adaptive. Agentic: systems that do work on a learner’s behalf, mapping the gap and personalizing in real time. Adaptive: assessment moves to authentic, simulation-based demonstration. Underneath, the non-negotiable: the human stays in the loop and nothing scales until tested for efficacy. Not a prediction I’m waiting on, the thing I’m building toward now.

The risk is real, AI makes it easy to build something and overclaim, so I’m careful about what I am and am not. My expertise is learning design and the student experience; that’s the domain I own. When I build, I start the way I make my beginning design students start, with a requirements document: who’s the audience, what’s the goal, what problem are we solving. The tools I make are prototypes to test an idea, not products I’d call proven, my campus bot and Render are in testing, not production. And I know where my expertise ends: for production engineering, security, psychometrics, and rigorous efficacy studies, I partner with experts, which is exactly what WGU Labs is for. I’d rather pilot, measure, and find out something already exists than ship and overclaim.

4A promising AI capability lands tomorrow. How do you decide whether and how to bring it in at scale?

My first question is always, what problem are we solving, is this real or is it the shiny new thing. I learned that the hard way as an instructional technologist: we once took “free” SharePoint because of a Microsoft contract instead of defining the problem first, and spent two years forcing the wrong tool to build an intranet. So even if a capability looks exciting, I’d write a clear requirements document tied to a student outcome, then run a small pilot with a power user who’s genuinely on board, never with skeptics. I’d test whether it actually moves the outcome, expand to a second pilot, and only scale once it’s proven. That test-before-you-scale discipline is exactly how WGU Labs already works.

5Where should there always be a human in the loop, and how do you protect data and trust?

I can’t think of many places where there shouldn’t be a human in the loop. I start every class modeling responsible use and teaching critical thinking, because many students were told AI is cheating, so I reframe it: you’re here to learn to think, so use it, but interrogate what it gives you. Same with my agents, I always review and iterate the output rather than trust it blindly. On data, my rule is simple: no student PII goes into AI. My tools collect a first name at most, I work from sandboxed copies of courses, and nothing touches live student data. If security built a vetted, FERPA-compliant path, I’d be open to it, but I’d never improvise that myself. With working adults, trust is the whole game, and you earn it by collecting the minimum.

6How do you assess real competency when AI can produce the work?

I assess what students can do by scaffolding the process, not just grading the final artifact. Every project starts with thumbnail sketches, a design brief, and storyboards, so I watch the thinking develop. AI is welcome as a thinking partner or to polish writing, but not to make the art, because the point is to learn the craft, and a finished-looking AI image with no process behind it is a conversation. Because everything is staged and personal, like a goals statement that has to be theirs, AI shortcuts don’t get a student through. That’s authentic, competency-based assessment, and it matters more than ever: when AI can fake the output, you assess the process and what the student can actually produce.

7Pitch an AI idea for your first 90 days.

I’d want to validate whether there’s a need for a portable career-launch tool, because I’ve built one and the early signal is promising. It’s called Render. In my capstone, students pull everything from the semester into it, aligned to one real reach job they choose on day one: job search, resumes, mock interviews, a skills-gap analysis, and a personal learning plan with a schedule to close the gaps. They graduate owning it, it lives on their machine and keeps working after the course ends, and faculty in other disciplines have already asked for a version. For WGU’s working adults, a take-with-you, continued-learning plan tied to real goals fits CBE well. But I’d start in discovery: confirm the need and check whether something already exists, then pilot and measure before building.

8How would you think about belonging and persistence in a self-paced model?

This is a question I care about, because connected, supported students persist, and self-paced models are where that’s hardest. On my campus I attack it two ways: a bot that connects students to support services they don’t always know to ask for, and a Discord that ties faculty, tutoring, and students together informally, where students help each other and we run critiques. That peer layer is what sets our async courses apart. WGU already has real strengths here, the mentor model and early-alert notifications, so I wouldn’t reinvent it. I’d start by measuring it: do students actually feel supported and connected, and where are the gaps. Then I’d test lightweight, always-on ways to add peer connection without breaking self-pacing. Human-in-the-loop, measured against persistence, piloted before scaling.

Round 2 · Behavioral, PM & Closing

9Tell me about a time you turned a new technology into something people actually used.

Early in my career I had a graphic design job where on day one someone handed me HTML and said learn it, and a coworker and I built one of the first Fortune 100 company websites. The web didn’t exist as a medium when I trained, so I carried what I knew about design and how people read into a brand-new tool. I think about AI exactly the same way. This past year I learned it by building with it, then brought it into my classes so students get literate and responsible with it, and built Render so the whole job-search process lives in one place they own. The throughline is always the same: learn the new medium on its own terms, carry the durable design principles into it, and make something people actually use.

10Tell me about aligning people who didn’t report to you.

Most of my influence has nothing to do with authority. I co-chair the Student Support and Success domain of our district AI Resource Center, working across all 10 colleges, none of whom report to me, so it’s all shared purpose and listening. On my own campus I’ve gotten faculty to try AI not by mandating it but by starting an informal community of practice and a Discord where early adopters share what works, and letting curiosity spread. As a product manager at an ed-tech company I worked from requirements engineers had built and had to align design, engineering, and stakeholders without owning any of them. The pattern is always the same: anchor on the student outcome everyone already cares about, make it easy and low-risk to say yes, and let the wins do the convincing.

11Tell me about managing a complex project from initiation to completion.

The clearest one is leading our campus LMS team. I led the LMS team during the Blackboard era and contributed to the evaluation and RFP that ended with our district moving to Canvas. That meant defining requirements with faculty, and evaluating vendors against those requirements. I was not on the migration team; my part was the requirements, the evaluation, and supporting faculty on my campus. It’s exactly the discipline I’d bring to AI decisions at WGU: define the problem and the requirements first, evaluate against them, pilot, then roll out with support, rather than buying or building first and forcing it to fit later.

12Describe a project that stalled or failed. What did you learn?

An early version of my campus support bot taught me a real lesson. When students asked about courses, the AI would confidently invent course names and numbers that didn’t exist, because it was pulling from training data instead of our actual catalog. It looked polished and was wrong, which is the worst combination in a student-facing tool. So I pulled it back, added guardrails so it only draws from verified information, and made “never trust the model for specifics” a rule. The lesson was that fluency isn’t accuracy, and anything student-facing has to be grounded and tested before it goes live. That’s a big part of why I’m so careful now about the line between a prototype and a production tool.

13How do you measure whether a program-experience change actually worked?

I try to measure the outcome, not the activity. With our lowest-success foundation course, the redesign isn’t “done” because we shipped it, it’s measured by whether the success rate actually moves, which we check against the retention research it’s grounded in. For something like student connection, I’d use support-and-belonging surveys and persistence data, not how many features we added. In Render there’s a funnel diagnosis that reads a student’s real activity to find where their search is stalling. The principle is the same one I’d bring to WGU: judge a change by learning, retention, and engagement, not by what got built.

14A faculty group resists an AI rollout. What do you do?

I’ve lived this, so I’d start with empathy, not a mandate. A lot of faculty are anxious about AI, around cheating, around being replaced, around extra work, and those concerns are legitimate. I’d never roll out to skeptics first. I’d start with willing power users, make the value obvious with their own students, and create a low-pressure community of practice where people share what’s working instead of being trained at. I’d also be honest about the guardrails, no student PII, human in the loop, AI as a partner and not a replacement. Adoption follows trust and visible wins, not pressure. On my campus that informal, voluntary approach has done far more than any top-down push could.

15What does a Product Owner own, and how do you prioritize when everything’s urgent?

I’ll be upfront that my PSPO certification is in progress, I’m finishing it this summer, so I’m speaking from product experience plus what I’ve studied. A Product Owner owns the value of the product and the ordered backlog, deciding what gets built next and, just as importantly, saying no to protect the goal. When everything feels urgent, I prioritize by student impact: what moves the outcome for the most learners, tested in small increments. Ship a thin slice, check whether it actually works, then scale, rather than committing big to something unproven. That’s the same evidence-first rhythm WGU Labs uses, and it’s how I’d run AI pilots here.

16What questions do you have for us? (pick 3 or 4)

What does success look like for this role in the first 6 to 12 months? How does the Program Experience Futures team work day to day with WGU Labs and the academic programs? How are AI pilots scoped, governed, and tested for efficacy today? How do you balance moving fast on AI with responsible-AI guardrails at this scale? And practically, what’s the remote or in-office expectation for this specific role? I’d also love to know the first program-experience problem you’d want this person to take on. (The efficacy and remote ones are the highest value.)
