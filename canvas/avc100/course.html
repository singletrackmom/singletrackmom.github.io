Introduction to Digital Arts

AVC 100

In 8 weeks online, you make a travel poster, a photo postcard and a short animation about one place, then publish all three on your own portfolio site in Google Sites.

Module 0Getting StartedWeek 1 · about 3 hours

Objectives

  • 0.1 Set up and test every account and app the course uses (Course Competency 1)
  • 0.2 Record and share a short screen recording of your work, with your voice (Course Competency 4)
  • 0.3 Introduce yourself on Discord with two examples of your work, each with alt text (Course Competency 1)

How this course works

  • Course: AVC 100, Introduction to Digital Arts. 1 credit, lecture and lab, 8 weeks, fully online. There are no set class meeting times. Plan on about 5 hours a week, 40 hours in all.
  • What you make: three pieces about one place you choose: an 18 × 24 inch poster in Illustrator, a 4 × 6 inch photo postcard in Photoshop, and a short phone-size animation of the postcard in After Effects. At the end, you publish all three on a portfolio site you build in Google Sites.
  • Three steps: every project has three steps. Sketch on paper, Build in the software, then Refine from classmates’ feedback.
  • Each module uses the same order: Objectives, Overview, Watch, Read, Practice, Assessment and Critique. Each graded item names the objective it measures and the course competency it supports.
  • Log in to Canvas and Discord at least three times a week. If you cannot use Discord, post in the matching Canvas discussion instead.

Deadlines

Work is due Thursday or Sunday at noon, Arizona time, in the week shown on each item. Canvas shows the exact dates. For project steps, post on Discord by noon the day before. Late work loses 10% and is accepted for 24 hours only.

Your instructor

  • Your instructor’s name, photo and video are on the Canvas home page.
  • Canvas Inbox: the best way to reach your instructor (how to use the Inbox). Messages sent Monday to Friday get a reply within one business day.
  • Discord: ask course questions in the AVC100 channel, so everyone sees the answer.
  • Need more time? Message your instructor before the 24-hour late window ends, for any reason.

Critique guidelines

Critique means giving feedback on design work. Talk about the work, not the person. Start each comment with “I noticed,” “I wondered” or “What if.” Name a design principle and the part of the work that shows it, for example: “The headline is the first thing I see.”

Accessibility of your work

Your work must also work for people who cannot see it or hear it. About 10% of each rubric is for this.

  • Images: add alt text to every image that matters. Alt text is one or two sentences that describe the image for someone who cannot see it.
  • Animation: post a one-sentence description of the motion. If it has sound, add captions.
  • Captions: make them automatically (YouTube Studio or Premiere Pro Speech to Text), then fix every line by hand.

Module 1Graphic Design Poster: IllustratorWeeks 2 to 4 · about 10.5 hours

Objectives

  • 1.1 Build a five-color palette for each of five made-up clients and explain how each fits the client’s audience (Course Competency 2)
  • 1.2 Trace shapes with the Illustrator Pen tool, making closed paths with clean anchor points (Course Competency 3)
  • 1.3 Sketch and capture twenty thumbnails for a travel poster, and explain your choice by naming its hierarchy and contrast (Course Competency 4)
  • 1.4 Build an 18 × 24 inch vector travel poster with a set palette, limited type and a clear focal point, then revise it from two peer critiques and export a print-ready PDF (Course Competency 2)

Overview

Choose one destination: a city, a country or a national park. You use it in all three projects. In this module you make a tall travel poster in the style of classic travel posters. Use bold, simple shapes of the place’s best-known features. For Zion National Park, that could be bighorn sheep, red cliffs and Angels Landing. Use one title typeface and a set palette (a small group of colors). First you do two short exercises: a color palette and the Pen tool.

Key words: vector art is made of paths, so it stays sharp at any size. Thumbnails are small, quick sketches. Hierarchy is the order in which the eye reads things. The focal point is where the eye goes first. In critique, look for contrast, hierarchy and alignment.

Setup: install Illustrator from Creative Cloud. Make a free Miro account with your Maricopa email. Miro is an online whiteboard. You keep every project step on one Miro board.

Watch

  • One captioned instructor video: The Poster, Start to Finish. Its Refine section is used for all three projects.
  • Sarah Renae Clark, “Color Theory Basics”
  • The Futur, “10 Rules to Help You Rule Type”

Read

  • Kendra Cherry, “Color Psychology: Does It Affect How You Feel?”

  • Reference images: Google Images and the National Park Service photo search. Use them for ideas and tracing only, never in the final poster.

Exercise: Draw with the Pen Tool (10 points, Exercises, Week 3)

Measures Objective 1.2 (Course Competency 3).

  1. In Illustrator, choose File, then New. Set Width to 8.5 in and Height to 11 in. Choose View, then Rulers, then Show Rulers. Drag guides out from the rulers to line up your shapes.
  2. With the Pen tool, copy the practice shapes: a wavy line, a row of circles, a row of cursive loops, a square, a triangle, a star and a row of ocean waves. Click for straight lines. Click and drag for curves.
  3. Drag the handles on a few anchor points to change the curves. Then draw a few shapes of your own.
  4. Save as Adobe PDF and submit it in Canvas.

Rubric (10, all Objective 1.2): practice shapes as closed paths with clean anchor points (5); handles adjusted and your own shapes (2); guides used to line up shapes (1); PDF submitted (1); AI-use log row (1).

Poster Step 1: Sketch (10 points, Process Work, Week 3)

Measures Objective 1.3 (Course Competency 4).

  1. Collect at least 10 reference images of your destination’s best-known features.
  2. Draw 20 thumbnails of the poster on paper, taller than wide. Photograph or scan them flat, in even light and in focus, so the lines are clear in Miro.
  3. Choose one title typeface. Build a five-color palette in Adobe Color.
  4. Mark the thumbnail you will build. Write two sentences about its hierarchy and contrast.
  5. Put everything on one Miro board.

Poster Step 2: Build (10 points, Process Work, Week 4)

Measures Objective 1.4 (Course Competency 2).

  1. Choose File, then New. Set Width to 18 in and Height to 24 in. This page is your artboard.
  2. Choose File, then Place, and place a reference image. In the Layers panel, set its opacity to about 30% and lock the layer.
  3. Make a new layer. Trace a few bold, simple shapes with the Pen tool, such as a ridge or a cactus. Skip the small details.
  4. Fill the shapes with your palette from the Swatches panel. Start with the background.
  5. Type the destination name with the Type tool in your title typeface.
  6. Choose Save As, then Adobe PDF. Place the PDF on your Miro board.

Rubric (10, all Objective 1.4): best-known features as simple vector shapes (3); palette and title type used (1); 18 × 24 inch artboard (1); one clear focal point (1); alt text on the board screenshot (1); Discord post and three critique comments added to the board (2); AI-use log row (1).

Poster Step 3: Refine (10 points, Process Work, Week 4)

Measures Objective 1.4 (Course Competency 2).

  1. Make at least two changes based on specific critiques from Step 2: shapes, spacing, color or type.
  2. Hold Shift to select objects, then line them up with the Align panel. Make sure the poster reads from across the room. Simplify anything too busy.
  3. Write two or three sentences: what you changed, why, and which design principle it uses.
  4. Export a print-ready PDF at 18 × 24 inches in CMYK (the color mode printers use), and a JPG for the web.

Rubric (10, all Objective 1.4): revised from two specific critiques (3); what changed and why, naming the principle (2); print-ready PDF at full size in CMYK (2); alt text on the board screenshot (1); Discord post and three critique comments (1); AI-use log row (1).

Module 2Photographic Postcard: PhotoshopWeeks 5 to 6 · about 10 hours

Objectives

  • 2.1 Cut out three subjects from their backgrounds with Select Subject, the Quick Selection tool and Select and Mask, and clean up the edges (Course Competency 3)
  • 2.2 Sketch and capture ten postcard layouts that use the poster’s typeface and palette, and name the selection method each part needs (Course Competency 4)
  • 2.3 Composite a destination photo, a cut-out animal and type into a 1200 × 1800 pixel PSD, with each part on its own layer (Course Competency 2)
  • 2.4 Revise the postcard from two peer critiques and prepare the file at the correct size, resolution and color mode (Course Competency 5)

Overview

The postcard shows the same destination as your poster. It has three parts: a photo of the place as the background, an animal cut out of a different photo and placed on top, and type in the poster’s typeface and palette. It is tall, 4 × 6 inches at 300 ppi (pixels per inch), which is 1200 × 1800 pixels.

Key words: to composite is to combine parts of several photos into one image. A PSD is a Photoshop file that keeps each layer separate. Keep every part on its own layer. Module 3 animates these layers, and a flattened file (all one layer) cannot be animated. In critique, look for proximity, repetition and white space.

Setup: install Photoshop from Creative Cloud.

Watch

One captioned instructor video: The Postcard: Select and Build. Rewatch the photographing and Refine sections of the Module 1 video.

Read

  • Using images the right way. Most images online belong to someone. Using them without permission breaks copyright law, even for a class. Use Google Images for ideas only. For your final photos, use Unsplash, Pexels (its color filter helps match your palette) or Pixabay, or take your own.
  • Creative Commons, “About CC Licenses”. CC0: no credit needed. CC BY: give credit. CC BY-NC: no commercial use. CC BY-ND: no changes, so you cannot cut out an animal from it.

Practice

  • On one practice photo, try Select Subject and then the Quick Selection tool on the same subject. Compare the results.

Assessment

Exercise: Select Objects (10 points, Exercises, Week 5)

Measures Objective 2.1 (Course Competency 3).

  1. Download three practice photos from Pexels, or use your own subjects on a real background: ice cream cone (easy), potted plant (medium), golden retriever (fur).
  2. Make a new 1500 × 1500 pixel file at 300 ppi. Fill it with a color using the Paint Bucket tool.
  3. For each photo: choose Select Subject. Add any missed areas with the Quick Selection tool.
  4. Open Select and Mask. For the dog, brush the fur edge with the Refine Edge Brush tool. Set Output To: Layer Mask and click OK. A layer mask hides the background without deleting it.
  5. With the Move tool, drag the layer onto your page. Hold Shift and use Free Transform to resize it.
  6. Choose Export As, then JPG, and submit it in Canvas.

Rubric (10, all Objective 2.1): three subjects cut out (4); clean edges, including Select and Mask on the fur (3); JPG submitted with alt text (2); AI-use log row (1).

Module 3Social Media Animation: After EffectsWeeks 7 to 8 · about 10 hours

Objectives

  • 3.1 Move one layer into the frame with two Position keyframes, and explain in your own words what a keyframe does (Course Competency 3)
  • 3.2 Sketch and capture four to six storyboard frames with arrows, planning the order and timing of each move (Course Competency 4)
  • 3.3 Import a layered PSD into After Effects as a composition, and animate the animal and the type with pairs of Position keyframes that match the storyboard (Course Competency 2)
  • 3.4 Revise the animation from two peer critiques and export a trimmed H.264 MP4 of about five seconds (Course Competency 5)

Overview

The animation is your postcard in motion. It is about five seconds long and tall, like an Instagram or TikTok story. The background stays still. The animal slides in from one side and the type from the other. You already made the art, so the new skill is motion.

Key words: a keyframe marks where a layer is at one moment. Each move uses two Position keyframes: one just outside the frame, and one where the layer ends up on the postcard. A storyboard is a set of quick sketches that plan the motion. A composition is an After Effects scene. H.264 MP4 is a common video file that plays almost anywhere. In critique, look for balance, emphasis and movement.

Setup: install After Effects and Adobe Media Encoder from Creative Cloud.

  • Adobe, Adobe Learn: After Effects (the getting-started tutorials)

Assessment

Exercise: Animate with Keyframes (10 points, Exercises, Week 7)

Measures Objective 3.1 (Course Competency 3).

  1. Choose Composition, then New Composition. Set it to 1080 × 1920 pixels and 5 seconds.
  2. Drag in an image, such as one of your Select Objects cut-outs.
  3. Select the layer and press P. At the start of the timeline, click the Position stopwatch. Drag the image just outside the frame. Move the playhead about one second. Drag the image to where it should land. Select both keyframes and press F9 (Easy Ease) so the move slows to a stop.
  4. Press the spacebar to preview. Press N to end the work area there. Choose Composition, then Add to Adobe Media Encoder Queue, and export an H.264 MP4.
  5. Write one sentence, in your own words, on what a keyframe does.

Rubric (10, all Objective 3.1): two Position keyframes bring the image into the frame (5); keyframe explained in your own words (2); MP4 submitted (2); AI-use log row (1).

Animation Step 2: Build (10 points, Process Work, Week 8)

Measures Objective 3.3 (Course Competency 2).

  1. Choose File, then Import, then File. Choose your postcard PSD. Set Import Kind to Composition – Retain Layer Sizes, so each part comes in on its own layer.
  2. In the Project panel, double-click the composition to open it.
  3. Select the animal layer and press P. At the start, click the stopwatch and drag the animal just outside its entrance edge. Move about one second and drag it to where it rests. Select both keyframes and press F9.
  4. Do the same on the type layer, from the other side, to match your storyboard.
  5. Press the spacebar to preview. Move to the end of the motion and press N to cut the extra time.
  6. Choose Composition, then Add to Adobe Media Encoder Queue. Choose H.264, set the Output File to a folder you can find, and click the green play button.

Rubric (10, all Objective 3.3): layers imported separately, and both parts move as storyboarded (3); typeface and palette from the postcard (1); about five seconds, trimmed (1); one clear focal point in the final frame (1); one-sentence description of the motion with the post (1); Discord post and three critique comments added to the board (2); AI-use log row (1).

Video: The Animation: Keyframes to Export (about 12 minutes)

  • One idea: a keyframe records where something is at one moment. Set two and the software draws the frames between. Ask: in your own words, what does a keyframe do?
  • Storyboard: four to six frames showing what happens over time, with timing in numbers (animal 0 to 1 second, type 1 to 1.5). The final frame matches the postcard.

Take Your Work With You (ungraded, Week 8)

Your Maricopa Google account may close after you leave the college. Do this before the course ends, so you keep everything you made even if you stop out, transfer or graduate. It takes about 45 minutes.

  1. Save your files in one place. Make one Google Drive folder for this course. Put in it your Illustrator, Photoshop and After Effects files, exported PDF, JPG and MP4 files, sketch photos and your AI-use log.
  2. Copy everything to a personal Google account. Make a personal Google account if you do not have one. First make your own copy (File, then Make a copy) of any file someone shared with you, because files you do not own are not copied. Then, signed in to your Maricopa account, open Google Takeout Transfer and copy your Drive and Gmail to your personal account (see Google’s help page). It can take up to a week, and the copies arrive unshared. If Transfer is not available on your account, use Google Takeout to download your Drive as .zip files and save them on your computer.
  3. Keep your portfolio site. It lives on your Maricopa account, so move it before you leave: share it with your personal email as an editor, sign in with that account, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing is blocked, rebuild it at sites.google.com with the same text and images, and publish it as Restricted again. If you already have a Behance portfolio from another class, move your Behance profile to a free personal Adobe account before your school account closes.
  4. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
