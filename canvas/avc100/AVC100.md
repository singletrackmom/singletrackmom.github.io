### The tool lesson always lands the Saturday *before* the project opens

# AVC 100 · Fall Restructure (3-Part Projects)

**Status: PROPOSAL.** Nothing edited yet. Decisions needed at the bottom.

**Goal:** Give all three projects the same 3-part shape, so students learn the rhythm once and reuse it, and so critique finally lands where it can change the work. Also cut the video load, because other faculty will copy this course and every video is a maintenance liability.

**Audience:** Me, and the faculty who will copy this course down and teach from it.

**Process:** Every project runs Sketch → Build → Refine. The software warm-up exercise is due the same day as Part 1. Never more than two graded items due on one day.

---

## The pattern

| | Part 1 · Sketch | Part 2 · Build | Part 3 · Refine |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Poster** (Illustrator) | Thumbnails + plan | Develop | Act on critique, finalize, export |
| **Postcard** (Photoshop) | Sketch + plan | Build | Act on critique, finalize, export |
| **Animation** (After Effects) | Storyboard | Build | Act on critique, finalize, export |

**Part 1 is always sketching. Part 3 is always acting on feedback.** The software exercise is due with Part 1, so students touch the software the same week they plan in it.

## ⚠️ The thing this actually fixes (the real reason to do it)

**Right now the critique is decorative.** Students post to Discord two days before a deadline, receive feedback, and then submit. They have no time to act on it. Everyone knows this, which is why peer critique in most courses is polite noise.

**In the 3-part structure, the critique round sits between Part 2 and Part 3.** Feedback arrives *after* they have built something and *before* they finalize it, which is exactly where critique belongs in a real design process.

**Part 3 is "act on the critique."** That is the whole assignment. The rubric writes itself:

> Identified at least two specific pieces of feedback · Made a visible, defensible change in response · Explained what changed and why · Exported final files correctly

**Important design choice:** the feedback that drives Part 3 is **peer critique, not my grading.** If Part 3 depended on my turnaround, the structure would collapse the first week I got behind. My grade on Part 2 is confirmation, not the input. This is also better pedagogy: taking critique from peers is what actually happens in a studio.

# AVC 100 · Objectives Rewrite

**Status: PROPOSAL.** Rewritten from the repo file. **Re-verify against the sandbox `.imscc`** once it lands, in case the sandbox objectives differ.

**Goal:** Make every objective observable, so it can actually be assessed, and so the principles-of-design scaffold stops being passive.

**Audience:** Me, and the faculty copying this course.

**Process:** Revised Bloom's (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). Ban the unmeasurable verbs. Target level for a 100-level studio: **Apply, Analyze, Create.**

---

## The rule

> **If you cannot observe it, you cannot assess it.**

"Understand," "learn about," and "be introduced to" describe what happens inside a student's head. Nobody can see inside a student's head, so nobody can grade it.

**The trap:** *Understand* is a valid Bloom's **level** and an invalid objective **verb**. The level names the cognition. The verb has to name the **observable evidence** of it.

---

## 🔴 The pattern problem

**"You will also be introduced to a few of the principles of design."**

This line appears **three times**, once per unit. It is passive and unmeasurable. *Being introduced to* something is not an outcome, it is something that happens to the student.

**And this is the most important content in the course.** You told me terminology, critique, and design principles are what AVC100 is actually for. That content is currently carried by the weakest objective in the file.

**The fix:** students do not get *introduced to* the principles. They **identify and name them in each other's work.** That is observable, gradeable, and it is exactly what the critique component needs.

| Unit | Principles introduced | New measurable objective |
|---|---|---|
| Illustrator | Contrast, Hierarchy, Alignment | "**Identify** contrast, hierarchy, and alignment in a classmate's poster, and **name** the element each one is acting on." |
| Photoshop | Proximity, Repetition, White space | "**Locate** proximity, repetition, and white space in a classmate's postcard, and **describe** the effect each has on your eye." |
| After Effects | Balance, Emphasis, Movement | "**Analyze** how balance and emphasis change over time in a classmate's animation." |

## How to build this in Canvas

Canvas supports **outcome groups**, which is exactly the two-tier structure.

1. **Outcomes** → **+ Group**. Create five groups, one per competency, named for the district text:
   *1 · Operate a computer* · *2 · Create digital art and design projects* · and so on.
2. Inside each group, **+ Outcome** for each of *my* objectives that rolls up to it. Objective 2 lives in two groups; Canvas allows an outcome in one group, so **file each objective under its primary competency** and note the secondary in the description.
3. On each assignment's rubric: **Find Outcome** → pick **my objective**, not the competency.
4. **The gradebook now reports mastery per objective, and per group.** Per objective for teaching. Per group for the program review.

**The competency reporting comes free.** That is the entire point of the two tiers.

---

## Why this is the better answer

**Grading the district's competencies directly would be bad teaching.** "Operate a computer" cannot be scored meaningfully, gives a student nothing to aim at, and would not tell me anything I want to know about their work.

**Grading my objectives is good teaching, and it satisfies the district anyway.** *"Trace a reference shape with the Pen tool, producing closed paths with clean anchor points"* is observable, gradeable, and something a student can act on. Competency 3 is evidenced by it whether or not anyone says the word "competency" out loud.

> **The competencies are a floor, not a curriculum.** The roll-up table proves the floor is met. The objectives are what the course is actually about.

## Photoshop Selection Practice (Cut It Out)

### Objective

Learn the Photoshop selection tools by cutting three subjects out of their backgrounds and arranging them like a sticker sheet. This is a low-stakes warm-up before the postcard project, so it is about practice, not design. You will also be introduced to a few more of the principles of design.

### Resources

Use the three practice photos provided, or your own photos if you have something appropriate. Either way, pick subjects with a **real background**, not plain white, so you actually practice the skill. All three are free on Pexels, no credit required: click **Free Download** and choose the largest size.

- **Easy** (clear single object): [melting ice cream cone on stone](https://www.pexels.com/photo/close-up-shot-of-melted-ice-cream-on-cone-9887878/)
- **Medium** (leaf edges, busier shape): [potted plant on a stone wall](https://www.pexels.com/photo/potted-plant-on-stone-wall-16827804/)
- **Trickier** (fuzzy fur, use Select and Mask): [golden retriever on grass](https://www.pexels.com/photo/adult-golden-retriever-sitting-on-grass-field-1001976/)

### Assignment

> **Principles of Design Spotlight**
>
> This exercise highlights three more principles of design. As you place your cut-outs, keep these in mind:
>
> - **Scale and proportion:** the size of elements relative to each other, and how size signals importance.
> - **Alignment:** lining elements up so the design feels organized and connected.
> - **Space:** using the empty (negative) space on purpose; what you leave out matters as much as what you put in.

Cut out three things from three different photos and arrange them on one page like a sticker sheet.

**The selection tools.** A selection tool tells Photoshop "I want THIS part, not the rest." Easiest first:

- **Select Subject** (a button at the top): one click and Photoshop finds the main subject. Start here.
- **Object Selection tool:** hover over a thing, it highlights, click to grab it.
- **Quick Selection tool:** paint over the subject and the selection grows to the edges.
- **Magic Wand tool:** selects by matching color. Fine on simple backgrounds, struggles on busy ones.
- **Select and Mask with the Refine Edge brush:** for cleaning up fuzzy edges like fur, hair, and feathers.

**Steps.** Take these one at a time. The **menus** (File, Edit, Select) are the words across the very top of the screen. The **toolbar** is the tall strip of icons down the left side. The **Options bar** is the row of choices just under the menus.

*Make your sticker page*

1. Open **Adobe Photoshop** (find it on your desktop or in your list of apps and double-click it).
2. At the top, click the **File** menu, then click **New**.
3. A box opens. In the **Width** box type `1500`. In the **Height** box type `1500`. Make sure the little menu beside them says **Pixels**. In the **Resolution** box type `300`.
4. Click the blue **Create** button. A blank white page appears. This is your sticker page.

*Fill the background with a color*

5. In the toolbar on the left, find the **Paint Bucket tool** (it looks like a tipping paint can). If you only see a **Gradient tool**, click and hold that icon for a second and choose **Paint Bucket tool** from the little menu that pops out.
6. Near the bottom of the toolbar there are two overlapping color squares. Click the **top square** (the foreground color). A color window opens. Click a color you like, then click **OK**.
7. Move your mouse onto the white page and **click once**. The whole page fills with your color.

*Open your first photo and cut out the subject*

8. Click the **File** menu, then **Open**. Find the photo you downloaded, click it once, then click **Open**. It opens in its own tab at the top.
9. In the toolbar, click the **Object Selection tool** (a dotted square with a small arrow). Then, in the **Options bar** at the top, click the **Select Subject** button and wait a second. A moving dotted line, called marching ants, appears around the main object. That is your selection.
10. If it missed a spot, click the **Quick Selection tool** in the toolbar and gently **drag** over the missed area to add it to the selection.

*Copy it onto your sticker page*

11. Click the **Edit** menu, then **Copy** (or hold the **Ctrl** key and press **C**; on a Mac hold **Command** and press **C**).
12. At the top, click the **tab for your sticker page** to switch back to it.
13. Click the **Edit** menu, then **Paste** (or **Ctrl/Cmd + V**). Your cut-out object appears on the page on its own layer.

*Resize and move it*

14. Click the **Edit** menu, then **Free Transform** (or hold **Ctrl/Cmd** and press **T**). A box with small square handles appears around your object.
15. To resize, click a **corner handle** and drag. **Hold the Shift key while you drag** so the object does not stretch out of shape. When it is the size you want, press **Enter** (**Return** on a Mac).
16. In the toolbar, click the **Move tool** (the arrow at the very top). Now click your object and drag it wherever you want on the page.

*Do it twice more*

17. Repeat steps 8 through 16 with your second and third photos, until three cut-outs sit on your page like stickers. Arrange them however you like.

*Clean up the fuzzy one*

18. Open your trickiest photo (the furry one). Click **Select Subject** in the Options bar like before.
19. In the **Options bar**, click the **Select and Mask** button. A new workspace opens.
20. On the left, click the **Refine Edge Brush tool** (a brush icon). Gently **brush along the furry edge**. Photoshop cleans up the wispy bits.
21. Click **OK** at the bottom to return. Then copy and paste this one onto your sticker page just like the others.

### Submission

When all three cut-outs are on your page, click the **File** menu, then **Export**, then **Export As**, choose **JPG**, and click **Export**. Save it, then upload that JPG here in Canvas. The points are for trying all the selection tools and getting three clean-ish cut-outs. Once you can do this, you are ready for the postcard project.

# ILLUSTRATOR, Graphic Design Poster

## Color Palette Exercise (short, ~3 min)

- **Intro (15s) Say:** "Quick one. Before we design, every project needs a color palette. We will build one in Adobe Color."
- **Build it (1.5 min) Say:** "I go to color dot adobe dot com. I pick a color harmony on the left, like Analogous or Complementary, then drag the dots on the color wheel until I have five colors I like for my topic. A desert poster might be warm oranges and tans." **Do:** Open Adobe Color, choose a harmony, drag the wheel to set 5 colors.
- **Save it (45s) Say:** "I save it to my library, or I just write down the five hex codes. In Illustrator I can add these as swatches so they are one click away." **Do:** Save the theme / copy the hex values.
- **Wrap (15s) Say:** "Five colors that fit your topic. Put your palette on your Miro board. That is it."

## Lesson and Exercise: Illustrator, Pen and Shape practice (short, ~4 min)

- **Intro (20s) Say:** "The main Illustrator skill is making clean vector shapes. We will practice with the Pen tool and the shape tools, no design pressure."
- **Shapes (1 min) Say:** "First the easy way. The Rectangle, Ellipse, and Polygon tools make instant shapes. Click and drag, hold Shift for a perfect circle or square." **Do:** Draw a few shapes with the shape tools.
- **Pen tool (1.5 min) Say:** "Now the Pen tool, the big one. Each click drops an anchor point and straight lines connect them, so I can trace any straight-edged shape. To curve a line, I click and drag, and handles appear that bend the path. Trace the practice shapes provided, straight ones first, then the curvy ones." **Do:** Click anchor points to trace a straight shape, then click-drag to make curves.
- **Wrap (30s) Say:** "Trace the practice shapes, fill them with color, and turn them in. Once the Pen tool feels okay, you are ready to build your poster."

- **Intro (20s) Say:** "Before Illustrator, we plan the poster on a Miro board."

An animated version of your postcard: a short, vertical animated video, the kind you see as an Instagram or TikTok story or reel. The pieces you already made will move. For example, your animal flies in from one side and your type slides in from the other. You built the art in your postcard, so this project is about giving those pieces motion.

## Step 1: Storyboard

### Objective

Plan the motion of your animation before you build it, reusing the same destination, animal, and type from your postcard.

### Assignment

A **storyboard** is a few frames that show what happens over time, start to finish. Thumbnails show where things sit; a storyboard shows how they move. Sketch about **4 to 6 frames:**

1. **The opening:** your destination background alone.
2. **The animal enters:** draw an arrow showing which direction it flies in from.
3. **The type enters:** draw an arrow showing which direction it comes in from.
4. **The final hold:** everything in place, matching your finished postcard.

Then add a short note on the order and timing of the moves, and gather it all on one Miro board (listed in Submission below).

Deliverables for Step 1 gathered on one Miro board:

1. Your **storyboard frames** (4 to 6), sketched on paper with direction arrows (take a photo of this to add to your Miro board).

3. A short **note on the order and timing** of the moves.
4. Take a screenshot of your Miro board and submit to Discord 2 days before the deadline to receive feedback from your classmates.
5. Give feedback to at least 4 classmates.
6. Add the feedback you gave to your classmates to your Miro board.
7. Submit the screenshot of your final Miro board with feedback attached here for your grade.

| Critique given to at least 4 classmates (added to Miro board) | 3 pts |
| Final Miro board submitted with all elements including feedback | 2 pts |

---

## Step 2: Build

### Objective

Build your animation by giving the pieces of your own postcard some motion. You already made the art, so now you bring your postcard into After Effects and make each piece move with a couple of keyframes.

### Assignment

After Effects has a **Project** panel at the top left, a **Composition** preview in the middle, a **Timeline** along the bottom, and the **menus** across the top. Two keys you will use: **P** for Position (to make things move) and **S** for Scale (to resize).

1. **Import your postcard.** Click the **File** menu, then **Import**, then **File**. Choose your postcard PSD file, set **Import As** to **Composition**, then click **Import**. Your background, animal, and type each come in on their own layer.
2. **Open it.** In the **Project** panel at the top left, double-click your postcard composition to open it in the Timeline. It opens already vertical at your postcard size, which is perfect for a story.
3. **Slide in the animal.** Click the animal layer, then press the **P** key so its Position shows. Move the playhead to the very start, click the **stopwatch** next to Position, and drag the animal just off the edge it flies in from. Move the playhead ahead about 1 second, then drag the animal to its resting spot. Those two markers (keyframes) make it slide in.
4. **Slide in the type.** Do the exact same thing on the type layer, coming in from the other side, matching your storyboard.
5. **Preview and trim.** Press the **spacebar** to watch it play. When it looks right, move the playhead to the moment your animation ends, then press the **N** key to set the end of your work area there so you do not export dead time.
6. **Export your MP4.** Click the **Composition** menu, then **Add to Adobe Media Encoder Queue**. Media Encoder opens with your animation in a list. Choose **H.264** from the Format menu, and set Match High Bitrate. Now look at the **Output File** name (in yellow): click it, choose a folder you will remember (your **Desktop** is easiest), and name the file with your own name. Then click the green **play** button at the top right. When it finishes, your **MP4** is sitting in that folder, ready to submit.

### Submission

1. Your finished animation, exported as an **MP4**.
2. **Post your MP4 to Discord** 2 days before the deadline so classmates can watch it and give feedback. Drag the MP4 file straight into the channel. If Discord says the file is too large, put it on your Miro board or Google Drive and paste the link instead.
3. Give feedback to at least 4 classmates by replying to their posts.
4. Add your MP4 (or its link) and the feedback you received to your Miro board.
5. Submit your **MP4** and a screenshot of your final Miro board (with feedback) here in Canvas for your grade.

| Criteria | Full Marks |
|----------|-----------|
| Postcard imported as a composition with layers separated | 4 pts |
| Animation works, the animal and type move | 5 pts |
| Text and pieces match the poster's typeface and palette | 1 pt |
| MP4 exported | 1 pt |

| Final Miro board submitted with MP4 and feedback | 2 pts |

# AVC 100 · Photoshop module · Using images the right way (draft to adapt)

## Using images the right way

You cannot just grab an image off the internet and use it in your work. Most images online belong to someone, and using them without permission is copyright infringement, even for a class project. So before you pull a photo into Photoshop, a few rules.

**Google Images is for inspiration and tracing only.** Use it to gather reference and get ideas. Do not use those images in your final piece.

**Pull your final photos from free, licensed sources.** These sites give you high-resolution photos that are already cleared for use, just click Free Download:

- Unsplash (unsplash.com)
- Pexels (pexels.com), which has a color filter to match your palette
- Pixabay (pixabay.com)

**What "Creative Commons" means.** Creative Commons (CC) is how creators tell you what you are allowed to do with their work. The common ones:

- CC0 / public domain: use freely, no credit needed.
- CC BY: use freely, but credit the creator.
- CC BY-NC: use and credit, non-commercial only.
- CC BY-ND: use and credit, but no changes, so you could not cut the animal out of it.

Always check the license before you use something.

**Rule of thumb for this class:** if you did not make it, and it is not clearly licensed for reuse, do not use it. When in doubt, use Unsplash, Pexels, or Pixabay, or make it yourself.

## 🔴 FINDING 2 · Lora will not render in Canvas

You asked for Lora, your design-system typeface. **It cannot work in Canvas.**

Lora is a Google Font, loaded with a `<link>` tag. **Canvas strips `<link>` tags when it sanitizes a page on save.** Your editor preview will look correct, and every student will see a fallback serif. This is the silent failure mode, and it is why the tool restricts you to five typefaces.

## 🟡 FINDING 4 · Objectives are not measurable

Checked against the banned-verb list. **This course is written at the wrong Bloom's level throughout.**

AVC100 is a 100-level intro course, so objectives should live in **Understand → Apply → Analyze.** Students should be *making* things, not *knowing about* them.

### Rewrites

| Assignment | Current (unmeasurable) | Rewritten (observable) | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Color Palette** | "Students will understand color theory." | "Using Adobe Color, **construct** a five-color palette and **justify** each choice against the poster's mood." | Create / Evaluate |

# HOW TO READ THE DATA

You said the data part is the part you are not good at. Here is exactly what to do, and none of it needs statistics.

## Step 1 · The confidence gains (Q1 to Q4)

For each student, subtract. Then average the differences.

- **Software gain** = Q2 minus Q1
- **Knowing-where-to-go gain** = Q4 minus Q3

Report it as: *"On average, students rated their software confidence 1.8 points higher at the end than they recalled feeling at the start, on a 5-point scale."*

**Do not** average Q1 separately and Q2 separately and compare those. Subtract within each student first, then average. That is the whole method.

**A gain under 0.5 is noise. Over 1.0 is real. Over 1.5 is a strong result.**

## Step 2 · The supports table (Q5)

For each support, count two numbers:

- **Reach:** what percent did NOT choose "Did not use it"? *(Did they even encounter it?)*
- **Impact:** of the people who used it, what percent said "Helpful" or "Very helpful"?

**These are different problems with different fixes.**

- **Low reach, high impact** → students who found it loved it, but most never found it. **This is a visibility problem. Move it, link it, require it.** Discord is the likeliest candidate here.
- **High reach, low impact** → everyone hit it, nobody valued it. **This is a quality problem, or it is filler. Cut it or rebuild it.**
- **High reach, high impact** → keep, and do more of it.
- **Low reach, low impact** → cut it. It is costing you time and giving nothing back.

Put this in a simple 2x2. It will tell you what to build next in about ten seconds.

## Step 3 · Persistence (Q11 and Q12)

Report the raw number: *"Of N respondents, X seriously considered dropping. Their reasons were…"*

Then read Q12 answers one by one and group them by theme. Do not count. **Read them.** Five students telling you the same thing in their own words is stronger evidence than a Likert average, and it will tell you what to fix.

## Step 4 · Difficulty versus workload (Q13, Q14, Q15)

Cross-tabulate difficulty against workload. You are looking for one specific pattern:

**"About right" difficulty + "too heavy" workload** = the course is correctly pitched but oversized. That is a seat-time finding, it is fixable, and it connects directly to the accreditor's concern about credit hours in online courses.

If overwhelm (Q15) is `Often` or `Almost always` for more than about a third of respondents, the course is not too hard, it is too dense. Different fix.

## Step 5 · The open text (Q19 to Q21)

Read all of them before you count anything. Pull out the exact sentences that repeat. **Quote them verbatim in your writeup.** One student sentence is worth more than a bar chart to any reader, and it is the only thing that will land in an interview.

# CAVEATS TO STATE OUT LOUD

Any honest writeup of this data has to say these things. Saying them makes the rest of it more credible, not less.

1. **Survivor bias.** Only students who completed the course answered. The people this redesign most needed to help may not be represented at all.
2. **Retrospective self-report.** Students are recalling how they felt in week one, not reporting it in week one. That is a known, accepted method, and it is still self-report.
3. **Small n.** A summer session of AVC 100 is not a study. It is a signal.
4. **Summer sessions have low persistence regardless of design.** Do not attribute the whole thing to the course.
5. **No control group.** I cannot claim the redesign caused anything. I can claim students who took the redesigned course reported X.

**Nothing here proves the redesign worked. It tells me whether it is worth testing properly in the fall, at full length, with a comparison.** That is what this is for.
