Multimedia Storytelling

Case study · Learning Design · AI Tools & Strategy

Course designStory forms and tools

A redesign of Multimedia Storytelling for visual majors. Students gather a story that matters to them, build an AI they can question about it, then tell the story themselves in the medium they choose.

Goal

Build the writing and communication skills visual students are often missing, through the thing they already want to make: a story. The competencies are fixed and every student moves through the same method. The medium is theirs to choose.

Audience

Digital Media Arts, Animation, Photography, and Gaming students in a fully online, asynchronous course. Most think in images first and write second. The course has no prerequisites, so it is designed for a student who has never edited video, recorded an interview, or written a script.

Process

The anchor is Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir Artificial: A Love Story, in which her father builds a chatbot of her grandfather from his archive of letters and she tells the family story by talking with it and drawing it. Students do the same at a smaller scale. The story is personal to them but not necessarily about them: a grandparent’s move to Arizona, a coach’s first season, a shop on a corner that is gone. Consent from the person whose story it is comes first.

I designed the course backward from the eleven district competencies (Wiggins and McTighe) and kept every activity in constructive alignment with them (Biggs). Then I ran it past synthetic review panels: an instructional designer, four writing faculty, four design and media faculty, and a synthetic student. When the writing panel made it read like an English class, the design panel rebuilt it as a visual studio that keeps the writing designers do on the job: loglines, a pitch, a treatment, a two-column script, alt text, and case-study copy. I made every decision.

The method every student follows

  1. Choose a story and a narrator, and get consent.
  2. Gather an interview and an archive of photos, documents, sound, and sketches.
  3. Build the Teller, an AI grounded only in that archive, and test what it gets right and wrong.
  4. Shape the story: logline, pitch, visual treatment, and script.
  5. Storyboard it and cut an animatic.
  6. Produce a 2 to 4 minute story in the student’s chosen form.
  7. Archive and present it as a portfolio case study, with peer critique at every stage.

The eleven district competencies are the course outcomes, each an observable action at a stated level (I to III).

How success is measured

Ten scaffolded milestones build one story across the semester. A midterm project pairs the Teller with a short motion micro-story, and the final, The Living Archive, brings together the finished story, its key art, the preserved archive, a screen-recorded demonstration of the Teller, and a designed case-study page. Every form is graded on the same rubrics, and points sit on what the student authors: choices, curation, writing, design, and edit decisions. How clever the Teller sounds, or how polished a generated image looks, is never graded.

Pacing and interaction

Two noon deadlines a week, with work posted to the class Discord the day before for critique and three critique comments due with every submission. Workload was cut until every estimate, written for a student new to the tool, fit the credit hours. Five live studio sessions are recorded for anyone who cannot attend.

Technology

How I use AI

I built the course with my own course-builder skill and the synthetic panels above, which reviewed each draft from a different seat and caught what one designer working alone would miss. The same pipeline produced the complete Canvas import package.

How students use AI

  • The Teller. Each student builds one in Gemini Notebook, grounded only in their archive, then tests it, writes its guardrails, and critiques where it overreaches.
  • A working tool at every stage. Gemini for story starters, quick visual studies, interview rehearsal, and fact-checking leads; Adobe’s AI for transcription, text-based editing, audio cleanup, and captions.
  • A critique lens. One question runs through the course from Kurzweil: when a machine speaks about a person, or draws one, what does it get right, what does it miss, and who decides?
  • A short AI-use log on every milestone: the tool, what it did, and what the student kept, changed, or rejected.
  • A hard line. No synthesized voice or likeness of a real person. A narrator can be drawn or animated by hand, never generated.

Gemini and Adobe Creative Cloud are the defaults because the district already pays for both, but students are not limited to them. The full range of forms and tools is on the story forms and tools tab.

Status

Designed and fully built as a Canvas course: fifteen weekly modules, ten milestones, eight rubrics, a consent and release workflow, and a dated schedule. It has not been taught yet.
