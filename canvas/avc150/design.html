Multimedia Storytelling

Courses and Programs · Case study

Course designThe Teller and toolsThe full course

A redesign of Multimedia Storytelling for visual majors. Students build an AI storyteller from the archive of a story that matters to them, then interview it to draw the story out.

Goal

Develop the writing, interviewing and editorial judgment that visual students often lack by having them author an AI storyteller, the Teller, and then question it. Each student grounds a Teller in Gemini Notebook in one narrator’s consented archive, specifies its voice in writing, audits its errors, designs its visual identity, and records an edited interview with it. The recorded interview is both the finished story and its archive.

The AI is the object of authorship and critique, not a shortcut to the product. Assessment is authentic and process-based: points go to what the student writes, tests and corrects, never to how fluent the Teller sounds.

Audience

Digital Media Arts, Animation, Photography and Gaming students, most of whom think in images first and write second. The course is a general education course with no prerequisites, so it assumes no prior experience building an AI tool, conducting an interview or editing video, and it provides equivalent paths for dual-enrollment students under 18.

Process

The anchor text is Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir Artificial: A Love Story, in which her father builds a chatbot of her grandfather from his archive and she works through the family story by interviewing it. Students reproduce that method at a smaller scale with a story that is personal to them but not necessarily about them: a grandparent’s move to Arizona, a coach’s first season, a shop that is gone. Consent from the person whose story it is precedes every step.

The course is backward-designed from the eleven district competencies (Wiggins and McTighe) with constructive alignment of every activity and assessment (Biggs). Writing is situated in professional genres: a pitch, an interview guide, machine instructions, a voice guide, an error audit, a curator’s statement and case-study copy. Scaffolding runs from a complete model (the instructor’s demo Teller) to a guided rehearsal (the Mini Teller) to independent production.

Pacing and interaction

Fifteen weekly modules of about eight hours, each in the same order (Objectives, Overview, Watch, Read, Practice, Assessment, Critique, Due). Two noon deadlines a week, with project steps posted to Discord for peer critique the day before. Formative feedback returns before the next step depends on it, and any process step may be resubmitted once. Five optional live studio sessions are recorded.

AI in This Course

Every AI use is assigned, located in a specific assignment, and bounded. Students use Gemini only with their Maricopa (Google Workspace for Education) account, under which their data is not reviewed by people, not used to train models and not used for ads. Gemini writes and gives feedback; it never designs.

How AI Supports Learning Here

  • AI as interviewee. Students learn questioning and story structure by drawing a story out of a Teller; the questions are assessed, not the answers. The first exercise distinguishes a story from a topic using the demo Teller’s replies.
  • AI as audience for precise writing. Instructions and the voice guide are written for a machine reader, so imprecision surfaces as observable error. Students compare each answer against the guide and revise (instructions v1 to v3).
  • AI as object of critique. The error audit applies held-out questions on gaps, source conflicts and motives; students classify each error, trace it to a source or instruction, correct it and retest. Kurzweil’s question frames the analysis: when a machine speaks for a person, what does it get right, what does it miss, and who decides?
  • Grounded generation made visible. Gemini Notebook cites its sources, so students learn to separate grounded claims from invention where the archive is thin.
  • Verify and revise. The Module 1 AI Primer has students prompt with role, task, context and format, revise twice, and fact-check three claims against sources in a narrated recording. It also addresses the costs: training on artists’ work, bias, and energy use (data centers drew about 1.5% of global electricity in 2024, per the International Energy Agency).
  • Metacognitive disclosure. Every process step carries an AI-use log row (tool; purpose; what was kept, changed or rejected), and “No AI used” is a complete entry. Both projects add an AI-use statement and a credit line.

Guardrails

  • Account and data. Maricopa account only, under the district’s Gemini for Education terms. The instructor never places student work in a public AI tool.
  • Consent before upload. No narrator material enters any AI tool until the Signed Agreement authorizes AI processing; the consent video never does. Sensitive topics (immigration or legal status, legal cases, health) are removed first. A narrator who declines AI still participates through an equivalent path graded the same.
  • Rules for every Teller. It discloses that it is an AI, answers only from the archive, speaks in the first person only with written consent, and is never public. Its answers are never altered in the edit, and every internal cut is marked.
  • Never AI-made. Every visual (portrait, archive cards, journal pages, conversation map, key art) comes from archive material and the student’s own drawing and photography. No voice or likeness of a real person is cloned.
  • Students under 18. Where Gemini features are restricted, equivalent paths carry the same grade: a typed or in-person rehearsal, GCC Library databases instead of Deep Research, the student’s own reading instead of a synthetic voice, and an instructor-run Teller built from the student’s sources. A parent or guardian co-signs consent.

What Students Build

  • The Mini Teller. A Gemini Notebook from six Practice Archive items, 120 to 200 words of instructions, a six-question test rated accurate, partly accurate or invented, and two documented fixes.
  • The Teller. A Gemini Notebook grounded in at least eight consented sources, specified by a Teller Design Form (sources, mode, three limits), instructions revised through three versions, a 300 to 450 word voice guide, and an error audit log. It operates in narrator or first-person mode as the agreement permits.
  • Its preservation record. The archive package exports the source list, final instructions, voice guide and audit log alongside the recording, so the story outlasts the tool.

How to Adapt This

  1. Open with a finished model. Build a grounded notebook from a public-domain primary source and have students interview it in the first week, before they build their own.
  2. Assess the specification, not the output. Grade the instructions, a structured test log (answerable, unanswerable and scene questions, each rated) and the documented fixes.
  3. Gate uploads behind consent and log every use. A consent item that names AI processing, plus one AI-use log row per assignment, transfers to any course that handles other people’s material.

The tools for each stage, the consent options and the rules for every Teller are on the Teller and tools tab.

  • Hardware: a Windows or macOS computer that runs Premiere Pro; laptops and hotspots are available through GCC’s Office of Information Technology, and campus labs are open to every student.

How the Course Was Tested

Before launch, the course went through three reviews: synthetic student walk-throughs, a quality-rubric review, and an alignment and AI-exposure audit. Synthetic review panels (an instructional designer, writing faculty, and design and media faculty) also reviewed each draft. No real students were involved in testing.

Synthetic Student Walk-Throughs

The testers are synthetic students: AI agents, each built from one persona in the Student Journey Barriers Study. Each agent worked through the course module by module as that student and logged every point where it got stuck or could not finish. A fixer pass applied the findings, and a second round re-ran all eight agents against the revised course.

Quality and Alignment Reviews

The course was reviewed against the SUNY OSCQR online course quality rubric, and every objective was audited against the revised Bloom’s taxonomy (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001) so that each sits at or below its parent competency and is measured by a graded item.

Every Assessment Checked for AI Resistance

Every graded assessment was audited for AI exposure with Oregon State University Ecampus’s Bloom’s Taxonomy Revisited, asking whether a student could hand the task to an AI and pass. The design answers are process evidence and versioned drafts, work tied to the student’s own narrator and archive, recorded explanation, peer critique on Discord, the AI-use log, and the rule that Gemini never designs.

Accessibility

Built with Universal Design for Learning to WCAG 2.1 AA: captioned instructor videos with corrected transcripts, a Canvas alternative to Discord, an approved alternate recorder, and a path for every student, including screen-reader users. Student work meets the same standard: captions generated automatically and then corrected by hand, corrected transcripts, alt text, tagged documents, and case-study pages checked with WAVE, worth about 10 percent of each media rubric.

Status

Designed and fully built: fifteen weekly modules from Getting Started to the showcase, twenty-three process steps, seven graded discussions, two projects, a three-part consent package and a Canvas import package. Offered every fall.
