Multimedia Storytelling

Courses and Programs · Case study

Course designThe Teller and toolsThe full course

A redesign of Multimedia Storytelling for visual majors. Students build an AI storyteller from the archive of a story that matters to them, then interview it to draw the story out.

Goal

Build the writing and communication skills visual students are often missing, through something they want to make: a storyteller. Students write its voice, test what it gets wrong, design how it looks, and produce the recorded interview that tells the story.

Audience

Digital Media Arts, Animation, Photography, and Gaming students. Most think in images first and write second. The course has no prerequisites, so it is designed for a student who has never built an AI tool, recorded an interview, or edited video.

Process

The anchor is Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir Artificial: A Love Story, in which her father builds a chatbot of her grandfather from his archive and she works through the family story by interviewing it. Students do the same at a smaller scale. The story is personal to them but not necessarily about them: a grandparent’s move to Arizona, a coach’s first season, a shop on a corner that is gone. Consent from the person whose story it is comes first.

I designed the course backward from the eleven district competencies (Wiggins and McTighe) and aligned every activity to them (Biggs). Synthetic review panels (an instructional designer, writing faculty, design and media faculty, and a synthetic student) reviewed each draft. The writing lives where designers use it on the job: a pitch, an interview guide, the Teller’s instructions and voice guide, an error audit, a curator’s statement and case-study copy. I made every decision.

The method every student follows

In Week 1, students interview a demo storyteller built from a public-domain Federal Writers’ Project life history. In Week 2 they build a small one from a fictional archive. Then they build their own.

  1. Choose a story and a narrator, and get consent in three parts that name AI processing, the storyteller’s mode and any synthetic voice.
  2. Gather an interview and an archive of photos, documents, sound, and sketches.
  3. Build the Teller in Gemini Notebook, grounded only in that archive.
  4. Give it a voice: a written voice guide and instructions, in the first person or as a narrator.
  5. Test it with an error audit, and revise what it gets wrong.
  6. Map and design the conversation and the Teller’s visual identity.
  7. Record and edit a 3 to 5 minute interview with it, the finished and archived story.
  8. Present the Teller live, the recording and a curator’s statement, with peer critique at every stage.

How success is measured

Twenty-three process steps build one Teller. Project 1, the Mini Teller, closes Week 2. The final, The Living Archive, brings together the Teller, the recorded interview, the error audit and curator’s statement, key art, the archive package and a case-study page. Points go to what the student authors, tests and fixes, never to how fluent the Teller sounds.

Pacing and interaction

Two noon deadlines a week (one in Week 1), with Discord critique before each project step, and about eight hours of work a week. Five live studio sessions are recorded for anyone who cannot attend.

Access

Built with Universal Design for Learning to WCAG 2.1 AA, with a path for every student, including screen-reader users. Student work is accessible too: hand-corrected captions, corrected transcripts, alt text and tagged documents, about 10 percent of each media rubric.

Technology

How I use AI

I built the course with my own course-builder skill and the synthetic panels above, which reviewed each draft from a different seat and caught what one designer working alone would miss.

How students use AI

  • The Teller. Each student builds one in Gemini Notebook, grounded only in their archive, writes its voice, audits its errors and interviews it.
  • A working tool at every stage. Gemini for story starters, interview rehearsal and feedback; Adobe’s AI for transcription, text-based editing, audio cleanup, and captions.
  • A critique lens. One question runs through the course from Kurzweil: when a machine speaks about a person, or as one, what does it get right, what does it miss, and who decides?
  • A short AI-use log on every process step: the tool, what it did, and what the student kept, changed, or rejected.
  • Clear limits. Every Teller says it is an AI. Gemini never designs or generates images or video. A synthetic voice, labeled as AI, may read the Teller’s answers; no one clones a real person’s voice or likeness.

The tools for each stage are on the Teller and tools tab.

Status

Built as a complete course: fifteen weekly modules, one per week, from Getting Started to the showcase, with twenty-three process steps, two projects and a three-part consent package. Offered every fall, first offering Fall 2027.
