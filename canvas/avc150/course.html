AI Primer (10 points, Process Work)

What this is. Your first time using Gemini in this course, and it takes about an hour. No experience with AI is needed. Complete Technology Setup first.

What Gemini is. Gemini is an AI chat tool from Google. You type a request, called a prompt, and it writes an answer. It learned from an enormous amount of text written by people, so it can sound knowledgeable about almost anything. It does not know you, and it has never been where you have been.

Why this assignment. Some assignments in this course use Gemini for research and feedback. Before you use it for that, you will see for yourself what it does well, where it falls short, and what you bring that it cannot.

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account, so what you enter is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models. See AI in This Course for Google’s exact wording.

This assignment measures Module Objective 0.2, which supports Course Competency 8.

Part 1: Sign in

  1. Open Chrome and go to gemini.google.com.
  2. If you see Sign in in the top right corner, click it. Sign in with your Maricopa account: your MEID followed by @maricopa.edu, then your MEID password on the Maricopa sign-in page.
  3. Check the top right corner. It should show your Maricopa account. If it shows a personal Gmail account, click the picture and switch to your Maricopa account.

If Gemini does not open or says it is not available for your account (this can happen for students under 18), message your instructor in Canvas Inbox. You will receive an equal option with the same grade.

Part 2: Ask the question

  1. Click the box at the bottom that says Ask Gemini.
  2. Type Do I need to learn AI to get a good job in the digital media industry? and press Enter.
  3. Copy your prompt and Gemini’s answer into a Google Doc.

Part 3: Read it like a critic

  1. In your Doc, mark:
    • one point you agree with;
    • one point that sounds generic, overstated or like a sales pitch;
    • one question you would rather ask a working professional in the field.
  2. Gemini is made by Google, a company that sells AI. Write one sentence on how that might shape its answer.

Part 4: Give it better directions

A stronger prompt tells Gemini four things: a role (who it should act as), a task (what to do), context (who it is for and why) and a format (how the answer should look). Example: You are a hiring manager at a small design studio in Phoenix. List the skills you look for in a new junior designer, and say honestly where AI skills rank among them. I am a first-year community college student. Use a short ranked list.

  1. Rewrite your prompt using all four parts, and add one detail about yourself: the kind of work you want to do. Press Enter.
  2. Copy the new prompt and answer into your Doc. Write one sentence on how the answer changed.

Part 5: Reflect

  1. In four to six sentences: Did either answer change how you think about AI and your career? Where might a tool like this help you in your own work, and where would you not want to use it?

Part 6: Start your AI-use log

  1. At the bottom of your Doc, add a table with three columns: Tool, What I asked, and What I kept, changed or rejected. Add one row for this assignment. Every assignment in this course has a row like this, so your instructor can see how you used AI and what was your own.

Submit the link to your Google Doc.

Grading (10 points): critic’s mark-up 3; revised prompt with your own detail 3; reflection 2; AI-use log row 2.

Module 1

The Practice Teller

Week 2 · 10 hours

Objectives

  • 1.1 Organize and describe archive items with accurate metadata, specific alt text and a corrected transcript excerpt (Course Competency 10)
  • 1.2 Build a Teller in Gemini Notebook from an archive, write its instructions, and test and correct its answers (Course Competency 6)
  • 1.3 Record and caption a short interview with a Teller that draws out one moment of the story (Course Competency 8)
  • 1.4 Explain the difference between a story and a topic, using two of the demo Teller’s answers as examples (Course Competency 1)

Overview

This week you make the whole project once, in a small version. You use the Practice Archive: interviews, letters, documents and old photos about a fictitious narrator, Ada Ruiz. Her family settled at a farm workers’ camp near Glendale, and her mother sewed her a dress from flour sacks for a camp dance. You build a Practice Teller from it in Gemini Notebook, test it, and record a short interview with it. The archive has mistakes planted on purpose. Finding them is part of the work.

Start by interviewing the instructor’s demo Teller, built from a real 1930s life history that is now free to use, so you see what you will build.

The course idea comes from cartoonist Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir Artificial: A Love Story, in which her father builds a chatbot of her grandfather from his letters and writing, and she gets to know her grandfather by talking with it. She is a cartoonist, not an AI expert, so her way in is the same as yours: a story she cared about and a lot of questions.

One captioned instructor video: what a story is, a live interview with the demo Teller, then the archive and the Teller, with a guided lab on building and interviewing the Practice Teller.

Projects Like This

Each of these projects follows the same steps as this course: collect an archive, build an AI that answers from it, then interview it. They are optional; choose any you like. Some of these stories deal with loss. In this course, every Teller is built with consent from the person whose story it is (or their family), or from public sources.

  • USC Shoah Foundation, Dimensions in Testimony: Holocaust survivors answered hundreds of questions on camera. You ask a question and hear the recorded answer that fits. It is the closest real-world version of a Teller.
  • NPR, “Creating a ‘Dadbot’ to Talk with a Dead Father” (radio interview; free transcript): James Vlahos recorded hours of interviews with his father, then built a chatbot that answers in his father’s words.
  • PA Media in The Irish News, “US Artist Laurie Anderson Models AI Chatbot on Late Husband”: the artist Laurie Anderson writes back and forth with an AI that writes in the style of her late husband, the musician Lou Reed.

How to Build a Teller in Gemini Notebook

You build every Teller in this course the same way: the Practice Teller this week, and your real Teller in Module 5. Gemini Notebook (it was called NotebookLM) answers only from the sources you give it and shows where each answer came from. The instructor video in this module shows each screen. Google sometimes renames a button; if a label is different, look for the same idea.

  1. Sign in. Go to notebook.google.com and sign in with your Maricopa Google account (your MEID@maricopa.edu). Never use a personal account: the Maricopa account keeps your narrator’s material private and out of AI training.
  2. Make a notebook. Select Create new notebook. Name it so you can find it: “Teller: Practice Archive” this week, and “Teller: [your project title]” in Module 5.
  3. Add your sources. Select Add sources, then upload from Google Drive or your computer. Add the corrected transcript (a Google Doc), photographed letters and documents (PDF), photographs with their captions, and any short audio. With a real narrator, add only what the Signed Agreement clears for AI. This week, add the Practice Archive files from the course folder.
  4. Name every source. Rename each one so a citation makes sense, for example “Interview 1, corrected transcript” or “Letter to Wilma, 1938.”
  5. Check every source. Open each source in the Sources panel and make sure the words came through. If a handwritten page did not read well, type it up and add the typed copy as its own source.
  6. Write the instructions in a Google Doc first. Use the starter below. Keep the Doc: its version history is part of your grade.
  7. Give the Teller its instructions. In the Chat panel, open the chat settings (Configure chat), choose Custom, paste your instructions and select Save.
  8. Test it. Ask three questions: one the archive can answer, one it cannot, and one that asks for a scene (“Take me to the day…”). Select the small numbers in each answer to see the exact source passage it used.
  9. Fix and test again. If the Teller invents something, change one line of the instructions, save the Doc as a new version, paste the new instructions and ask the same question again.
  10. Share it for grading. Select Share and add your instructor’s Maricopa email. Keep access restricted: never “anyone with the link” for a real narrator.
  11. Log it. Add a row to your AI-use log: what you built, what you tested and what you changed.

Starter instructions (replace the brackets):

You are the Teller for [name or “the narrator”]’s story. Start your first answer with: “I am an AI built from an archive of [name]’s interviews and papers.” Answer only from the sources in this notebook, and cite them. Speak as [a narrator who knows this archive / [name], in the first person, as the Signed Agreement allows]. If the sources do not answer a question, say “The archive does not say,” and suggest a question it can answer. Never guess names, dates or feelings. Do not discuss [topics the narrator ruled out in the agreement]. Keep answers under 150 words unless asked for more.

Module 2

The Pitch and Consent

Week 3 · 8 hours

Objectives

  • 2.1 Present a story idea to peers for feedback, and respond to theirs in writing (Course Competency 5)
  • 2.2 Explain how voice, place and culture shape your story and your Teller in a pitch package (Course Competency 2)
  • 2.3 Explain in plain language how AI tools will process a narrator’s material and how the Teller will speak (Course Competency 3)

Overview

The real project begins. You choose whose story your Teller will hold, ask that person informally, and propose the project in a pitch package. You also choose the Teller’s mode. In narrator mode, the Teller knows the archive and speaks about the person. In first-person mode, it speaks as the person, like the grandfather bot in Artificial. First person needs the narrator’s written agreement. Both modes are graded the same, and every Teller says it is an AI.

You also choose one of three story paths. All three are graded the same.

PathWho the Teller speaks asConsent package
Real narrator (main path)The narrator, or a narrator voiceRequired
YourselfYou, in a self-interviewNot needed
Character or iconA fictional character, a brand figure (like Barbie), a place or object, or a historical figure in the public domain, built from public sourcesNot needed. You keep a source list (title, link, license or fair-use note) and a credit line instead.

How to choose: if a real person will share their own story with you, use the real narrator path. If the story is yours, use the yourself path. If you can know the figure only through public sources, use the character or icon path (the character path).

The character path has these rules:

  • Your instructor approves your figure and topic in the Pitch Package, before you build anything.
  • No living real person speaks in the first person. A living real person may only be described by a narrator voice. The Teller never quotes or imitates them.
  • The Teller still opens by saying it is an AI built from an archive of public sources.
  • A brand or trademarked character is unofficial student commentary. Use this credit line: “Unofficial student commentary; [character] is a trademark of [owner].” The finished piece stays Restricted on your portfolio site.
  • The story still needs your own angle. Write a short “Why this figure matters to me” statement, and build your interview questions from your own question about the figure.

Take Your Work With You (ungraded, Week 15)

Your Maricopa Google account may close after you leave the college. Do this before the course ends, so you keep everything you made even if you stop out, transfer or graduate. It takes about 45 minutes.

  1. Save your files in one place. Make one Google Drive folder for this course. Put in it your MP4 master and SRT caption file, Premiere Pro project, portrait, archive cards, key art, conversation map, error audit log and curator’s statement and your AI-use log. Keep your narrator’s archive only as your Signed Agreement allows. Videos you posted to YouTube with your Maricopa account are not copied, so download your own YouTube videos into the folder too.
  2. Copy everything to a personal Google account. Make a personal Google account if you do not have one. First make your own copy (File, then Make a copy) of any file someone shared with you, because files you do not own are not copied. Then, signed in to your Maricopa account, open Google Takeout Transfer and copy your Drive and Gmail to your personal account (see Google’s help page). It can take up to a week, and the copies arrive unshared. If Transfer is not available on your account, use Google Takeout to download your Drive as .zip files and save them on your computer.
  3. Take your Teller with you, only as your agreement allows. Your Teller lives in Gemini Notebook on your Maricopa account and does not move to another account. Keep its instructions, voice guide and source list in your course folder; that is what rebuilds it. On the character path, you may rebuild it from your source list. Otherwise, rebuild it in a personal account only if your narrator’s Signed Agreement allows it, load only the items the agreement allows, and never share it publicly.
    • Know the privacy difference. Your Maricopa account keeps your Gemini work out of AI training (see AI in This Course). A personal account does not have the same protections, and Google may use your activity to improve its AI, depending on your settings. In your personal account, open Gemini, then Settings & help, then Activity (Gemini Apps Activity), and decide whether to turn off Keep Activity. Your narrator’s private material never goes into a personal account without their written yes.
  4. Move your Google Site. Takeout Transfer copies Drive, Gmail and Photos. Do not count on it to move your case-study page. First keep the content: put the site’s text in a Google Doc and its images in your course folder, then open each published page and save it as a PDF (File, then Print, then Save as PDF). Then try a direct copy: in the site editor, choose Share and add your personal email as an editor. Sign in with your personal account, open the site, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing outside Maricopa is blocked, rebuild the site in your personal account at sites.google.com with the same content and publish it again. The web address changes, so update it on your resume and profiles.
  5. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
