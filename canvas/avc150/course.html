The complete course design, written to be adopted: the course map, seat time, assessment plan and rubrics, AI integration, consent and privacy, and every week of content.

Design concept

Students choose a story that is personal to them but not necessarily about them: a grandparent’s move to Arizona, a coach’s first season, a family recipe, a shop on a corner that is gone. With consent from the person whose story it is (the narrator), they record an interview and gather an archive of photographs, documents, sound, reference images and visual journal pages. They build an AI Teller in Gemini Notebook, grounded in that archive, so the archive can be questioned and searched. Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir Artificial: A Love Story (Catapult, 2023) is the anchor text: her father builds “Fredbot” from her grandfather’s letters and lecture notes, and she tells the family story by talking with it and drawing it. That is the model for this course: a visual artist who writes, and who uses an AI as one tool among many.

Students then do what no Teller can do for them. They pitch the story with a moodboard, design style frames, write a visual treatment and a two-column script, storyboard and cut an animatic, design key art and titles, and produce a 2 to 4 minute digital story in one of three visual forms. Audio supports the picture: clean narration, the narrator’s real voice, sound design and a music bed. The semester ends with The Living Archive, a portfolio piece made of the finished story, its key art, the preserved archive, a screen-recorded demonstration of the Teller and a designed case-study page.

AI is used at every stage as a creative collaborator, built on the two platforms the district pays for. Google Gemini supplies the Gemini app, image generation for moodboards and style exploration, Gemini Notebook for the Teller, Deep Research for background, and Gemini in Docs, Sheets, Slides and Gmail for the writing. Adobe Creative Cloud supplies production: Photoshop, Illustrator, InDesign, Premiere Pro, After Effects, Audition and Adobe Express, with AI-assisted transcription, editing, audio cleanup and captioning, and Firefly and Generative Fill where the account has generative credits. The course keeps one honest critique running through it, borrowed from Kurzweil: when a machine speaks about a person, or draws one, what does it get right, what does it miss, and who decides?

Design principles

  • Backward design: the 11 board-approved competencies (C1 to C11) are the course outcomes. Every objective, activity and assessment traces to them in the instructor notes; students see the outcomes in plain language.
  • One big project, scaffolded by 10 milestones; one mid-semester project (The Teller and the Micro-Story) whose style frames set the look of the final.
  • Visual first, audio supporting. Every week produces something a viewer can look at.
  • Writing as a professional skill: every writing task is the kind a designer, animator or editor writes at work, and every one feeds a visual artifact.
  • Authentic assessment at Apply level or higher. No exams.
  • Grade what the student authors: choices, curation, writing, design, editing, testing, judgment. Never grade how clever the Teller sounds or how polished a generated image is on its own.
  • OSCQR, Regular and Substantive Interaction (RSI), UDL and WCAG 2.1 AA designed in from the start.
  • Workload honesty: every task is estimated for a student new to the tool, and the course was cut until the estimates fit 120 hours.

1.2 Deadline rhythm and calendar

How the rhythm works. Each module opens on Monday at noon (Week 1 opens on the first Monday of the term). Every week from Week 2 on has two deadlines, never more:

Cycle Post for critique (critiqued items only) Deadline (Canvas “Due”) Hard close (Canvas “Available until”)
Cycle B (midweek) Wednesday at noon, in the assignment’s Discord channel Thursday at noon: the work and your comments on at least three classmates’ posts, submitted together in Canvas Friday at noon. Work submitted between the deadline and the hard close loses 10%; nothing is accepted after it
Cycle A (weekend) Saturday at noon, in the assignment’s Discord channel Sunday at noon: the work and your comments, submitted together in Canvas Monday at noon. Same rule

Week 1 has a single deadline: post by Wednesday at noon, due Thursday at noon, hard close Friday at noon. Nothing in this course is ever due at midnight. Items without critique have only the deadline and the 24-hour late window (10% deduction) to the hard close. Work posted for critique after the posting time may receive less peer feedback, because classmates comment on the work that is there when they sit down to critique. You still owe your own three comments by the deadline. Between deadlines students have three to four days to make the work (Sunday noon to Thursday noon is four days; Thursday noon to Sunday noon is three).

Returning on the late-window day (stated in the same words on the How Deadlines Work page and in the critique protocol): “Your required critique is three comments by the deadline. Some classmates will post during the late window, after most feedback has been given. If you can, return on the late-window day (Monday for the weekend cycle, Friday for the midweek cycle) and leave a comment on any work posted late. This is not graded and earns no points; it is what a good member of an online creative community does. At some point in the semester you may be the one waiting for feedback, and it is worth more to a late project than the points it lost.” This is encouraged and visible (it is pinned in every Discord channel) but it is not a deadline, carries no points and adds no third deadline to any week.

Canvas settings: Due = the deadline; Available until = the hard close; Late Policy on, deducting 10% per day late (the window is one day, so the deduction is 10% once); Missing Submission policy per the instructor’s standing practice.

2. Seat-time reconciliation

Rule applied: 3 credits x 40 = 120 student hours. Online delivery of a 3-credit, 6-load Lec+Lab course = 40 hours direct instruction + 80 hours outside work. Target 2.67 direct + 5.33 outside per week.

Direct instruction: recorded lectures and demonstrations, guided labs, Studio Sessions (live, recorded and required to watch), instructor-facilitated peer critique in Discord (reading classmates’ posts and writing critique comments), and reviewing individualized instructor video feedback. Outside work: reading and viewing, practice, checkpoints, milestone and project work, making discussion posts, surveys.

How the estimates were made: each task is estimated for a student using the tool for the first time, on the default Adobe and Gemini path (with the Chromebook backup checked for the heaviest tasks), following the minimum viable spec stated on the assignment. Reading is estimated at about 25 pages of prose per hour and about 60 pages of graphic memoir per hour. A critique round (viewing at least three classmates’ posts and writing three comments of 40 to 80 words) is estimated at 0.5 hours; the Week 15 showcase round, with full films and case-study pages, at 1.5. Logging AI use is budgeted inside each milestone (about 10 minutes). Visual tasks are estimated at the minimum viable spec, not at portfolio polish: a style frame at 15 to 25 minutes, a moodboard at about an hour including AI exploration, key art at about an hour from existing assets.

3.3 Project 1: The Teller and the Micro-Story (Week 8, 20%)

Competencies (instructor view): C2, C3, C4, C8, C10

Deadlines · Post the micro-story for critique: Saturday of Week 8 at noon (Discord) · Due: Sunday of Week 8 at noon (Canvas: the full submission plus your comments on three classmates’ micro-stories) · Hard close: Monday of Week 9 at noon (10% deduction between the deadline and the hard close; nothing accepted after). The Teller link, logs and design form go in Canvas only.

3.8 Critique protocol

A self-interview: a classmate or Gemini asks the questions from the student’s own guide; the student is the narrator, and the story is about a place, an object or the student’s own memory. Counts fully for M3; consent items then cover only the student. 4. A GCC-arranged volunteer narrator, if a pool exists (CONFIRM). 5. Last resort: a public oral history from an openly licensed or public-domain collection the student has a personal connection to, plus a self-interview about that connection (license confirmed by the instructor).

No student has to choose a story that exposes their family, and no one has to explain why they chose a different one.

There is no item for synthesizing the narrator’s voice or likeness; that is not permitted in this course. That includes AI images, animation or video generated to resemble the narrator or any real, identifiable person. Students may draw or animate a narrator by hand, or stylize their own photographs of a narrator who agreed to item 3, because those are authored, not generated.

Withdrawal: the narrator may withdraw at any time. The student then removes the material from the Drive folder, Gemini Notebook sources, any image tool library, any discussion post and any posted video, and tells the instructor. Anything already seen by classmates cannot be unseen; the information sheet says so.

Sensitive information: students do not ask about immigration or legal status, legal cases, health details or criminal history. If a narrator raises them, the student offers to stop recording, and cuts those passages from every transcript before any AI upload. These details never go into the Teller or any public version, even with consent. Students do not upload identification numbers, financial, medical or immigration documents to any tool.

Third parties: people mentioned but not interviewed are not named or shown recognizably in public versions without a reason.

Cultural protocols: some stories belong to a community. Students ask the narrator what may be shared and with whom, record the answer, and do not upload restricted or sacred material to any AI tool or use it as a style reference. In Arizona, tribal nations hold sovereignty over cultural knowledge and imagery; students talk with the instructor before using such material (CONFIRM whether GCC has guidance or a liaison).

Grief: content notes appear in Getting Started and Weeks 1, 2 and 7 (grief, death, migration, AI recreations of people who have died), with links to GCC Counseling and the Dean of Students [links to confirm]. A student choosing a death within about the past year checks in with the instructor first; this is a conversation, not a gate. For someone who has died, the student chooses the archivist voice or first person; first person requires written consent from the family member or estate holder (release item 7). A Teller about someone who has died is never public in either voice and is shown only by screen recording. In D6, Tellers about someone who has died are shown only to classmates who opt in.

Minors: - Minor as narrator: a guardian signs; the minor agrees on the recording; first name only or none in any shared version; no Teller outside the course; no recognizable images of the minor in public versions without guardian consent. - Minor as student: interviews only adults the student already knows, or by phone or video call with a guardian aware; never alone with an unfamiliar adult. Guardian consent for any public posting.

Recording law: Arizona is a one-party consent state (A.R.S. § 13-3005; Digital Media Law Project summary). This course requires everyone recorded to agree anyway. Some states require all-party consent for calls; students interviewing across state lines follow the stricter rule. Students filming b-roll in public places avoid recognizable bystanders or ask permission.
