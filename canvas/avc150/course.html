Multimedia Storytelling

THE COURSE, MODULE BY MODULE · AVC 150 · 3 CREDITS

When it runs. Offered every fall, fully online. The first offering is Fall 2027.

What students make. A Living Archive: a 2 to 4 minute digital story about someone else’s memory, told in the medium the student chooses (animation or motion comic, illustrated or photo documentary, or live-action or archival video), with its key art, the preserved archive, an AI “Teller” that answers questions from that archive, and a designed case-study page.

Two passes. In Weeks 1 to 3 everyone makes a complete 30 to 45 second version from a shared, fictional Practice Archive, so they see the finish line before they ask anyone for anything. From Week 4 they make the real story with a real narrator who has consented to every step.

Seat time: Module 0 (about 3 hours) and Module 2 (about 3 hours) share Week 1. Module 1, the AI Primer, runs across Weeks 1 and 2 (about 6 hours). Module 3 runs about 5 hours in Week 2 and Module 4 about 7 hours in Week 3. Modules 5 to 16 run about 8 hours each (2.5 to 3 hours of instruction and critique, 5 to 5.5 hours of outside work). 120 hours in all.

Deadline rhythm: everything is due at noon, Arizona time, never midnight. Late work is accepted for 24 hours at 10% off; nothing is accepted after noon the day after. Projects and project steps are posted to the class Discord by noon the day before they are due, with three critique comments by the due time; exercises, quizzes, readings and reflections have no Discord step. Two deadlines a week. Midweek: post Wednesday, due Thursday, closes Friday. Weekend: post Saturday, due Sunday, closes Monday. If a hard close would fall on a college holiday, that cycle moves one day earlier.

The rules. Gemini writes and gives feedback; it never designs or generates images or audio. Students make every visual (frames, moodboards, journal pages, storyboards, key art) from archive material and their own drawing, photography and footage. AI may brainstorm, draft and tighten writing, draft metadata and alt text, rehearse interviews, transcribe, clean audio, repair a photograph and power the Teller. AI may not generate images, video or voices; imitate any real person’s voice or likeness, even with permission; or change a word presented as a narrator’s quotation. Every process step carries a three-field AI-use log row (tool; what I used it for; what I kept, changed or rejected; “No AI used” is a complete entry). Project 1 and the Final carry a full AI-use statement and a credit line. Grades go to what the student authored, never to how fluent the Teller sounds.

Consent and privacy. Students rehearse every document on the fictional narrator first. With a real narrator, consent comes three ways before anything is uploaded: a signed Consent Affirmation before the interview (Week 5), a video-recorded consent the narrator reads on camera at the start of the interview (Week 6), and a Signed Agreement after the interview (Week 7) whose yes-or-no items name AI processing by large language models in Google Gemini and Adobe tools. Nothing of the narrator’s goes into any AI tool until that agreement is signed with the AI item marked yes, and the consent video never goes into any AI tool. A narrator who says no to AI still takes part; the student works by hand. A narrator may withdraw at any time: the student deletes the material from every tool within seven days, confirms it in writing, and finishes the term with the Practice Archive or a self-interview at no grade penalty. Students use Gemini only with their Maricopa accounts: under the district’s Google for Education agreement, what they enter is not reviewed by people, not used to train AI models, and not used for ads (see Google for Education, Gemini for Education and the Gemini education FAQ). Private material lives in Canvas only, never Discord, and the Teller is never public; it is shown only by screen recording.

AI in This Course (page text; the same text is the syllabus AI section)

This course uses generative AI as part of the assignments. Our industry advisory board encourages us to teach current industry practice, and employers now expect graduates to work with AI. Using AI here is not cheating. Cheating is turning in work you did not do as if you did. In this course, AI use is assigned, it is disclosed in an AI-use log with every assignment, and you are graded on your own thinking: your research, your story decisions, your writing and your production work. Other courses may set different rules, and those rules apply in those courses.

AI does not make your work. Gemini helps with research, writing, feedback and planning, and Adobe tools help transcribe, clean audio and caption. Gemini never designs and never generates images or audio. Your frames, storyboards, key art, narration and edit decisions are your own, and you will show your process.

You will learn to question it. AI makes confident mistakes and reflects bias in its training data. A machine transcript can mishear a name, and a Teller can invent an answer where an archive has a gap. This course teaches you to check every answer against the source and to think critically about what AI gets wrong, whose story it tells, and what it costs.

Accessibility of Your Work (Start Here page text; it applies to every submission and every Discord post)

Everything you make in this course has to work for someone who cannot see it or cannot hear it.

  • Video: accurate captions. Start with automatic captions (YouTube Studio, Premiere Pro Speech to Text, Google Vids or Meet), then read every line against the audio and fix names, terms and timing by hand. Automatic captions alone are not enough.
  • Audio: a transcript (auto-transcribe in Premiere Pro or Gemini Notebook, then correct it by hand) and, for sound pieces, a short text description of the important non-speech sounds.
  • Images: alt text on every meaningful image, one or two sentences on what matters (never “image of”); mark decorative images as decorative.
  • Documents and slides: real headings, readable contrast and alt text (in Google Docs and Slides, right-click an image, then Alt text); export PDFs with tags.
  • Web pages: in Google Sites or Adobe Portfolio, headings in order, link text that says where it goes, alt text, captions on embedded video, and a check with WAVE or a screen reader.
  • Discord: the same rule. Add a description (alt text) to every image you upload, and post captions or a transcript with every audio or video post.

Every rubric for media work includes captions or a transcript and alt text, about 10 percent of the points.

Overview

Why we use AI. You are here because you want to tell stories with pictures, sound and words, in film, animation, journalism, podcasts, museums or your own studio. The people who will hire you have already decided AI skills matter: in Microsoft and LinkedIn’s survey, 66% of business leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills, and 71% said they would rather hire a less experienced candidate who has them. Three out of four knowledge workers already use AI on the job. Our industry advisors asked us to teach it. Leaving you without these skills would put you behind the people you will compete with.

Many of you have serious doubts about AI, and some of those doubts are right. AI is trained on artists’ and writers’ work, often without permission. It makes confident mistakes: a machine transcript can put words in a narrator’s mouth, and a chatbot can invent a family history that never happened. It reflects the biases in its training data, including whose stories and voices it has heard most. Data centers already use about 1.5% of the world’s electricity, and the International Energy Agency expects that to more than double by the end of the decade. This course does not hide those problems.

Here is the case for learning it anyway. Refusing AI does not slow it down; it only removes your voice from how it gets used. The storytellers who decide how AI is used in their newsrooms, studios and archives are the ones who understand it well enough to be trusted. Employers also say creativity, communication and critical thinking matter more than ever, because AI cannot do those for you. This course keeps the storytelling in your hands, teaches you to use AI for what it does well, and to catch it when it is wrong. You can leave as an informed critic; you cannot be one without knowing how it works.

Is this cheating? No. AI use in this course is assigned, it is disclosed in your AI-use log, and you are graded on your own thinking: your research, your story decisions, your writing and your production work.

How your work is protected. Use Gemini only with your Maricopa account. Under the district’s Google for Education agreement, what you enter is not reviewed by people, not used to train AI models, and not used for ads; read the exact wording at Google for Education, Gemini for Education and the Gemini for Google Workspace education FAQ. The instructor never puts your work into a public AI tool. A narrator’s material goes into an AI tool only after their Signed Agreement is signed with AI processing marked yes.

Overview

This week you do the first two parts of the project on the Practice Archive: collect and build a Teller. An archive is the preserved set of files, descriptions and consent records behind a story. Metadata is information about an item: what it is, who made it, when, where it came from, and who may use it. A machine transcript is always a first draft, corrected against the audio before you trust it. Your Teller is a Gemini Notebook that answers only from the sources you load, with citations, and follows instructions you write. A grounded tool can still invent an answer where the archive has a gap; part of your job is to catch that and fix it. The Practice Archive has a few problems built in on purpose.
