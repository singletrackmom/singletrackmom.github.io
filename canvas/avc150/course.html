AVC 150

Students build an AI storyteller, the Teller, from one person’s consented archive, then interview it to tell that person’s story.

Accessibility of Student Work

Everything students make must work for someone who cannot see it or cannot hear it. Media rubrics give about 10% of the points to this.

  • Video: captions generated automatically (YouTube Studio, Premiere Pro Speech to Text, Google Vids or Meet), then every line corrected by hand against the audio.
  • Audio: a transcript, auto-transcribed and then corrected by hand.
  • Images: alt text on every meaningful image, one or two sentences on what matters; decorative images marked decorative.
  • Documents and pages: real headings, readable contrast, alt text, descriptive link text, tagged PDFs; web pages checked with WAVE.
  • Discord: alt text on every image; captions or a transcript with every audio or video post.

Module 1Getting Started: AI, Story and the Demo TellerWeek 1 · about 8 hours

Objectives

  • 1.1 Set up and verify every core course account, and record and share a short narrated screen recording (Course Competency 8)
  • 1.2 Apply the role, task, context and format formula to revise a prompt twice, check three claims in the result against a cited source, and explain why a large language model can be wrong (Course Competency 8)
  • 1.3 Describe how AI tools can change the language, image and power of a personal story, citing two course sources (Course Competency 3)
  • 1.4 Explain the difference between a story and a topic, using two of the demo Teller’s answers as examples (Course Competency 1)

Overview

In this course you build a storyteller, called a Teller: an AI in Gemini Notebook that knows one person’s story because you built its archive. You then interview it, and the recorded, edited interview is your finished story. The model is Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir Artificial: A Love Story (Catapult, 2023), in which her father builds a chatbot of her grandfather from his archive of letters and writing, and she works through the family story by interviewing it.

This week you set up your accounts and recorder, learn how a large language model differs from a grounded tool (a model predicts likely words and does not know facts; Gemini Notebook answers from the sources you give it and cites them, but can still invent where the archive has a gap), and interview the instructor’s demo Teller, built from one life history recorded by the Federal Writers’ Project in the 1930s and now in the public domain. You see on the first day what you will build.

How to Build a Teller in Gemini Notebook

You build every Teller in this course the same way: the Mini Teller this week, and your real Teller in Module 6. Gemini Notebook (it was called NotebookLM) answers only from the sources you give it and shows where each answer came from. The instructor video in this module shows each screen. Google sometimes renames a button; if a label is different, look for the same idea.

  1. Sign in. Go to notebook.google.com and sign in with your Maricopa Google account (your MEID@maricopa.edu). Never use a personal account: the Maricopa account keeps your narrator’s material private and out of AI training.
  2. Make a notebook. Select Create new notebook. Name it so you can find it: “Teller: Practice Archive” this week, and “Teller: [your project title]” in Module 6.
  3. Add your sources. Select Add sources, then upload from Google Drive or your computer. Add the corrected transcript (a Google Doc), photographed letters and documents (PDF), photographs with their captions, and any short audio. With a real narrator, add only what the Signed Agreement clears for AI. This week, add the Practice Archive files from the course folder.
  4. Name every source. Rename each one so a citation makes sense, for example “Interview 1, corrected transcript” or “Letter to Wilma, 1938.”

Overview

The Living Archive is your final project and your portfolio piece. A curator’s statement is the text beside an exhibited work that tells a visitor what it is and how to read it. Yours explains what your Teller gets right, where it fails, and what a visitor should trust.
