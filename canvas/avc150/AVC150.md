## The idea

Rebuild AVC 150 so the story is told through an AI the students make. Students collect the raw material of a story (interviews, photos, audio, writing, objects), then build a bot that tells it, or a character inside it that people can talk to. Could be a small language model, a chatbot, a Gemini Gem, or something else. Format still open.

**Whose story:** a story that is personal to the student but not necessarily about them: a family member’s, a community member’s, or a place or object they have a connection to. Same as Kurzweil telling her grandfather’s story. Consent from whoever’s story it is becomes part of the assignment.

Where the idea came from: Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir *Artificial: A Love Story* (Catapult, 2023). Her father builds a chatbot of her grandfather Fred from his archive of letters, music and writing, and she works through the family story by talking with it and drawing it. It’s the whole course in one book: collect the archive, build the teller, then make a multimedia story about what the bot gets right and wrong. https://amykurzweil.com/

### Official competencies (MCCCD)

1. Present an overview of storytelling, including elements of the story, the power of the story and a variety of ways to present a story. (I)
2. Explain the impact of voice, place and culture in storytelling. (I)
3. Describe the importance of effectively blending language, image and power in the creation and production of a personal story. (I, II, III)
4. Identify, describe and apply the basic principles, methods and techniques used to craft a personal story. (II)
5. Identify, develop and present the elements of the personal story for peer review. (II)
6. Edit and finalize the story in preparation for the creation of a digital story. (II)
7. Use basic design principles to lay out and produce a storyboard for the personal story. (III)
8. Use computer hardware and software, tools, commands and procedures to manipulate graphics, video and sound in producing and editing the digital story. (III)
9. Edit and produce a short digital personal story. (III)
10. Archive the story on relevant technology. (III)
11. Present the digital story for peer review. (III)

The district note says faculty keep authority over pedagogy, methods, sequencing and assessment.

### Official outline (MCCCD)

- **I. Introduction to Storytelling:** components of the story; significance of the story (voice, place, culture); blending language, image and power.
- **II. Crafting a Personal Story:** creating a journal (people, places, events, memories); writing for self-discovery; identifying elements of the story (text, voice, images); peer editing; finalizing the storyline.
- **III. Creating the Digital Story:** storyboarding (methods, design principles, layout, production); scanning and preparing images; sound and digital video; editing and producing the digital story (graphics, visuals, sound, timing); archiving a digital story; presenting a digital story.

### How the AI idea fits the competencies

- **The bot is the archive (competency 10).** “Archive the story on relevant technology” is the opening: a Gemini Notebook grounded in the student’s collected material is an interactive archive. This is the strongest hook and needs no curriculum change.
- **The bot can’t replace the digital story (competencies 8 and 9).** Students still edit and produce a short digital story with graphics, video and sound. The bot comes after, or alongside, as the archive and an interactive way to present it (competency 11).
- **The journal becomes the archive (outline II.A).** People, places, events and memories are exactly what students collect and upload.
- **Whose story fits “personal” (competencies 3 to 9, outline II.B).** Personal means personal to the student, not only about them. Telling a grandparent’s or community member’s story still leads to self-discovery, which is the arc of Kurzweil’s book.

## Weak points to solve before building

- **A model trained from scratch is the weakest version for this course.** A tiny model (nanoGPT-style) trained on one student’s pile of material copies style, not content: repetition, made-up words, no memory of the facts. It also turns a no-prereq humanities course into a Python and GPU course.
- **The competencies still rule.** “Create, edit, produce and archive a multimedia story” has to be met. The bot works best as the container for the story, not a replacement for it.
- **Personal stories plus AI tools = privacy.** Students would upload family interviews and photos. Use district-approved tools (Gemini student accounts) and teach consent for anyone recorded. Character.ai is not FERPA compliant; skip it.
- **Grading a bot is hard.** Grade what students author (archive, persona design, script, test log), not how clever the bot sounds.
- **Another new course build.** The UX course is already parked until January.

## Who else is doing something like this

- **Amy Kurzweil, *Artificial: A Love Story* (Catapult, 2023).** Graphic memoir about her father building an AI chatbot of her grandfather from his archive of writing. The origin of this course idea and a strong anchor text for options 1 and 2. A New Yorker and Kirkus best book of 2023. - **Erasmus University Rotterdam, “Create Your Own ChatGPT”** (João Gonçalves and Sarah Young). Undergrads train a language model from scratch on texts they choose, then write a reflective report. - **WashU Center for Teaching and Learning.** Chatbot Blueprint and how-to for building Gemini Gems for a course. Useful for writing the assignment. https://ctl.wustl.edu/creating-gemini-gems-chatbots-to-support-teaching/

## Version 3 change log

### What Version 3 does, in one paragraph

Version 2 read like a writing class. Version 3 makes it a visual storytelling studio that still builds writing and communication skills, because those are skills Digital Media Arts and Animation majors need and often lack. Writing was not stripped out. Writing for its own sake (free-floating analysis paragraphs, prose journals, reflective-writing readings) was replaced with the writing visual professionals do on the job: loglines, pitches, a visual treatment, a two-column audio/visual script, storyboard annotations, shot lists, captions, alt text, on-screen type, a tagline, a client-style email to the narrator, written critique, a maker’s statement and case-study copy. Every one of those pieces feeds a visual artifact. Students choose one of three visual forms; audio stays in a supporting role (clean narration, interview audio, sound design and a music bed). The AI Teller moves from Gemini Gems, which education accounts lose in June 2027, to **Gemini Notebook** (formerly NotebookLM), with Gemini skills as an optional add-on once school accounts have them. The calendar follows Michelle’s AVC183 rhythm: two noon deadlines a week (Thursday and Sunday), posting to Discord for critique at noon the day before, three critique comments due with the work, a 24-hour late window at a 10% deduction, and nothing due at midnight. Workload stays at 120 hours: every addition replaced something of equal or larger size.

### Design concept

Students choose a story that is personal to them but not necessarily about them: a grandparent’s move to Arizona, a coach’s first season, a family recipe, a shop on a corner that is gone. With consent from the person whose story it is (the **narrator**), they record an interview and gather an **archive** of photographs, documents, sound, reference images and visual journal pages. They build an AI **Teller** in Gemini Notebook, grounded in that archive, so the archive can be questioned and searched. Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir *Artificial: A Love Story* (Catapult, 2023) is the anchor text: her father builds “Fredbot” from her grandfather’s letters and lecture notes, and she tells the family story by talking with it and drawing it. That is the model for this course: a visual artist who writes, and who uses an AI as one tool among many.

Students then do what no Teller can do for them. They pitch the story with a moodboard, design style frames, write a visual treatment and a two-column script, storyboard and cut an animatic, design key art and titles, and produce a 2 to 4 minute digital story in one of three visual forms. Audio supports the picture: clean narration, the narrator’s real voice, sound design and a music bed. The semester ends with **The Living Archive**, a portfolio piece made of the finished story, its key art, the preserved archive, a screen-recorded demonstration of the Teller and a designed case-study page.

AI is used at every stage as a creative collaborator, built on the two platforms the district pays for. Google Gemini supplies the Gemini app, image generation for moodboards and style exploration, Gemini Notebook for the Teller, Deep Research for background, and Gemini in Docs, Sheets, Slides and Gmail for the writing. Adobe Creative Cloud supplies production: Photoshop, Illustrator, InDesign, Premiere Pro, After Effects, Audition and Adobe Express, with AI-assisted transcription, editing, audio cleanup and captioning, and Firefly and Generative Fill where the account has generative credits. The course keeps one honest critique running through it, borrowed from Kurzweil: when a machine speaks about a person, or draws one, what does it get right, what does it miss, and who decides?

### Design principles applied

- Backward design: the 11 board-approved competencies (C1 to C11) are the course outcomes. Every objective, activity and assessment traces to them in the instructor notes; students see the outcomes in plain language.
- One big project, scaffolded by 10 milestones; one mid-semester project (The Teller and the Micro-Story) whose style frames set the look of the final.
- Visual first, audio supporting. Every week produces something a viewer can look at.
- Writing as a professional skill: every writing task is the kind a designer, animator or editor writes at work, and every one feeds a visual artifact.
- Authentic assessment at Apply level or higher. No exams.
- Grade what the student authors: choices, curation, writing, design, editing, testing, judgment. Never grade how clever the Teller sounds or how polished a generated image is on its own.
- OSCQR, Regular and Substantive Interaction (RSI), UDL and WCAG 2.1 AA designed in from the start.
- Workload honesty: every task is estimated for a student new to the tool, and the course was cut until the estimates fit 120 hours.

## 2. Seat-time reconciliation

**Rule applied:** 3 credits x 40 = 120 student hours. Online delivery of a 3-credit, 6-load Lec+Lab course = 40 hours direct instruction + 80 hours outside work. Target 2.67 direct + 5.33 outside per week.

### 3.3 Project 1: The Teller and the Micro-Story (Week 8, 20%)

**Competencies (instructor view):** C2, C3, C4, C8, C10

**Scenario:** A community oral-history project in the Phoenix area is piloting AI “Tellers” that let visitors search a family or community archive by asking questions. The style frames you design for the micro-story also become the visual direction for your final piece.

**Steps (student-facing):**
1. Load at least 12 archive items into your Teller in Gemini Notebook: your transcript excerpt, at least five captioned images with their metadata, your visual field journal pages, and any documents your narrator approved for AI upload. Continue from your Week 7 prototype.
2. Complete the Teller instruction scaffold in your own words (who the Teller is, whose archive it holds, its voice, what it must never do, what it says when the archive has no answer). Place the instructions in the notebook’s custom instruction setting, or load them as a source titled “Teller Instructions” if that setting is not available. You may ask Gemini to help draft and tighten the instructions; record that in your log.
3. Choose the Teller’s voice. In the **archivist voice**, the Teller speaks about your narrator in the third person and quotes the archive (for example, “In the Week 5 interview, Rosa says…”). In the **first-person voice**, the Teller speaks as the person, still quoting the archive. First person requires written consent: from a living narrator on release item 6, or, for someone who has died, from the family member or estate holder on release item 7. Either voice is graded the same. In every case the Teller is never public; it is shown only by screen recording.
4. Test the Teller with 20 questions in total. The 10 questions from your Week 7 prototype count. Include at least 5 questions the archive cannot answer. Rate each answer accurate, partly accurate, or invented. Change your instructions or sources to fix at least three problems, then ask those questions again.
5. Choose one item from the archive (a photograph, an object, a recording, a place) and write a micro-story script of 120 to 150 words in the two-column A/V template: narration and sound on the left, the picture on the right. If you interviewed yourself, use a clip of your own interview.
6. Design three to five **style frames** in your chosen form. A style frame is a finished still that shows exactly how the final piece will look: composition, color, texture, type and light. At least one frame includes on-screen type (a title or a caption). You may use Gemini image generation (or Firefly, if your account has generative credits) to explore composition, palette and texture; the final frames are made mostly from the archive and your own drawing, photography or footage, and any generated element is labeled in your log.
7. Record your narration and edit a 45 to 60 second sequence: your style frames with limited motion (a pan, zoom, parallax move or one animated element), your narration, the narrator clip and the ambient sound you recorded in Week 5. Use Enhance Speech or a similar tool to clean the audio if needed. Add captions. Export as MP4.
8. Complete the full AI-use statement (4.3).

### 3.8 Critique protocol

Feedback has three parts: **I noticed** (a specific description of what is on the screen or page), **I wondered** (a question about a choice), **What if** (one concrete suggestion). Feedback addresses the work, never the person, and treats every story as belonging to someone real. Written replies name what they are looking at (a frame number, a timecode, a line of the script) so the maker can find it.

**Required critique:** at least three comments of 40 to 80 words by the deadline, at every critiqued assignment.

**Returning on the late-window day:** Your required critique is three comments by the deadline. Some classmates will post during the late window, after most feedback has been given. If you can, return on the late-window day (Monday for the weekend cycle, Friday for the midweek cycle) and leave a comment on any work posted late. This is not graded and earns no points; it is what a good member of an online creative community does. At some point in the semester you may be the one waiting for feedback, and it is worth more to a late project than the points it lost.

### 4.4 Consent, privacy and safety

**Narrator menu (no reason required for any choice):**
1. A family member.
2. A community member the student already knows: a coach, employer, mentor, librarian, faith leader or neighbor.
3. **A self-interview:** a classmate or Gemini asks the questions from the student’s own guide; the student is the narrator, and the story is about a place, an object or the student’s own memory. Counts fully for M3; consent items then cover only the student.
4. A GCC-arranged volunteer narrator, if a pool exists (CONFIRM).
5. Last resort: a public oral history from an openly licensed or public-domain collection the student has a personal connection to, plus a self-interview about that connection (license confirmed by the instructor).

No student has to choose a story that exposes their family, and no one has to explain why they chose a different one.

**Consent sequence (following Oral History Association practice):**

| When | Document | How it may be given |
|---|---|---|
| Week 3, after M1 is approved | Informal ask; the student explains the project briefly | Conversation |
| Week 4, M2 | **Project Information Sheet** sent with the student’s project email and the OHA “For Participants” page; **Narrator Agreement to Be Recorded** | E-signature (Google Form), a photo of a signed page, or a text or email reply; the narrator also says yes on the recording slate in Week 5 |
| Week 5 or 6, after the interview (M4) | **Release** with separate yes/no items (below) | E-signature or signed page; guardian signature if the narrator is under 18 |
| Week 13 to 14 | **Narrator review** of the rough cut | Narrator watches; student records the response and any requested changes in the review form |
| Week 15 | End-of-semester Teller plan confirmed | Release item 10 |

**Upload rule:** nothing from the interview goes into any AI tool until the release is signed with item 4 checked. A student who is waiting transcribes a 10-minute excerpt by hand or with a local tool.

**Release items** (each yes or no):
1. My recording and transcript may be used in the student’s class project.
2. My materials and the student’s Teller may be shared with the instructor and classmates inside the course for peer review, including in the course’s private Discord server. (If no: the student shares only a screen recording with private details cut, or uses an alternate discussion prompt.)
3. My photographs and documents may be scanned and used (list attached).
4. My words, recordings, photographs and documents may be uploaded to the AI tools named on the information sheet, in the student’s district Google account (and, for image tools, the student’s district Adobe account).
5. A Teller may answer questions about me in the third person, quoting my words.
6. A Teller may speak as me in the first person, quoting my recorded words (living narrator; default no). The Teller is never public in any voice; it may be shown only by screen recording, as item 9 allows.
7. For a story about someone who has died: a Teller may speak as [name] in the first person, quoting the archive. Signed by the family member or estate holder: name, relationship, signature (default no). The archivist voice needs no extra consent.
8. The finished story and its key art may be shown: class only / unlisted link (default) / public. Name used: full name / first name only / no name.
9. A short screen recording of the Teller may appear on the student’s case-study page. The Teller itself is never public.
10. At the end of the semester the Teller is: deleted (default) / kept by the student until a stated week / transferred to me. The archive is copied to me and to the student’s personal storage: yes / no.
11. Restrictions: topics, people not to be named, material not to be shared, or a date before which something may not be public.

There is no item for synthesizing the narrator’s voice or likeness; that is not permitted in this course. That includes AI images, animation or video generated to resemble the narrator or any real, identifiable person. Students may draw or animate a narrator by hand, or stylize their own photographs of a narrator who agreed to item 3, because those are authored, not generated.

**Withdrawal:** the narrator may withdraw at any time. The student then removes the material from the Drive folder, Gemini Notebook sources, any image tool library, any discussion post and any posted video, and tells the instructor. Anything already seen by classmates cannot be unseen; the information sheet says so.

**Sensitive information:** students do not ask about immigration or legal status, legal cases, health details or criminal history. If a narrator raises them, the student offers to stop recording, and cuts those passages from every transcript before any AI upload. These details never go into the Teller or any public version, even with consent. Students do not upload identification numbers, financial, medical or immigration documents to any tool.

**Third parties:** people mentioned but not interviewed are not named or shown recognizably in public versions without a reason.

**Cultural protocols:** some stories belong to a community. Students ask the narrator what may be shared and with whom, record the answer, and do not upload restricted or sacred material to any AI tool or use it as a style reference. In Arizona, tribal nations hold sovereignty over cultural knowledge and imagery; students talk with the instructor before using such material (CONFIRM whether GCC has guidance or a liaison).

**Grief:** content notes appear in Getting Started and Weeks 1, 2 and 7 (grief, death, migration, AI recreations of people who have died), with links to GCC Counseling and the Dean of Students [links to confirm]. A student choosing a death within about the past year checks in with the instructor first; this is a conversation, not a gate. For someone who has died, the student chooses the archivist voice or first person; first person requires written consent from the family member or estate holder (release item 7). A Teller about someone who has died is never public in either voice and is shown only by screen recording. In D6, Tellers about someone who has died are shown only to classmates who opt in.

**Minors:**
- *Minor as narrator:* a guardian signs; the minor agrees on the recording; first name only or none in any shared version; no Teller outside the course; no recognizable images of the minor in public versions without guardian consent.
- *Minor as student:* interviews only adults the student already knows, or by phone or video call with a guardian aware; never alone with an unfamiliar adult. Guardian consent for any public posting.

**Recording law:** Arizona is a one-party consent state (A.R.S. § 13-3005; [Digital Media Law Project summary](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/arizona-recording-law)). This course requires everyone recorded to agree anyway. Some states require all-party consent for calls; students interviewing across state lines follow the stricter rule. Students filming b-roll in public places avoid recognizable bystanders or ask permission.

   > This course runs on two noon deadlines a week. Nothing is ever due at midnight.
   >

   >

   >
   > **The Final Project** is due Wednesday of finals week at noon and closes Thursday of finals week at noon. It has no critique.
   >
   > **Week 1** has one deadline: post by Wednesday at noon, due Thursday at noon, closing Friday at noon. From Week 2 on, every week has one midweek and one weekend deadline, and never more than two.
   >
   > **The late window.** Work submitted after the deadline and before the hard close (24 hours) is accepted with a 10% deduction. Nothing is accepted after the hard close. If an emergency affects a deadline, contact the instructor before the hard close.
   >
   > **Posting late for critique.** Work posted for critique after the posting time may receive less peer feedback, because classmates comment on the work that is there when they sit down to critique. You still owe your own three comments by the deadline.
   >
   > **Your critique.** For every critiqued assignment, comment on at least three classmates’ posts by the deadline, using the critique protocol (I noticed, I wondered, What if). Each comment is 40 to 80 words and names the frame, timecode or line it is about.
   >
   > **Returning on the late-window day.** Your required critique is three comments by the deadline. Some classmates will post during the late window, after most feedback has been given. If you can, return on the late-window day (Monday for the weekend cycle, Friday for the midweek cycle) and leave a comment on any work posted late. This is not graded and earns no points; it is what a good member of an online creative community does. At some point in the semester you may be the one waiting for feedback, and it is worth more to a late project than the points it lost.
   >
   > **What goes in Canvas.** One submission per assignment: your work (file or link), a link or screenshot of your Discord post, the feedback you received (screenshot or short summary), and links or screenshots of your three comments. Use the critique record template. Some assignments have no critique because they contain private material (consent forms, releases, recordings, transcripts, Teller links, the final archive).
