## Storyteller rebuild (24 Oct 2026, supersedes the film-based design below)

The course is about building a storyteller tool, not making a film. Students build a storyteller in Gemini Notebook from a consented archive, then interview it to pull the story out, the way Amy Kurzweil interviews her grandfather’s bot in *Artificial*. The recorded, edited interview (3 to 5 minutes) is the produced story and the archive of the story.

- Build tool: Gemini Notebook only.
- The storyteller speaks as the person (first person, only if the narrator agrees to that mode on the consent form) or as a narrator. It always says it’s an AI. Labeled synthetic voice allowed; no cloned voices or likenesses.
- Week 1 demo storyteller: the instructor’s, built from a public-domain WPA Federal Writers’ Project life history (Library of Congress).
- Early weeks are compressed so Weeks 6 to 15 go to building, testing, designing and recording the storyteller.
- 15 modules, one per week, 8 hours each, 120 hours (restructured 24 Oct 2026 from 17). Module 1 (Week 1) merges Getting Started, the AI Primer and What Is a Story?, with the demo storyteller interview; Modules 2 to 15 are the old Modules 3 to 16. Week 1 has one deadline (Thursday). Projects are 30 points each; the Final is due Friday of finals week at noon. Backup of the 17-module version: `drafts/avc150-17-modules/`.
- Gone: the 2 to 4 minute film, story forms, A/V script, animatic, film storyboard and rough cut. The conversation map replaces the storyboard.
- The Canvas import file (`AVC150_Fall2027_DRAFT.imscc`) is now the storyteller version, rebuilt from `course.html` (15 modules, Fall 2027 dates). The film version is in `drafts/avc150-before-storyteller/`.

## The idea

Rebuild AVC 150 so the story is told through an AI the students make. Students collect the raw material of a story (interviews, photos, audio, writing, objects), then build a bot that tells it, or a character inside it that people can talk to. Could be a small language model, a chatbot, a Gemini Gem, or something else. Format still open.

**Whose story:** a story that is personal to the student but not necessarily about them: a family member’s, a community member’s, or a place or object they have a connection to. Same as Kurzweil telling her grandfather’s story. Consent from whoever’s story it is becomes part of the assignment.

Where the idea came from: Amy Kurzweil’s graphic memoir *Artificial: A Love Story* (Catapult, 2023). Her father builds a chatbot of her grandfather Fred from his archive of letters, music and writing, and she works through the family story by talking with it and drawing it. It’s the whole course in one book: collect the archive, build the teller, then make a multimedia story about what the bot gets right and wrong. https://amykurzweil.com/

## What the course bank says

- **AVC150 Multimedia Storytelling**, 3 credits, no prerequisites. Gen ed: AHM and HU.
- Official description: students find and develop their personal stories, using digital technology to create, edit, produce and archive a multimedia story.
- Current course record runs 2026 Fall to current (Governing Board approved 27 Feb 2024).
- It’s a choice course in the district’s Animation and Time-Based Media AAS, the Animation CCL, the Time-Based Media CCL, and the Applied Storytelling certificate (Estrella Mountain, South Mountain).
- Lec + lab, 6 periods, occupational subject type.

### Official competencies (MCCCD)

1. Present an overview of storytelling, including elements of the story, the power of the story and a variety of ways to present a story. (I)
2. Explain the impact of voice, place and culture in storytelling. (I)
3. Describe the importance of effectively blending language, image and power in the creation and production of a personal story. (I, II, III)
4. Identify, describe and apply the basic principles, methods and techniques used to craft a personal story. (II)
5. Identify, develop and present the elements of the personal story for peer review. (II)
6. Edit and finalize the story in preparation for the creation of a digital story. (II)
7. Use basic design principles to lay out and produce a storyboard for the personal story. (III)
8. Use computer hardware and software, tools, commands and procedures to manipulate graphics, video and sound in producing and editing the digital story. (III)
9. Edit and produce a short digital personal story. (III)
10. Archive the story on relevant technology. (III)
11. Present the digital story for peer review. (III)

The district note says faculty keep authority over pedagogy, methods, sequencing and assessment.

### Official outline (MCCCD)

- **I. Introduction to Storytelling:** components of the story; significance of the story (voice, place, culture); blending language, image and power.
- **II. Crafting a Personal Story:** creating a journal (people, places, events, memories); writing for self-discovery; identifying elements of the story (text, voice, images); peer editing; finalizing the storyline.
- **III. Creating the Digital Story:** storyboarding (methods, design principles, layout, production); scanning and preparing images; sound and digital video; editing and producing the digital story (graphics, visuals, sound, timing); archiving a digital story; presenting a digital story.

### How the AI idea fits the competencies

- **The bot is the archive (competency 10).** “Archive the story on relevant technology” is the opening: a Gemini Notebook grounded in the student’s collected material is an interactive archive. This is the strongest hook and needs no curriculum change.
- **The bot can’t replace the digital story (competencies 8 and 9).** Students still edit and produce a short digital story with graphics, video and sound. The bot comes after, or alongside, as the archive and an interactive way to present it (competency 11).
- **The journal becomes the archive (outline II.A).** People, places, events and memories are exactly what students collect and upload.
- **Whose story fits “personal” (competencies 3 to 9, outline II.B).** Personal means personal to the student, not only about them. Telling a grandparent’s or community member’s story still leads to self-discovery, which is the arc of Kurzweil’s book.

## Weak points to solve before building

- **A model trained from scratch is the weakest version for this course.** A tiny model (nanoGPT-style) trained on one student’s pile of material copies style, not content: repetition, made-up words, no memory of the facts. It also turns a no-prereq humanities course into a Python and GPU course.
- **The competencies still rule.** “Create, edit, produce and archive a multimedia story” has to be met. The bot works best as the container for the story, not a replacement for it.
- **Personal stories plus AI tools = privacy.** Students would upload family interviews and photos. Use district-approved tools (Gemini student accounts) and teach consent for anyone recorded. Character.ai is not FERPA compliant; skip it.
- **Grading a bot is hard.** Grade what students author (archive, persona design, script, test log), not how clever the bot sounds.
- **Another new course build.** The UX course is already parked until January.

## Ways to do it, lowest lift first

| Option | What students make | Tools | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Story archive bot** | Collect the archive, upload it as the bot’s knowledge, write the narrator’s instructions so it tells the story from the material | Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM) (Gemini student accounts, Google Classroom can assign Gems) | Low tech, fits “archive a multimedia story,” the collecting is the storytelling | Bot can wander from the source; under-18 limits |
| **2. Character interview** | Write a character bible; the bot plays the character; classmates interview it | Gemini Notebook, or a Gemini skill once available | Strong on character and worldbuilding | Mostly text; needs a rubric for the interview |
| **3. Bot as narrator or game master** | Branching story with rules; the bot runs it, the reader chooses | Gemini Notebook or a Gemini skill, or Twine with AI help | Teaches story structure and branching | Easy to drift into game design |
| **4. Talk-to-the-story, video version** | Record short video or audio answers to 30 to 50 questions; the bot picks the right clip for each question | Premiere/Audition for the clips, a Gemini Notebook or small web page as the router | Most multimedia; the student’s own voice; the AI routes, it doesn’t write. The Dimensions in Testimony model, scaled down | Most production time; the router needs building |
| **5. Train-a-tiny-model lab** | One week: train a small model on a chosen text and see what it learns | nanoGPT-style notebook, Google Colab | Demystifies AI; strong reflection piece | Not a storytelling tool; keep it to one lab |

**Leaning:** 1 and 4 as the spine (collect, then build the teller), 2 as a mid-semester project, 5 as one lab. Not decided.

## Decision: third story path, character or icon (25 Oct 2026)

Approved by Michelle. Three paths, all graded the same, with identical assessments and points:

| Path | Who the Teller speaks as | Consent package |
|---|---|---|
| Real narrator (main path) | the narrator, or a narrator voice | required, as before |
| Yourself | self-interview | not needed |
| Character or icon | a fictional character, a brand figure (e.g., Barbie), a place or object, or a public-domain historical figure, built from public sources | not needed; source list (title, link, license or fair-use note) and a credit line instead |

Rules: no living real person speaks in the first person (only described by a narrator voice, never quoted or imitated); the Teller still opens with its AI disclosure (“an archive of public sources”); brand characters carry “Unofficial student commentary; [character] is a trademark of [owner].” and stay Restricted on the portfolio site; a “Why this figure matters to me” statement and questions grown from the student’s own question meet the personal-story competencies; heavy topics get a content note at the start of the recording and the GCC Counseling Services link; the instructor approves every character-path topic at the pitch (S2). Under 18: no self-harm, sexual content or graphic violence; body image as media critique with instructor approval; if Gemini blocks sources or answers, switch topics or use the narrator or self-interview path at no grade penalty. Controversial topics are welcome as inquiry (cite sources, more than one view, Teller answers never presented as fact about a real person). Model project: Talking Barbie interviewed about her 6′7″ proportions and the prevalence of eating disorders, built from Barbie’s history and eating-disorder research.

Where: Start Here (Consent and Privacy), Module 1 AI in This Course (Teller rules), Module 3 (path table, decision rule, rules, S2 steps, instructor notes, video outline), and short “Character path:” lines in S3, S4, S5, S6, S8, S10, S14, S15, S16, S19, S20, S21, S23, the Final, the portfolio and Take Your Work With You. design.html (meta description, Guardrails) and forms.html (What a Teller Is, Consent Options) mention the three paths.
