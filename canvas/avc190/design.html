Introduction to Web Design and UX

Courses and Programs · Case study

Course designThe full course

A first web design course built for designers. Students take a ready-made client brand from a style tile to a published, responsive, accessible website, test it with real people along the way, and finish by turning their hand-built design into a small generator for one more page.

Goal

Teach students to design websites that express a brand and serve the people who use them, and to defend every decision with evidence from usability and accessibility testing. The course centers visual design for designers, then subjects each visual choice to empirical testing with real people.

The term is a single authentic project: a published, responsive, five-to-seven-page website for a fictitious client that Gemini role-plays. Because this is not a branding course, each student chooses one of four ready-made brand kits (logo, colors with hex values, a Google Fonts pairing, voice, photo style and key messages); Graphic Design and Digital Media Arts students may bring a brand of their own with approval. Gemini plays the owner and supplies the brief and the interview answers; the student supplies the research questions, the structure, every visual and the defense.

Audience

A first-year course that assumes no prior web or coding experience. No coding is required: every student builds and publishes in Google Sites, and a code option is available. A short See the Code lesson shows the HTML and CSS that Sites writes, so students understand what the accessibility checks are reading. It serves four programs.

Process

Backward design set the sequence: the final website and case-study rubric was written first, with one criterion per competency, and each of the twenty-four process steps builds a skill that rubric assesses. Research precedes form: discovery, personas and content come before any visual, so students design around real content rather than placeholder text. Proto-personas are labeled as assumptions and checked in two rounds of usability testing with adult participants. Gemini never stands in for a test participant.

Outcomes

The six course competencies, each mapped to module objectives written with the revised Bloom’s taxonomy and to criteria in both projects. A student can:

  1. Analyze target audiences, user needs, competitor websites, and website objectives.
  2. Develop website structures using customer journeys, user flows, content strategies, sitemaps, and wireframes.
  3. Produce graphics and visual concepts for web publication using typography, color, and imagery.
  4. Design responsive website interfaces using visual design, navigation, and usability principles.
  5. Evaluate website designs for usability, accessibility, and user experience.
  6. Produce an interactive, multi-page website for publication using contemporary web design and publishing tools.

Pacing and interaction

About eight hours a week across fifteen modules (Module 0 Getting Started, then fourteen project modules), each in the same order. Two noon deadlines a week; project steps are posted to Discord the day before for structured peer critique (I notice, Because, What if). Formative feedback on the discovery and test-plan steps returns within 48 hours, before the next step depends on it. Office hours are held twice a week and recorded.

AI in This Course

Gemini is used with the Maricopa (Google Workspace for Education) account only, under which student data is not reviewed by people, not used to train models and not used for ads. Gemini writes, role-plays and writes code from the student’s design; it never designs, and no AI-generated image, logo, icon, mockup or layout is accepted in any assignment.

How AI Supports Learning Here

  • AI as client. Role-play gives every student a distinct, responsive stakeholder without recruiting a real business. Students practice discovery interviewing, quote the answer behind each follow-up, and translate answers into measurable objectives.
  • AI as unreliable narrator. Because the client invents freely, students classify its claims as verified, fictional or invented, building source evaluation into research.
  • Draft and edit. Students record the reading level of Gemini’s copy and of their edit, and compare drafted and rewritten alt text, so the editorial judgment is visible and assessable.
  • AI as developer, student as designer. Students specify inputs, outputs, rules and style-tile values before prompting, then verify the result with known-answer test cases, a keyboard-only pass and a screen reader. AI-written code is treated as a draft that must earn its place.
  • Verify and revise. The AI Primer in Module 0 teaches prompting with role, task, context and format, two revisions, and a fact-check of three claims against sources. It addresses the costs as well, including bias and energy use (data centers drew about 1.5% of global electricity in 2024, per the International Energy Agency).
  • Hand first, generator last. Students build the whole site by hand before Module 13, then turn it into a design system with two agents and use it once, for a campaign landing page. The grade rests on the rules, the agent testing and the student’s edits.
  • AI-use log. Every submission logs the tool, the request, what was kept, what was changed and why; “None” is a valid entry. The log doubles as evidence that each output was checked for accuracy and accessibility.

Guardrails

  • Account and data. Maricopa account only, under the district’s Gemini for Education terms; no other AI tool. The instructor never places student work in a public AI tool.
  • Research ethics. Card sorts and usability tests use adult participants, a scripted consent read word for word, coded notes (P1 to P3), and no sensitive data. Identifiable participant data never enters an AI tool, and AI never generates participants, quotes or results.
  • Never AI-made. Logos, type, color, layouts, imagery and interfaces are the student’s. Imagery is the student’s own or licensed for reuse and logged.
  • Authorship of code. Code built with Gemini Canvas counts as the student’s work when the design is theirs, the log shows the prompts and revisions, and the student can explain it in the defense.
  • Students under 18. If a Gemini feature is blocked, the student does not switch accounts or tools; the instructor supplies a written client brief, interview answers or copy drafts, and the assignment is graded the same way.

What Students Build

  • An AI-built interactive element. A Gemini Canvas app mocked up first in the Figma prototype, specified in a written prompt with the style-tile values, tested against at least five known answers, checked for keyboard access and labels, and exported to Google Sites. If it cannot be made accessible, a plain-text alternative sits on the same page.
  • A campaign landing page. One more page for the same client, built from the student’s own Figma styles and components, with copy drafted by a content agent, checked by a brand checker and approved word by word, and images from a licensed source.
  • A published website. Five to seven pages in Google Sites with a custom theme and an accessibility statement naming WCAG 2.1 AA, what was tested and known limits.

How to Adapt This

  1. Use AI as a role-played client. Have students write their questions in a Google Doc before the conversation, so version history proves authorship, and grade the questions and objectives rather than the answers.
  2. Require a specification before AI writes code. A written prompt with inputs, outputs, rules and style values, followed by known-answer tests and a keyboard check, turns code generation into a design and verification exercise.
  3. Grade the edit. Ask for the AI draft and the student’s revision side by side, with a measurable change such as reading level.

  • WAVE, the WebAIM Contrast Checker, NVDA and VoiceOver: accessibility checks before testing with people.

How the Course Was Tested

Before launch, the course went through synthetic student walk-throughs, a quality-rubric review, and an alignment and AI-exposure audit. A synthetic review panel (a senior web and UI designer, graphic design faculty, a web accessibility specialist and an instructional designer) also reviewed the draft. No real students were involved in testing.

Synthetic Student Walk-Throughs

The testers are synthetic students: AI agents, each built from one persona in the Student Journey Barriers Study. Each agent worked through the course module by module as that student and logged every point where it got stuck or could not finish. A fixer pass applied the findings, and a second round re-ran all eight agents against the revised course.

Changes recorded from the review passes include a dated schedule with closures handled, feedback timed to arrive before the next step depends on it, a rule that no AI-generated visual is accepted in any assignment, recorded explanations on discovery and the final reflection, and seat time balanced to about 120 hours.

Quality and Alignment Reviews

The course was reviewed against the SUNY OSCQR online course quality rubric, and every objective was audited against the revised Bloom’s taxonomy (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001) so that each sits at or below its parent competency and is measured by a graded item.

Every Assessment Checked for AI Resistance

Every graded assessment was audited for AI exposure, asking whether a student could hand the task to an AI and pass. The design answers are process evidence and versioned drafts, work tied to the student’s own client and test participants, recorded explanation, peer critique on Discord, the AI-use log, and the rule that Gemini never designs.

Accessibility

Designed with Universal Design for Learning to WCAG 2.1 AA, with captioned instructor video, free alternatives for any tool a student cannot run, and approved equivalents for assistive-technology users. Accessibility is also course content: students check their own sites with automated tools, a contrast checker and a screen reader before testing with people. Student work carries hand-corrected captions, corrected transcripts, alt text and tagged documents, worth about 10 percent of each media rubric.

Status

Designed and fully built: fifteen modules, twenty-four process steps, eight exercises and two projects. Offered every spring and fall.
