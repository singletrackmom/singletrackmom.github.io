How the Course Works

You design, build, test and publish a website for a made-up client. Gemini, the college AI tool, plays the client. You do all of the visual design. No coding is needed: you build the site in Google Sites.

The course is fully online, with no set class times. Plan on about 8 hours a week for 15 weeks. Every module has the same parts in the same order: Objectives, Overview, Watch, Read, Practice, Assessment and Critique. Each graded item shows its points, steps and rubric (the scoring guide).

What you need: a Windows or Mac laptop or desktop. A phone helps for testing but cannot replace a computer. All software is free to you. No computer or reliable internet? Borrow one with a Student Technology Request in Week 1.

Deadlines

Work is due at noon, Arizona time, every Thursday and Sunday. Process Work and Projects go on Discord by noon the day before, and your three critique comments are due with the work. Work up to 24 hours late loses 10%; after that it is not accepted. Week 1 has only the Thursday deadline. When a holiday lands on a deadline, the deadline moves one day later and the module says so. Thanksgiving week has no work.

Feedback on Process Work comes back within five days, and on Projects within seven. Messages get a reply within one business day. Emergency? Message your instructor before the 24 hours are up.

Critique on Discord

Join the Digital Media Arts Discord and find the AVC190 channel. A screen name is fine. Can’t use Discord? Post in the Canvas discussion Critique Without Discord, with no penalty. Every comment has three parts:

  1. I notice: what you saw, without judging. (“On the phone, the Order button sits below three paragraphs.”)
  2. Because: the design principle or evidence. (“Visual hierarchy: the main action should be easiest to see.”)
  3. What if: one clear suggestion. (“What if the button moved to the top?”)

Comment on the work, not the person. “Looks good” does not count. Once a week, comment on a classmate whose client type differs from yours. Comments on late posts are welcome but not graded.

Accessibility of Your Work

Everything you make must work for people who cannot see it or hear it. Every rubric gives points for this.

  • Video: captions. Start with automatic captions, then fix every error by hand.
  • Audio: a transcript, corrected by hand.
  • Images: alt text (a short description of what matters) on every meaningful image. Mark decorative images as decorative.
  • Documents and web pages: real headings, readable contrast, clear link text. Check pages with WAVE.
  • Discord: alt text on images; captions or a transcript on audio and video.

The Final Project

The whole term builds one published website. Every Process Work step is a piece of it. The two Projects are P1 Website Design Proposal (Week 8) and P2 Final Website and Case Study (Week 15). The site must have:

  • Five to seven pages: Home, About, services or products, the interactive element page, Visit or Contact, and one or two more if your sitemap needs them.
  • One interactive element beyond the menu that responds to what a visitor enters (an estimator, filter, quiz or finder), works with a keyboard, and takes under two minutes to use.
  • No real payments, logins or personal data. A form may appear only as a demo that says it is a class project.
  • An accessibility statement that names WCAG 2.1 AA (the web accessibility standard), what you tested and known limits.
  • A live link your instructor and classmates can open. A district-only link is fine if district rules require it.
  • Optional motion that respects the reduced-motion setting and never flashes more than three times a second.

These do not count: one long page with jump links, a Figma prototype only, a video or slideshow as the only interactive part, or an element that needs a mouse. The design freezes at the end of Week 13; Week 14 is for fixes.

AI in This Course

This course uses generative AI (Google Gemini) in assignments.

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account. That is Gemini for Education, the educational version of Gemini. Google states that what you enter there is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models (Gemini for Education; Google’s education FAQ). Your artwork and writing are not used to teach the AI. A personal Google account is different: depending on its settings, what you enter there may be used to improve Google’s AI. That is why every assignment uses your Maricopa account.

Industry expects it. In the 2024 Microsoft and LinkedIn Work Trend Index, 66% of business leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills, and 71% said they would rather hire a less experienced candidate with AI skills than a more experienced candidate without them (Work Trend Index). Our industry advisory board recommends that we teach these tools.

  • Using AI here is not cheating. Cheating is turning in work you did not do. Here, AI use is assigned, you list it in an AI-use log, and you are graded on your own thinking: your research, decisions and design. Other courses may have different rules.
  • Privacy. Your instructor never puts your work into a public AI tool.
  • AI does not make your work. Gemini helps with research, writing, feedback, planning and code. It never designs. Your logo, type, color, layouts, images and interface are your own, and you show your process. No AI-made images, logos, icons or mockups are accepted.
  • You will learn to question it. AI makes confident mistakes and repeats bias from its training data. You check every answer.
  • It has real costs. AI is trained on artists’ work, often without permission. Data centers used about 1.5% of the world’s electricity in 2024, and that may double by 2030 (International Energy Agency).
  • Which tools: only the Gemini app (including Gemini Canvas for code), Gemini in Docs and Sheets, and Gemini Notebook, with your Maricopa account.

E0 Figma Warm-Up: A Three-Page Site

Objectives

  • 1.2 Produce a simple three-page website layout in Figma with consistent navigation, readable text and named layers.

10 points, Exercises.

Overview

You will make three desktop pages in Figma for Morning Mesa Coffee Cart, a made-up business: Home, About and Contact. Your instructor gives you all the words and photos, so you can focus on learning the tool. Plan on one sitting of about 2.5 hours. You do not need components, auto layout or prototyping yet; those come in Modules 4 and 8. This is an exercise, so there is no Discord critique.

AI: none. This is hands-on practice in Figma. Your AI-use log for E0 says “None.”

Instructions

  1. Make your Figma account. Go to Figma for Education and sign up with your Maricopa email. Figma asks college students to confirm they are 18 or older for the education plan. If you cannot get it, sign up for the free Starter plan instead; it has everything this exercise needs. Still stuck? Your instructor can share a starter file for you to copy, or approve Penpot.
  2. Get the content. Open the Morning Mesa content pack from the link in the Canvas assignment. It has the words for each page and five photos. Use the words as they are; this exercise is about layout, not writing.
  3. Start a file. In Figma, choose Create (or the plus sign), then Design. Name the file “Your Last Name Figma Warm-Up.”
  4. Make three frames. A frame is one page. Press F for the Frame tool, then choose Desktop in the panel on the right. Make three desktop frames side by side. Double-click each frame’s name and rename them Home, About and Contact.
  5. Build the menu bar on Home. Press R for the Rectangle tool and draw a bar across the top of the frame. Press T for the Text tool and type the business name on the left and the words Home, About and Contact on the right.
  6. Add the words. With the Text tool, place the headline and paragraph on Home, the About paragraph on About, and the contact details on Contact. Make headlines large and body text at least 16 pixels. Use dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark one, so it is easy to read.
  7. Place the photos. Drag photos from your computer into a frame. Put at least one photo on each page. To resize a photo without stretching it, hold Shift while you drag a corner.
  8. Line things up. Select two or more layers, then use the alignment buttons at the top of the right panel (align left, align center, align top). Keep the same space at the left edge of every page.
  9. Group and copy the menu bar. Select the bar and its text and press Ctrl + G (Command + G on a Mac) to group them. Name the group Menu. Copy it, select the About frame and paste, then do the same on Contact. Check that the menu sits in the same spot on all three pages.
  10. Name your layers. In the Layers panel on the left, double-click each layer and give it a plain name, such as Headline, Hero photo or Contact email. No “Rectangle 4.”
  11. Share a view link. Click Share, set the link so anyone with the link can view, and copy it. Open the link in a private browser window to make sure it works.
  12. Export each page. Select a frame, find Export at the bottom of the right panel, choose PNG and click Export. Do this for all three frames.
  13. Submit in Canvas: the view link, the three PNG files, and alt text for each PNG: one or two sentences that say what the page shows.

Accessibility: check that your text is easy to read against its background, and write alt text for every PNG you submit.

Overview

Instructions

  • 6.1 Produce web-ready graphics and images at appropriate resolutions, formats and file sizes. (Course Competency 3)
  • 6.2 Design responsive home page mockups with a clear visual hierarchy at three screen widths. (Course Competency 4)

Overview

Web images follow different rules from print. Size is measured in pixels, not dots per inch. A hero image (the large image at the top of a page) may need to be 2,400 pixels wide to look sharp on a laptop, yet small enough in file size to load fast on a phone. Logos and icons go in SVG, a vector format that stays sharp at any size. Photos usually go in JPEG or WebP. Flat graphics or graphics with see-through areas go in PNG or SVG.

Then you design the home page. Responsive design means one page that rearranges itself for phone, tablet and desktop. Visual hierarchy means the most important thing is what people see first, at every width. This week you make a mockup: a full-color, still picture of the page.

Overview

Interface elements are the parts visitors touch: menus, buttons, links, form fields and cards. Each one has states. A button looks one way at rest, another when the mouse hovers over it, another when it has keyboard focus (it is selected with the Tab key), and another when it is pressed or turned off. This week you build these parts as Figma components (reusable parts). Then you link your mockups into a clickable prototype that people test next week. You also plan your build and decide on the code option.

Testing Rules and Consent Script

Card sorts and usability tests in this course are class learning activities. Follow these rules every time.

  1. Adults only. Participants are 18 or older. Do not recruit anyone younger, including siblings or classmates under 18.
  2. Mostly people outside the course. At most one participant per round may be a classmate.

Take Your Work With You (ungraded, Week 15)

Your Maricopa Google account may close after you leave the college. Do this before the course ends, so you keep everything you made even if you stop out, transfer or graduate. It takes about 45 minutes.

  1. Save your files in one place. Make one Google Drive folder for this course. Put in it your Figma files, exported graphics, case study, presentation recording and defense answers and your AI-use log. For each Figma file, choose File, then Save local copy, to download a .fig copy.
  2. Copy everything to a personal Google account. Make a personal Google account if you do not have one. First make your own copy (File, then Make a copy) of any file someone shared with you, because files you do not own are not copied. Then, signed in to your Maricopa account, open Google Takeout Transfer and copy your Drive and Gmail to your personal account (see Google’s help page). It can take up to a week, and the copies arrive unshared. If Transfer is not available on your account, use Google Takeout to download your Drive as .zip files and save them on your computer.
  3. Take your AI work with you. Save your interactive element and any code Gemini wrote for you: copy the Gemini Canvas app’s code into a file in your folder, and keep the prompt or spec you gave it. The instructions and source files are what rebuild it, so keep each one’s instructions in a Google Doc and its sources in your course folder. To keep your chats, use Google Takeout: choose My Activity, then Gemini Apps. After you leave, rebuild it in Gemini with your personal account: make a new one, paste the instructions and upload the sources. If you used a Gem or Gemini Notebook for client role-play or drafts, keep its instructions and sources the same way.
    • Know the privacy difference. Your Maricopa account keeps your Gemini work out of AI training (see Start Here). A personal account does not have the same protections, and Google may use your activity to improve its AI, depending on your settings. In your personal account, open Gemini, then Settings & help, then Activity (Gemini Apps Activity), and decide whether to turn off Keep Activity. Never put anyone else’s private information into a personal account.
  4. Move your Google Site. Takeout Transfer copies Drive, Gmail and Photos. Do not count on it to move your client website. First keep the content: put the site’s text in a Google Doc and its images in your course folder, then open each published page and save it as a PDF (File, then Print, then Save as PDF). Then try a direct copy: in the site editor, choose Share and add your personal email as an editor. Sign in with your personal account, open the site, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing outside Maricopa is blocked, rebuild the site in your personal account at sites.google.com with the same content and publish it again. The web address changes, so update it on your resume and profiles.
  5. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
