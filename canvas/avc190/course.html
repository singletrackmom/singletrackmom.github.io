The Final Project

The whole term builds one published website. Every Process Work step is a piece of it. The two Projects are Project 1: Website Design Proposal (Week 7) and Project 2: Final Website and Case Study (Week 15). The site must have:

  • Five to seven pages: Home, About, services or products, the interactive element page, Visit or Contact, and one or two more if your sitemap needs them.
  • One interactive element beyond the menu that responds to what a visitor enters (an estimator, filter, quiz or finder), works with a keyboard, and takes under two minutes to use.
  • No real payments, logins or personal data. A form may appear only as a demo that says it is a class project.
  • An accessibility statement that names WCAG 2.1 AA (the web accessibility standard), what you tested and known limits.
  • A live link your instructor and classmates can open. A district-only link is fine if district rules require it.
  • Optional motion that respects the reduced-motion setting and never flashes more than three times a second.

These do not count: one long page with jump links, a Figma prototype only, a video or slideshow as the only interactive part, or an element that needs a mouse. The design freezes at the end of Week 12; Week 13 is for fixes. In Week 14 you add one campaign landing page, made with your own design system (see Module 13).

AI Primer

This course uses Google Gemini in assignments. The rules for AI use and the AI-use log are in the AI section of the syllabus. Google’s own wording on how your Maricopa account protects your work: Gemini for Education and Google’s education FAQ.

AI Primer (10 points, Process Work)

What this is. Your first time using Gemini in this course, and it takes about an hour. No experience with AI is needed. Complete Technology Setup first.

What Gemini is. Gemini is an AI chat tool from Google. You type a request, called a prompt, and it writes an answer. It learned from an enormous amount of text written by people, so it can sound knowledgeable about almost anything. It does not know you, and it has never been where you have been.

Why this assignment. Some assignments in this course use Gemini for research and feedback. Before you use it for that, you will see for yourself what it does well, where it falls short, and what you bring that it cannot.

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account, so what you enter is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models. See AI in This Course for Google’s exact wording.

This assignment measures Module Objective 0.2, which supports Course Competency 6.

Part 1: Sign in

  1. Open Chrome and go to gemini.google.com.
  2. If you see Sign in in the top right corner, click it. Sign in with your Maricopa account: your MEID followed by @maricopa.edu, then your MEID password on the Maricopa sign-in page.
  3. Check the top right corner. It should show your Maricopa account. If it shows a personal Gmail account, click the picture and switch to your Maricopa account.

If Gemini does not open or says it is not available for your account (this can happen for students under 18), message your instructor in Canvas Inbox. You will receive an equal option with the same grade.

Part 2: Ask the question

  1. Click the box at the bottom that says Ask Gemini.
  2. Type Do I need to learn AI to get a good job in the digital media industry? and press Enter.
  3. Copy your prompt and Gemini’s answer into a Google Doc.

Part 3: Read it like a critic

  1. In your Doc, mark:
    • one point you agree with;
    • one point that sounds generic, overstated or like a sales pitch;
    • one question you would rather ask a working professional in the field.
  2. Gemini is made by Google, a company that sells AI. Write one sentence on how that might shape its answer.

Part 4: Give it better directions

A stronger prompt tells Gemini four things: a role (who it should act as), a task (what to do), context (who it is for and why) and a format (how the answer should look). Example: You are a hiring manager at a small design studio in Phoenix. List the skills you look for in a new junior designer, and say honestly where AI skills rank among them. I am a first-year community college student. Use a short ranked list.

  1. Rewrite your prompt using all four parts, and add one detail about yourself: the kind of work you want to do. Press Enter.
  2. Copy the new prompt and answer into your Doc. Write one sentence on how the answer changed.

Part 5: Reflect

  1. In four to six sentences: Did either answer change how you think about AI and your career? Where might a tool like this help you in your own work, and where would you not want to use it?

Part 6: Start your AI-use log

  1. At the bottom of your Doc, add a table with three columns: Tool, What I asked, and What I kept, changed or rejected. Add one row for this assignment. Every assignment in this course has a row like this, so your instructor can see how you used AI and what was your own.

Submit the link to your Google Doc.

Grading (10 points): critic’s mark-up 3; revised prompt with your own detail 3; reflection 2; AI-use log row 2.

Module 1

Meet the Client: Brand, Goals and User Needs

Week 2 · 8 hours

Objectives

Module 1 objectives. By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • 1.1 Identify a client’s business goals, website objectives and constraints from a discovery interview. (Course Competency 1)
  • 1.2 Produce a simple three-page website layout in Figma with consistent navigation, readable text and named layers. (Course Competency 4)

Overview

Every website starts with the client. This week Gemini becomes your client. You choose one of four ready-made brand kits for a fictitious business, and Gemini plays its owner (see the Client Brand Kits and the Client Prompt Kit below). Then you interview the owner in a discovery interview: a first meeting to learn what the business needs. You turn the answers into website objectives: what the site must do for the business and its visitors. Your questions and objectives are graded, because they are the designer’s work.

You also try Figma for the first time. In a short warm-up, you lay out three simple pages for a fictitious neighborhood coffee cart, using words and photos your instructor gives you. You will use Figma all term, so this is your chance to learn the basics before the big project.

Three kinds of design start here. Visual design is how the site looks. User interface (UI) design is how its buttons and menus look and behave. User experience (UX) design is what it is like to use.

Figma Warm-Up: A Three-Page Site

Objectives

  • 1.2 Produce a simple three-page website layout in Figma with consistent navigation, readable text and named layers.

This assignment measures Module Objective 1.2, which supports Course Competency 4.

10 points, Exercises.

Overview

You will make three desktop pages in Figma for Sidewalk Sips, a fictitious neighborhood coffee cart: Home, About and Contact. Your instructor gives you all the words and photos, so you can focus on learning the tool. Plan on one sitting of about 2.5 hours. You do not need components, auto layout or prototyping yet; those come in Modules 4 and 7. This is an exercise, so there is no Discord critique.

AI: none. This is hands-on practice in Figma. Your AI-use log for the Figma Warm-Up says “None.”

Instructions

  1. Make your Figma account. Go to Figma for Education and sign up with your Maricopa email. Figma asks college students to confirm they are 18 or older for the education plan. If you cannot get it, sign up for the free Starter plan instead; it has everything this exercise needs. Still stuck? Your instructor can share a starter file for you to copy, or approve Penpot.
  2. Get the content. Open the Figma Warm-Up Content Pack, a Google Doc linked in the Canvas assignment. It has the headline, the words for each page, the menu labels, the contact details and links to six photos. Download the photos you want to use. Use the words as they are; this exercise is about layout, not writing.
  3. Start a file. In Figma, choose Create (or the plus sign), then Design. Name the file “Your Last Name Figma Warm-Up.”
  4. Make three frames. A frame is one page. Press F for the Frame tool, then choose Desktop in the panel on the right. Make three desktop frames side by side. Double-click each frame’s name and rename them Home, About and Contact.
  5. Build the menu bar on Home. Press R for the Rectangle tool and draw a bar across the top of the frame. Press T for the Text tool and type the business name on the left and the menu labels from the content pack (Home, About and Contact) on the right.
  6. Add the words. With the Text tool, place the headline and paragraph on Home, the About paragraph on About, and the contact details, including the email hello@sidewalksips.example, on Contact. Make headlines large and body text at least 16 pixels. Use dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark one, so it is easy to read.
  7. Place the photos. Drag photos from your computer into a frame. Put at least one photo on each page. The content pack has alt text for each photo; use it when you write your page alt text. To resize a photo without stretching it, hold Shift while you drag a corner.
  8. Line things up. Select two or more layers, then use the alignment buttons at the top of the right panel (align left, align center, align top). Keep the same space at the left edge of every page.
  9. Group and copy the menu bar. Select the bar and its text and press Ctrl + G (Command + G on a Mac) to group them. Name the group Menu. Copy it, select the About frame and paste, then do the same on Contact. Check that the menu sits in the same spot on all three pages.
  10. Name your layers. In the Layers panel on the left, double-click each layer and give it a plain name, such as Headline, Hero photo or Contact email. No “Rectangle 4.”
  11. Share a view link. Click Share, set the link so anyone with the link can view, and copy it. Open the link in a private browser window to make sure it works.
  12. Export each page. Select a frame, find Export at the bottom of the right panel, choose PNG and click Export. Do this for all three frames.
  13. Submit in Canvas: the view link, the three PNG files, and alt text for each PNG: one or two sentences that say what the page shows.

Accessibility: check that your text is easy to read against its background, and write alt text for every PNG you submit.

Now the site gets its look. Your client already has a brand: the kit you chose in Week 2. Your job is to carry it onto the web. Start by reading the kit as signs. Semiotics is the study of signs: how a color, typeface, photo style or icon carries meaning before anyone reads a word. A raspberry wordmark in a round typeface says something different from a black sans serif in all capitals. Ask what each part of your kit tells your personas, and keep that meaning on every page.

The web adds rules. Type must stay readable on a phone. Colors must have enough contrast for people with low vision. A logo must work at 40 pixels tall in a menu bar. You put all of this on a style tile: one page that shows the brand’s web type, colors, buttons and image style, before you design full pages.

Web images follow different rules from print. Size is measured in pixels, not dots per inch. A hero image (the large image at the top of a page) may need to be 2,400 pixels wide to look sharp on a laptop, yet small enough in file size to load fast on a phone. Logos and icons go in SVG, a vector format that stays sharp at any size. Photos usually go in JPEG or WebP.

Overview

In a usability test, you watch real people try to use your design. You do not ask if they like it. You give them a task and watch what happens. Three to five people find most of the serious problems. This is where your persona guesses meet real people. You test your Figma prototype with three adults. They think aloud: they say what they are thinking as they work.

Testing involves real people, so the rules below apply to every session. The Testing Ethics and Consent Check confirms that you know them before you begin.

Testing Rules and Consent Script

Card sorts and usability tests in this course are class learning activities. Follow these rules every time.

  1. Adults only. Participants are 18 or older. Do not recruit anyone younger, including siblings or classmates under 18.
  2. Mostly people outside the course. At most one participant per round may be a classmate.
  3. Consent every time. Read the consent script below, word for word, before each session. Get consent in writing or on a recording.
  4. Collect as little as possible. No health, money, immigration or other private information. No names in your notes: participants are P1, P2 and P3; card sorters are S1 and S2.

You do not need to write code in this course. Google Sites writes it for you. But every page you build is made of HTML (the structure: what each part is) and CSS (the style: how it looks). Seeing it once helps you understand what you are building, and why the accessibility checks in this module work the way they do. Nothing here is on a test. Just look, try the Practice, and notice how it works.

Tags come in pairs

HTML marks each part of a page with tags. Most tags come in pairs: an opening tag, the content, and a closing tag with a slash.

<p>Every cake is made to order.</p>

Here <p> opens a paragraph and </p> closes it. A few tags stand alone, such as <img> for an image.

The page skeleton

Every page has the same frame. The head holds information for the browser, such as the title on the tab. The body holds everything you see. Inside the body, header, main and footer mark the big regions of the page.

<!doctype html>
<html lang="en">
  <head>
    <title>Pinwheel Cakes</title>
  </head>
  <body>
    <header>Logo and menu</header>
    <main>The content of this page</main>
    <footer>Hours, address and links</footer>
  </body>
</html>

Headings, paragraphs, links and images

<h1>Custom Cakes</h1>
<h2>How to order</h2>
<p>Order two weeks ahead.</p>
<a href="order.html">Start your cake order</a>
<img src="cake-counter.webp" alt="Three layer cakes on the shop counter">
  • <h1> is the main heading. A page has one. <h2> and <h3> come under it, in order, like an outline.
  • <a> is a link. The words between the tags are the link text, so they should say where the link goes.
  • <img> places an image. The alt part is the alt text you write in Google Sites.

Two kinds of lists

<ul>
  <li>Birthday</li>
  <li>Wedding</li>
</ul>
<ol>
  <li>Choose a size</li>
  <li>Choose a flavor</li>
</ol>

<ul> is a bulleted list, where order does not matter. <ol> is a numbered list, for steps. Each item is an <li>.

When a closing tag is missing

Browsers try to guess what you meant, and they often guess wrong. Here the heading never closes:

<h2>Our Cakes
<p>Every cake is made to order.</p>

The paragraph may show up big and bold, as part of the heading, and a screen reader may read it all as one long heading. The fix is one closing tag:

<h2>Our Cakes</h2>
<p>Every cake is made to order.</p>

What CSS does

CSS sets the look: fonts, colors, sizes and spacing. Styles can sit on each tag (inline styles), or in one stylesheet that the whole site shares.

<!-- Inline: the style is stuck to one heading -->
<h1 style="color: #A3214F;">Custom Cakes</h1>

/* Stylesheet: one rule styles every h1 on every page */
h1 {
  font-family: "Fredoka", sans-serif;
  color: #A3214F;
}

A stylesheet is better for three reasons. Every page stays consistent. One change updates every page at once. And the HTML stays clean and easier to read. Your Google Sites theme works the same way: change the heading font once, and every page changes.

Why structure matters for accessibility

Screen readers and other assistive technology read the HTML, not the picture on the screen. When you use Sites tools the right way, Sites writes the right HTML for you.

What you do in Sites and the HTML it makes
What you do in Google SitesThe HTML it makesWhy it matters
Use the Title, Heading and Subheading styles in order<h1>, <h2>, <h3>Screen reader users jump from heading to heading to find what they need. Big bold text that is not a real heading is invisible to them.
Add alt text to an imagealt="..."A screen reader reads it aloud. Without it, the image says nothing, or only its file name.

Take Your Work With You (ungraded, Week 15)

Your Maricopa Google account may close after you leave the college. Do this before the course ends, so you keep everything you made even if you stop out, transfer or graduate. It takes about 45 minutes.

  1. Save your files in one place. Make one Google Drive folder for this course. Put in it your Figma files, exported graphics, case study, presentation recording and defense answers and your AI-use log. For each Figma file, choose File, then Save local copy, to download a .fig copy.
  2. Copy everything to a personal Google account. Make a personal Google account if you do not have one. First make your own copy (File, then Make a copy) of any file someone shared with you, because files you do not own are not copied. Then, signed in to your Maricopa account, open Google Takeout Transfer and copy your Drive and Gmail to your personal account (see Google’s help page). It can take up to a week, and the copies arrive unshared. If Transfer is not available on your account, use Google Takeout to download your Drive as .zip files and save them on your computer.
  3. Take your AI work with you. Save your interactive element and any code Gemini wrote for you: copy the Gemini Canvas app’s code into a file in your folder, and keep the prompt or spec you gave it. The instructions and source files are what rebuild it, so keep each one’s instructions in a Google Doc and its sources in your course folder. To keep your chats, use Google Takeout: choose My Activity, then Gemini Apps. After you leave, rebuild it in Gemini with your personal account: make a new one, paste the instructions and upload the sources. If you used a Gem or Gemini Notebook for client role-play or drafts, keep its instructions and sources the same way.
    • Know the privacy difference. Your Maricopa account keeps your Gemini work out of AI training (see Module 0). A personal account does not have the same protections, and Google may use your activity to improve its AI, depending on your settings. In your personal account, open Gemini, then Settings & help, then Activity (Gemini Apps Activity), and decide whether to turn off Keep Activity. Never put anyone else’s private information into a personal account.
  4. Move your Google Site. Takeout Transfer copies Drive, Gmail and Photos. Do not count on it to move your client website. First keep the content: put the site’s text in a Google Doc and its images in your course folder, then open each published page and save it as a PDF (File, then Print, then Save as PDF). Then try a direct copy: in the site editor, choose Share and add your personal email as an editor. Sign in with your personal account, open the site, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing outside Maricopa is blocked, rebuild the site in your personal account at sites.google.com with the same content and publish it again. The web address changes, so update it on your resume and profiles.
  5. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
