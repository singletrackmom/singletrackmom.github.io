## Title

**Introduction to Web Design and UX**

**Use only the new title, description, competencies and outline below. Do not use anything from the old AVC190 (Art of Web Site Design).**

## Description

Introduction to website design and user experience principles. Audience and competitor analysis, site structure, and wireframes. Responsive interface design using typography, color, and imagery. Evaluation for usability and accessibility, and production of a multi-page website using industry standard tools.

## Competencies

1. Analyze target audiences, user needs, competitor websites, and website objectives. (I)
2. Develop website structures using customer journeys, user flows, content strategies, sitemaps, and wireframes. (I, II)
3. Produce graphics and visual concepts for web publication using typography, color, and imagery. (III)
4. Design responsive website interfaces using visual design, navigation, and usability principles. (III, IV)
5. Evaluate website designs for usability, accessibility, and user experience. (IV, V)
6. Produce an interactive, multi-page website for publication using contemporary web design and publishing tools. (IV, V)

## Outline

- **I. Audience Research and User Experience:** target audiences and user needs; website goals and competitor research; customer journeys and user flows.
- **II. Website Planning and Structure:** site structure; content strategy and information architecture; sitemaps, card sorting, and wireframes; content collection and organization.
- **III. Visual Web Design:** design principles for web publication; effective use of semiotics, color, and typography; image resolution; mood boards and visual direction; responsive layouts and visual hierarchy.
- **IV. User Interface and Interaction Design:** effective presentation elements; interface elements and interactive prototyping; usability and accessibility.
- **V. Web Technologies, Tools, and Publication:** website design and publishing platforms; artificial intelligence (AI) in web design; search optimization; website analytics and performance; multi-page website production and publication; website testing, critique, and refinement.

## 1. Course design summary

### Design concept

The course teaches students to design websites that look right for a brand and work for the people who use them. It is built for four groups of students: Graphic Design degree students, Digital Media Arts students, Animation students, and Web Design certificate students. It assumes no prior web experience and no coding, so it works as a first-year course.

The weight sits on two things, in this order of emphasis:

1. **Visual design for the web.** Typography, color, imagery, semiotics, layout, responsive hierarchy, and a brand carried from a style tile onto every page and every screen size. A graphic design student should finish the course able to show how an identity system becomes a working website.
2. **Usability and accessibility evaluation.** Heuristic review, competitor analysis, two rounds of usability testing with real people, and WCAG 2.1 AA checks, so every design decision can be defended with evidence.

Coding is light and optional. Every student builds and publishes with Google Sites, which needs no code; students who want to code can take the code option and write HTML, CSS and small JavaScript that they place in their Sites pages (section 7).

The whole term is one project: a multi-page, responsive, published website for a **fictitious client**. Gemini plays the client. It generates the client persona and the brief, answers the student’s discovery-interview questions in character, and drafts site copy. Gemini writes; it does not design. Students do all visual design: logo or wordmark, type, color, layout, imagery, and interface.

The project moves through the five outline sections in order.

| Weeks | Outline section | What the student produces for the client |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | I. Audience Research and User Experience | Website teardown, client discovery and goals, proto-personas, competitor analysis, customer journey and user flows |
| 4 to 5 | II. Website Planning and Structure | Content strategy and inventory, card sort, sitemap, edited copy deck, low-fidelity wireframes |
| 6 to 8 | III. Visual Web Design | Mood boards, brand-to-web style tile, web graphics kit, responsive mockups, and the Website Design Proposal (Projects, 30 points) |
| 9 to 11 | IV. User Interface and Interaction Design | Interface components with states, interactive prototype, usability test round 1, accessibility review, first site build |
| 12 to 15 | V. Web Technologies, Tools, and Publication | Full site build, AI-assisted interactive element, search and performance pass, test round 2, refinement, published Final Website and Case Study (Projects, 30 points) |

Backward design (Wiggins and McTighe) drove the order: the Final Website and Case Study rubric was written first, with one criterion per competency, and every earlier step was placed where it builds a skill that rubric checks.

### How students use AI, and where they may not

The district provides Google Gemini and Adobe Creative Cloud; the course uses both first. AI is taught as a writing, research and production aid that students must check. Every week has at least one task where the student checks AI output for accuracy and accessibility.

| Gemini may | Gemini may not |
|---|---|
| Role-play the fictitious client, generate the client persona and brief, and answer discovery-interview questions in character | Generate images, logos, icons, mood board images, layouts, or any other visual design work |
| Summarize competitor sites the student has already reviewed, for the student to check against the sites | Invent research data, usability test participants, quotes, or test results |
| Draft proto-persona text, content strategy notes, site copy, microcopy, and brand voice options that the student edits | Write the student’s critique comments, design rationales, reflections, or defense answers |
| Draft alt text, page titles, and search descriptions that the student checks and rewrites | Stand in for the accessibility check itself (automated and manual checks are still required) |
| Write code for an interactive element or for code-option blocks embedded in Sites, from the student’s own design | Make a design decision the student cannot explain |

Every submission carries a short AI-use log (tool, purpose, what was kept, what was changed). Full rules are in section 11.

### Who the course serves

| Program | Why the course matters to it | What the course asks them to bring forward |
|---|---|---|
| Graphic Design degree | Brands live on screens; employers expect designers to carry an identity system to the web | Identity, type and color skills applied to responsive layouts, interface components, and accessible contrast |
| Digital Media Arts | Websites are the hub for every other medium the student makes | Imagery, video and motion placed on the web at the right size, with captions and reduced-motion options |
| Animation | Studios, festivals and clients judge animators by their web presence; motion is part of interface design | Motion and illustration used for interface feedback and storytelling, kept accessible |
| Web Design certificate | The course is the design and UX foundation for the certificate | The full design-to-publication process, with the code option inside Google Sites available |

Assignment examples on each page name examples from more than one of these programs. A graphic design student always sees the brand carried onto the web: the style tile in Week 6 turns the client identity into web type, color tokens, image style and buttons, and every later step is checked against it.

### Relationship to AVC2xx

AVC190 is the gateway into AVC2xx UX Design for Interactive Media. The two courses share vocabulary on purpose so students do not relearn terms.

| Shared with AVC2xx Weeks 1 to 3 | Kept in AVC2xx only |
|---|---|
| UX versus visual design; human-centered design | Research with real participants for discovery (research plans, consent materials, interview sessions, synthesis) |
| The ten usability heuristics and the 0 to 4 severity scale | A cross-disciplinary capstone in the student’s own medium |
| Perception, Gestalt grouping, visual hierarchy, cognitive load | Emerging technologies (AR, VR, voice, spatial audio) |
| Accessibility (WCAG 2.1 AA, POUR principles) and inclusive design | Recorded capstone defense of an original functional project |
| The critique protocol (I notice, Because, What if) | |

AVC190 applies the shared vocabulary to websites for a fictitious client. Its discovery research is a role-played client interview and proto-personas built from public sources, not research with participants; real people take part only in usability tests. Students who complete AVC190 follow the AVC2xx extension path in that course’s Weeks 1 to 3.

### Publishing decision

**Decision: every student builds and publishes on Google Sites. Students who want to code (Web Design certificate students and anyone with an interest in HTML and CSS) can take the code option: they write HTML, CSS and small JavaScript, with code help from Gemini Canvas, and place it in their Sites pages with the Sites Embed tool (Embed code), or export a Gemini Canvas app into Sites. The code option is chosen in Week 9 with instructor approval.** The full comparison is in section 7.

Reasons for Google Sites:

1. **It keeps the grade on design and usability.** Students spend their build weeks on layout, hierarchy, content and testing, not debugging. The competencies ask students to produce a multi-page website with contemporary tools; they do not require code.
2. **It gives enough design control for a design course.** Custom themes allow a heading font and a body font chosen from a large font library, custom color palettes for sections, links and buttons, a logo, a favicon, and per-style size and spacing (source: [Steegle custom themes guide](https://www.steegle.com/google-sites/how-to/custom-themes), a third-party guide; confirm against [Google’s Sites Help](https://support.google.com/sites/answer/6372878) [verify]).
3. **It is district-provided and on for student accounts,** so nothing is bought and nothing new is installed.
4. **It is responsive by default.** Students still design phone, tablet and desktop layouts in Figma and then check that the build holds up at each width.
5. **It meets the interactivity requirement.** Interactive apps built in Gemini Canvas export directly into a Google Sites draft as a working page ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/publish-interactive-apps-from-gemini-app-to-Google-Sites.html)), and Sites has built-in interactive elements (navigation, buttons, collapsible text, image carousels, embedded maps and forms).
6. **It supports analytics.** Sites accepts a Google Analytics measurement ID ([Use Analytics with your site](https://support.google.com/sites/answer/97459)), if the district allows it on student sites [verify].

Limits the team should know about: layouts come from Sites section templates, navigation text uses the body text style, Sites may replace low-contrast theme colors with black or white, search settings are limited (for example, per-page search descriptions [verify]), and whether a student site can be published publicly outside the district domain depends on district sharing policy [verify]. The code option exists for students who want more control or who are in the Web Design certificate; their coded blocks live inside the same Google Sites site, the work is graded with the same rubrics, and code quality is not graded beyond accessibility and responsiveness.

Considered and not chosen: **Figma Sites** (publishes directly from Figma, but early accessibility criticism of its output and unconfirmed availability on the education plan; worth revisiting; [Figma accessibility help](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/35895955843735-Figma-Sites-collection-Make-your-Figma-Site-accessible)); **Adobe Express pages** (single page, not a multi-page site [verify]); **Adobe Portfolio** (included with Creative Cloud, but built around portfolio templates, not client sites [verify]); **WordPress, Webflow, Squarespace** (accounts or cost, and more setup than a first-year course can absorb); **Adobe XD** (discontinued; not used).

## 4. Deadline rhythm, calendar conventions, feedback and interaction

### Deadline rhythm

All times are **noon, Arizona time** (Arizona does not observe daylight saving time, so the time never shifts). Every deadline has three moments.

| Moment | Thursday deadline | Sunday deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Post the work to the Discord critique channel | Wednesday, 12:00 p.m. | Saturday, 12:00 p.m. |
| Deadline: submit to Canvas (work, process evidence, AI-use log, and three critique comments on classmates) | Thursday, 12:00 p.m. | Sunday, 12:00 p.m. |
| Hard close: work in the 24-hour window loses 10%; nothing is accepted after | Friday, 12:00 p.m. | Monday, 12:00 p.m. |

- Week 1 has one deadline (Thursday).
- Exercises (E items) are due at the same noon deadline as the week’s Process Work and follow the same late window, but they are not posted to Discord unless the page says so.
- Students are encouraged to comment on classmates who post late during the 24-hour window. This is ungraded “good karma” community citizenship and is never extra credit.
- Discord is the critique space. Screen names are allowed; students tell the instructor their screen name in the Start Here acknowledgement so critique can be credited.
- Every assignment page states all three times in a date box with a purple border (red at GCC export).

### Calendar conventions

- The course uses **week numbers only**. No calendar dates appear in this design or on Canvas pages until a term is chosen.
- Weeks are instructional weeks. A break week (for example spring break) is not numbered; the schedule resumes with the next week number after the break.
- Nothing is due on a holiday or during a break. If a noon deadline, Discord post time, or hard close falls on a college holiday, all three moments for that deadline shift one day later, and the shift is announced in Canvas at least one week ahead. Students are never asked to work through a closure.
- A week that loses a weekday to a holiday keeps its two deadlines but carries the lighter of its two Process Work steps; the instructor checks this when the term is set.

### Feedback turnaround

| Item | Instructor feedback returned |
|---|---|
| Process Work (Thursday items) | By the following Tuesday at noon |
| Process Work (Sunday items) | By the following Friday at noon |
| PW2 Client Discovery and Website Goals (Week 2) | Within 48 hours, so the proto-personas build on approved goals |

### Regular and Substantive Interaction plan

The instructor initiates interaction on a predictable schedule; it is not left to students to ask.

| Interaction | Frequency | Substantive because |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly announcement (text plus short video) | Every week, Monday afternoon | Previews the week, names common patterns from the last critique round, shows strong examples from student work (with permission) |

### How the defense is graded

The defense confirms that the student authored and understands the work (authentic assessment). It is graded inside the Final Website rubric, not as a separate performance score.

1. **Week 15, Wednesday noon:** the student posts the recorded client presentation (6 to 8 minutes, captioned) to Discord.
2. **Week 15, Thursday noon:** each student posts one defense question on three classmates’ presentations (this is the critique requirement for PW27). The instructor adds one question to every presentation.
3. **Week 15, Saturday noon:** the student posts defense responses, as a recorded video (2 to 4 minutes) or a written response, answering every question received.
4. **Week 15, Sunday noon:** the student submits the Final Website and Case Study in Canvas.

The instructor reviews the live site, the case study, the presentation and the defense together. A defense that answers with evidence and names trade-offs lifts criteria 4, 5 and 6; a defense that cannot explain a design decision or how the interactive element works, or that contradicts the case study, caps criterion 6 at Developing and triggers a short follow-up conversation before the grade is final. Unanswered questions lower criterion 6 by one level.

### Critique protocol (used every week)

Every critique comment has three parts and names at least one principle. This is the same protocol used in AVC2xx.

1. **I notice:** describe what you saw or did, in neutral terms. (“On the phone layout, the Order button sits below three paragraphs of text.”)
2. **Because:** name the principle or evidence. (“Visual hierarchy: the primary action should be the most visible element on a page whose goal is ordering.”)
3. **What if:** offer one specific, testable suggestion. (“What if the button moved above the paragraphs and used the accent color from your style tile?”)

Rules: critique the work, not the person; no “looks good” or “nice” without the three parts; comment on a classmate whose client type differs from yours at least once per week; disagree with evidence, not volume. The instructor models the protocol in Week 1 and comments in Discord throughout.

### The fictitious client

Students use the client prompt kit [instructor to create] in Gemini to generate their client. Each student’s client is unique; two students may pick the same client type, but Gemini generates different businesses, people and goals.

| Client type (examples) | Sample interactive element |
|---|---|
| Neighborhood bakery with custom cake orders | Cake size and flavor estimator |
| Mobile dog grooming service | Price estimator by breed size and service |
| Community climbing gym | Beginner class finder by age group and experience |
| Independent bookstore with author events | Event filter by month and genre |
| Small music venue | Show listing filter by genre and age policy |
| Plant nursery | “Which plant fits my light?” quiz |
| Animation or illustration studio for hire | Project-type selector that recommends a service package |
| Food truck with a weekly route | “Where are we this week?” schedule finder |

Client rules:

1. **Fictitious only.** No real business names, real people, or real logos. If Gemini produces a name that matches a real local business, the student renames the client.
2. **Gemini writes, the student designs.** Gemini may generate the client persona, the brief, interview answers, brand voice and draft copy. The student creates every visual: wordmark or logo, palette, type choices, layouts, interface and image treatment.
3. **Imagery.** Students use their own photography or illustration, or images with a license that allows reuse (for example [Openverse](https://openverse.org/) [verify], Unsplash [verify license terms], Pexels [verify license terms]), and log every source. No AI-generated imagery for design work.
4. **Regenerate once.** If the brief is unusable (no clear audience, a site that would need payments or logins), the student may regenerate it once in Week 2 and logs why.
5. **Bringing a brand.** A Graphic Design or Digital Media Arts student may use a fictitious brand they designed in another course, with instructor approval in Week 2; the web extension of that brand is new work in this course.

### Scope guardrails

- **Five to seven pages.** Home, About, a services or products page, the interactive element page, Visit or Contact, and one or two more that the sitemap justifies.
- **One interactive element beyond navigation,** with a scope that a first-time visitor can use in under two minutes.
- **No real transactions or personal data.** No payments, logins or accounts. A contact or order form may appear as a demonstration only; it must say it is a class project and must not collect real personal information.
- **Accessibility statement** on every site (a page or a footer section) that names the standard (WCAG 2.1 AA), what was tested, and known limits.
- **Design freeze at the end of Week 13.** Week 14 is for fixes, the case study, and the presentation.
- **Motion is optional** and must respect reduced-motion settings or offer a pause; nothing flashes more than three times a second.

### What “interactive” and “multi-page” mean

| Requirement | Meets it | Does not meet it |
|---|---|---|
| Multi-page | At least five published pages with consistent navigation and a shared visual system | One long page with anchor links; a Figma prototype only |
| Interactive | Navigation plus one element that responds to the visitor’s input (estimator, filter, quiz, finder) and works with a keyboard | An embedded video alone; a carousel alone; an element that only works with a mouse |
| For publication | A live URL that the instructor and classmates can open (or a district-only URL if district policy requires [verify]) | Screenshots of an unpublished draft |

### The case study

The case study is the portfolio piece. It is written for a hiring manager, 800 to 1,200 words or an equivalent web page or slide document, and includes: the client and the problem, audiences and objectives, competitor findings, structure (sitemap and flows), the visual system (style tile and before and after mockups), test findings with severity, what changed, how AI was used and checked, the accessibility statement, and what the student would do next.

4. Design responsive website interfaces using visual design, navigation, and usability principles.

<h2 style="background-color: #7a5080; color: #FFFFFF; padding: 12px 16px;">Week 1: What a Website Does: Design, Interface and Experience</h2>

## 11. Accessibility, UDL, usability testing ethics and consent, and AI-use disclosure

### Course accessibility (WCAG 2.1 AA, OSCQR)

- Every instructor video is captioned and has a transcript. Every image on Canvas pages has alternative text; decorative images are marked decorative.
- Title boxes and date boxes meet WCAG 2.1 AA contrast: white on `#7a5080` is about 6.4 to 1 in the public build, and white on `#CB3338` is about 5.2 to 1 at GCC export (calculated at build; confirm with the [WebAIM Contrast Checker](https://webaim.org/resources/contrastchecker/)).
- Headings follow a logical order (h2 title box, h3 sections). Tables have header rows. Links describe their destination.
- Discord is the critique space, but nothing graded happens only in Discord: every post is also submitted in Canvas, and a student who cannot use Discord may post critique in a Canvas discussion mirror [instructor to create] with no penalty.
- Students using assistive technology are not required to use any tool that does not work with it; the instructor approves an equivalent (for example Penpot or a structured Google Doc in place of a Figma task).
- Student work is held to the same standard: every student website meets WCAG 2.1 AA as far as the platform allows, carries an accessibility statement, and documents known limits (for example an embedded element that a screen reader cannot fully use, with its text alternative).

### Universal Design for Learning ([CAST UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/) [verify license])

| UDL principle | How the course applies it |
|---|---|
| Multiple means of engagement | Students choose their client type, visual direction, and whether to take the code option; a Graphic Design or Digital Media Arts student may extend a brand of their own; the rhythm is predictable every week |
| Multiple means of representation | Every module has an instructor video on one demonstration client, text readings, and examples across programs; every visual concept is shown and explained in words |
| Multiple means of action and expression | Written or recorded options for the teardown, proposal, presentation, reflection and defense responses; Sites-only and code-option builds graded by one rubric |

### Usability testing ethics and consent

The course includes card sorts (Week 4) and usability tests (Weeks 10 and 13) with real people. These are classroom learning activities, not research intended to produce generalizable knowledge. The instructor confirms with the district’s institutional review process whether any approval or exemption is needed before the course runs [verify with district IRB]. Until confirmed, these rules apply.

1. **Adults only.** Participants are 18 or older. Students do not recruit minors, including younger siblings or classmates under 18.
2. **Mostly people outside the course.** At most one participant per round may be a classmate in this course, so testing reflects first-time visitors.
3. **Informed consent every time.** Before each session the student reads the course consent script: purpose, what is recorded, how notes are de-identified, how long notes are kept, that participation is voluntary, and that the participant may stop at any time. Consent is written or recorded verbally.
4. **Minimal data.** No health, financial, immigration or other sensitive information. No identifying details in notes; participants are P1, P2, P3.
5. **Recordings.** Only with explicit consent, stored only in the student’s district Google account, never posted to Discord or Canvas, and deleted at the end of the course. Clips in a presentation need separate written consent and must not show faces or names.
6. **No disclosure of disability required.** Students never ask participants to disclose a disability or diagnosis. Students who want to test with people who use assistive technology discuss the plan with the instructor first.
7. **AI and participant data.** De-identified notes may be organized in Gemini Notebook under the district account. Identifiable participant data never goes into any AI tool. AI is never used to generate participants, quotes, or results.
8. **Honesty.** Reporting a session that did not happen, or inventing data, is academic misconduct.

### AI-use disclosure rules

1. **Log every use.** Each submission includes an AI-use log: tool, what you asked it to do, what you kept, what you changed, and why. “None” is a valid entry.
2. **Gemini writes; you design.** Gemini may generate the fictitious client, draft copy, alt text, titles and code. You create every visual: logo or wordmark, type, color, layout, imagery, and interface. No AI-generated imagery for design work.
3. **Check everything.** Every AI output that reaches the client site is checked for accuracy (fabricated facts, wrong prices, broken logic) and accessibility (alt text quality, labels, keyboard use, reading level). The log shows the check.
4. **Never a substitute for your thinking.** AI may not write your critique comments, reflections, design rationales, or defense answers, and may not stand in for test participants.
5. **AI-written code is allowed and disclosed.** An interactive element or embedded code block built with Gemini Canvas counts as your work when the design and decisions are yours, the log shows your prompts and revisions, and you can explain how it works in your defense.
6. **Fictitious means fictitious.** Do not ask Gemini to imitate a real business, a real person, or a living designer’s identifiable style.
7. **Use district tools for course data.** Use Gemini and Adobe under district accounts first. If you use another AI tool, never paste participant data or classmates’ work into it.
8. **Undisclosed AI use** is handled under the college academic integrity policy. When the instructor has a question about authorship, the first step is a conversation, not an accusation; the defense format makes that conversation natural.
