AI Primer

This course uses Google Gemini in assignments. The rules for AI use and the AI-use log are in the AI section of the syllabus. Google’s own wording on how your Maricopa account protects your work: Gemini for Education and Google’s education FAQ.

AI Primer (10 points, Process Work)

What this is. Your first time using Gemini in this course, and it takes about an hour. No experience with AI is needed. Complete Technology Setup first.

What Gemini is. Gemini is an AI chat tool from Google. You type a request, called a prompt, and it writes an answer. It learned from an enormous amount of text written by people, so it can sound knowledgeable about almost anything. It does not know you, and it has never been where you have been.

Why this assignment. Some assignments in this course use Gemini for research and feedback. Before you use it for that, you will see for yourself what it does well, where it falls short, and what you bring that it cannot.

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account, so what you enter is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models. See AI in This Course for Google’s exact wording.

This assignment measures Module Objective 0.2, which supports Course Competency 3.

Part 1: Sign in

  1. Open Chrome and go to gemini.google.com.
  2. If you see Sign in in the top right corner, click it. Sign in with your Maricopa account: your MEID followed by @maricopa.edu, then your MEID password on the Maricopa sign-in page.
  3. Check the top right corner. It should show your Maricopa account. If it shows a personal Gmail account, click the picture and switch to your Maricopa account.

If Gemini does not open or says it is not available for your account (this can happen for students under 18), message your instructor in Canvas Inbox. You will receive an equal option with the same grade.

Part 2: Ask the question

  1. Click the box at the bottom that says Ask Gemini.
  2. Type Do I need to learn AI to get a good job in the digital media industry? and press Enter.
  3. Copy your prompt and Gemini’s answer into a Google Doc.

Part 3: Read it like a critic

  1. In your Doc, mark:
    • one point you agree with;
    • one point that sounds generic, overstated or like a sales pitch;
    • one question you would rather ask a working professional in the field.
  2. Gemini is made by Google, a company that sells AI. Write one sentence on how that might shape its answer.

Part 4: Give it better directions

A stronger prompt tells Gemini four things: a role (who it should act as), a task (what to do), context (who it is for and why) and a format (how the answer should look). Example: You are a hiring manager at a small design studio in Phoenix. List the skills you look for in a new junior designer, and say honestly where AI skills rank among them. I am a first-year community college student. Use a short ranked list.

  1. Rewrite your prompt using all four parts, and add one detail about yourself: the kind of work you want to do. Press Enter.
  2. Copy the new prompt and answer into your Doc. Write one sentence on how the answer changed.

Part 5: Reflect

  1. In four to six sentences: Did either answer change how you think about AI and your career? Where might a tool like this help you in your own work, and where would you not want to use it?

Part 6: Start your AI-use log

  1. At the bottom of your Doc, add a table with three columns: Tool, What I asked, and What I kept, changed or rejected. Add one row for this assignment. Every assignment in this course has a row like this, so your instructor can see how you used AI and what was your own.

Submit the link to your Google Doc.

Grading (10 points): critic’s mark-up 3; revised prompt with your own detail 3; reflection 2; AI-use log row 2.

Module 1

Word/Meaning

Weeks 2 to 4 · 18.5 hours
Instructor notes
  • By Part 3 every student needs Illustrator or InDesign running. Message anyone with no vector work by the Part 3 Discord post.
  • Part 1 is handwritten on purpose. Return typed lists kindly and allow a resubmission inside the late window.

Video: Word/Meaning walkthrough (about 4 minutes)

  • The constraints: type only, black and white, lowercase sans serif, square, no distortion.
  • Show a strong thumbnail sheet: range of size and placement, positive and negative space.
  • Common mistakes: nouns instead of verbs, stretched letters, added images.

Objectives

  • 1.1 Classify the anatomy of letterforms in lettering you photograph, using the vocabulary of the reading (Course Competency 2)
  • 1.2 Generate 50 descriptive verbs and at least 20 hand thumbnails for each of five chosen words within fixed constraints (Course Competency 4)
  • 1.3 Construct five vector designs that express meaning through placement, scale, and positive and negative space (Course Competency 4)
  • 1.4 Revise your designs in response to peer critique and justify each final design in one sentence that names the principle it uses (Course Competency 6)
  • 1.5 Summarize one argument from Helvetica and explain your own position on it, citing where it appears in the film and a typeface you photograph in your own surroundings (Course Competency 1)

Overview

Make five words look like what they mean, using only type: no color, no pictures. Working with type alone shows how placement and scale carry meaning. You move from 50 handwritten verbs, to 100 thumbnails (small, quick sketches by hand), to vector designs, to a final. Vector art is built from points and lines, so it stays sharp at any size. You also watch the documentary Helvetica. Its big idea: every typeface takes a point of view.

Watch

Captioned videos from your instructor:

  • Word/Meaning walkthrough: the constraints, a strong thumbnail sheet, common mistakes (4 minutes)

Read

  • Typography Handbook (open textbook), anatomy and classification chapters
  • Google Fonts Knowledge, “Introducing type” lessons

Practice

  • Play Kern Type (about 15 minutes, on a computer). Kerning is adjusting the space between two letters. Screenshot your score for yourself. Not graded.

Assessment

Helvetica Reflection (10 points, Exercises)

This assignment measures Module Objective 1.5, which supports Course Competency 1.

How to watch the film:

  1. Open Helvetica on Kanopy, through the GCC Library.
  2. Select Watch Now, then Log into GCCAZ, and sign in with your MEID (your Maricopa student ID and login) and password.
  3. The film is about 80 minutes long. Turn on captions in the player if you want them.

What to submit:

  1. A photograph you take of Helvetica, or a typeface used in its place, where you live or work: a sign, a package, a storefront, a form. Add alt text.
  2. 100 words, plus or minus 10, typed into the text box. Name one argument someone in the film makes about Helvetica and about when it comes up. Say whether you agree, and connect your view to your photograph.

Do not summarize the film. You may disagree with a famous designer, but explain why.

AI: No AI use. Your photograph and your position are the evidence. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (10 points): names one specific argument and where it appears in the film 3; states a position and supports it 3; position is tied to your own photograph, with alt text 3; 100 words, plus or minus 10, complete sentences 1.

Reading: Letter and Type Anatomy (5 points, Exercises)

This assignment measures Module Objective 1.1, which supports Course Competency 2.

Read:

  • Lupton, E. (2010). Thinking with type (2nd ed.). Princeton Architectural Press. Read the Introduction, the type anatomy diagram (p. 32) and pp. 40 to 94. The book is on reserve at the GCC Library.
  • Free alternative: Typography Handbook (open textbook, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), the chapters on anatomy and classification.

Submit:

  2. On the photograph, use arrows to label three parts of the letters from p. 32 (for example, x-height, counter, serif). Add alt text.
  3. Two or three sentences: what do these letter parts tell you about the lettering’s purpose?

AI: No AI use. The photograph and labels are your own. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (5 points): own photograph with three correctly labeled anatomy terms 3; sentences connect the anatomy to the lettering’s purpose 2.

Word/Meaning Part 1: Fifty Verbs (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objective 1.2, which supports Course Competency 4.

  1. By hand, on paper, list 50 descriptive verbs (action words, such as shrink or bounce). Do not use nouns. You are not drawing an object.
  2. Circle your five favorites: the words you can show best with type.
  3. Under each of the five, write one sentence on the visual idea you have for it.
  4. Photograph the page flat, full frame, in even light.

AI: No AI use. The handwritten list is the evidence. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (10 points): fifty descriptive verbs, handwritten, no nouns 4; five chosen, each with a sentence on its visual idea 4; Discord post with alt text, three principle-based comments, compiled in Miro 2.

Word/Meaning Part 2: Thumbnails (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objective 1.2, which supports Course Competency 4.

Do not submit this part until you have feedback on Part 1.

  1. Create at least 20 thumbnail sketches of each word, 100 in all, on paper or a drawing tablet. Do not use computer type.
  2. Make each thumbnail about 2 by 2 inches, several on a page. The final is square, so sketch square.
  3. Draw black lowercase sans serif letters (letters without small feet) on white. Change size and placement. Use both the letters (positive space) and the empty space around them (negative space).

You may repeat a letter, use a capital if needed, and let letters overlap or run off the page. You may not use color, stretch or distort type, add backgrounds or images, or break letters apart.

AI: No AI use. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (10 points): at least 20 thumbnails per word, 100 total 4; by hand, lowercase sans serif, square 2; constraints held: no color, no distortion, no extra elements 2; Discord post with alt text, three principle-based comments, compiled in Miro 2.

Word/Meaning Part 3: Vector Designs (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objective 1.3, which supports Course Competency 4.

Do not submit this part until you have feedback on Part 2.

  1. Use Illustrator or InDesign, not Photoshop, so the type stays vector. Use Futura or another plain sans serif such as Helvetica Bold.
  2. Choose your strongest sketch for each word. Set each word on its own 8 by 8 inch page.
  3. Export each as PNG or JPG and place all five on one page.

AI: No AI use. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (10 points): five 8 by 8 vector designs, one per word, sharp 4; lowercase sans serif, black and white, no distortion 2; meaning carried by placement and scale 2; Discord post with alt text, three principle-based comments, compiled in Miro 2.

Word/Meaning Part 4: Final Designs (30 points, Projects)

This assignment measures Module Objectives 1.3 and 1.4, which support Course Competencies 4 and 6.

Do not submit this part until you have feedback on Part 3.

  1. Revise all five designs based on your feedback. Use vector type in black and white, lowercase unless one or two letters need a capital.
  2. Place all five on one Miro board with the 8 by 8 page outline showing.
  3. Under each design, write one sentence that names the design principle it uses (for example, “Scale contrast makes shrink read as diminishing”).
  4. Export one PDF, 8 by 8 inches, one design per page, High Quality Print.

AI: No AI use for the designs. Log “No AI used,” or log any Gemini feedback you asked for on your sentences.

Module 2

Recipe: Grids and Hierarchy

Weeks 4 to 7 · 21 hours
Instructor notes
  • After Step 3, run a short anonymous mid-semester check-in (a Canvas survey: what is working, what is not, one change).
  • Step 4 is the first image step: check sources and licenses before grading the design.

Video: The 12-column grid in InDesign (about 5 minutes)

  • Set up the 8 by 8 inch document with 12 column guides.
  • Save it as a template to reuse for every step.

Video: Placing versus pasting (about 4 minutes)

  • File, Place versus paste; read effective resolution in the Links panel (300 ppi).
  • RGB for screens, CMYK for print: how to tell which one an image is.

Video: Exporting the eight-page print PDF (about 4 minutes)

  • High Quality Print preset, eight 8 by 8 pages in the required order.
  • Color only in the image, never the type.

Objectives

  • 2.1 Explain three ways to create typographic hierarchy by setting one line of your own recipe three ways (Course Competency 2)
  • 2.2 Use InDesign to set a recipe on a 12-column grid and to place an outside image at correct effective resolution and color mode (Course Competency 3)
  • 2.3 Apply one hierarchy variable at a time (leading, kerning, weight, case, scale) and explain how each changes what reads first (Course Competency 2)
  • 2.4 Produce an eight-page, print-ready PDF that revises every design, after ranking all eight against the hierarchy goal (Course Competency 5)

Overview

One recipe, eight designs, a 12-column grid and one typeface. A grid is a set of columns that lines up everything on the page. Hierarchy is the order in which a reader notices things. Each step adds one more hierarchy tool: placement, then weight and case, then scale, then an image. You also learn to place images correctly for print.

Practice

Assessment

Reading: Hierarchy and the Grid (5 points, Exercises)

This assignment measures Module Objective 2.1, which supports Course Competency 2.

Read:

  • Lupton, E. (2010). Thinking with type (2nd ed.). Princeton Architectural Press. Read pp. 103 to 171. Look closely at the kerning, tracking (space across a whole word or line) and line-spacing examples.
  • Free alternative: Typography Handbook (open textbook, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), the chapters on spacing and hierarchy; and Baseline, Grid Systems.

Submit:

Choose one line from the recipe you will use in this project. Set it three times in InDesign. Each time, create hierarchy a different way (for example, placement, leading or weight). Leading is the space between lines. Export one PNG with all three versions. Label each with the method and one sentence on what now reads first.

AI: No AI use. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (5 points): three versions, each using a different hierarchy method 3; each labeled with what reads first and why 2.

Recipe Step 1: Placement Only (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objectives 2.2 and 2.3, which support Course Competencies 2 and 3.

  1. Find a recipe with words you can play with in type (“stir,” “creamy,” “upside down”). Edit it to 50 to 100 words with a title, ingredients and method.
  2. In InDesign, create an 8 by 8 inch document with 12 columns. Set the recipe in Helvetica 12 point (or another plain sans serif).
  3. Build hierarchy with placement, leading and kerning only. No bold, no italic, no size change. The title reads first. You may repeat words.
  4. Black type on white. Never stretch type.
  5. Make two different designs.

AI: Optional: Gemini may help edit the recipe text. Log what you asked and what you kept. The layouts are yours.

Grading (10 points): Recipe edited to 50 to 100 words with title, ingredients and method 2; 8 by 8 document with a 12-column grid in use 2; hierarchy from placement, leading and kerning only 3; two distinct designs 1; Discord post with alt text, three principle-based comments, compiled in Miro 2.

Recipe Step 2: Weight and Case (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objective 2.3, which supports Course Competency 2.

  1. Start a fresh page with the same grid and 8 by 8 format.
  2. Use bold, italic and all caps on purpose to build hierarchy. Do not change type size.
  3. Make two designs that look nothing like Step 1.

AI: No AI use. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (10 points): fresh page, same grid and format 2; weight and case used deliberately for hierarchy 3; size held at 12 point 1; two designs distinct from Step 1 2; Discord post with alt text, three principle-based comments, compiled in Miro 2.

Recipe Step 3: Scale (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objective 2.3, which supports Course Competency 2.

  1. Same 8 by 8 page, same grid, same typeface. Add scale.
  2. Black type on white, never stretched.
  3. Make two designs that look nothing like the first four.

AI: No AI use. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (10 points): scale used purposefully for hierarchy 3; grid, format and typeface held 2; two designs distinct from all four earlier 2; type never stretched 1; Discord post with alt text, three principle-based comments, compiled in Miro 2.

Recipe Step 4: Image in Negative Space (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objectives 2.2 and 2.3, which support Course Competencies 2 and 3.

  1. Find an image you have the right to use, from Wikimedia Commons, Library of Congress Free to Use or your own photograph. Record its source and license.
  2. Place it in InDesign (File, Place). Do not paste it. Check its effective resolution in the Links panel: you need 300 ppi for print. Effective resolution is the image’s real detail at the size you placed it. Note whether it is RGB (for screens) or CMYK (for print).
  3. Use the same page, grid and typeface. You may use scale, weight and case. Put the image in the negative space.
  4. Make two designs that look nothing like the other six.

AI: No AI-generated images. Log “No AI used.”

Grading (10 points): image in negative space, not dominating 3; placed, not pasted, at adequate effective resolution; source and license given 3; two designs distinct from all six earlier 1; type never stretched; hierarchy holds 1; Discord post with alt text, three principle-based comments, compiled in Miro 2.

Recipe Step 5: Final (30 points, Projects)

This assignment measures Module Objectives 2.3 and 2.4, which support Course Competencies 2 and 5.

  1. Rank your eight designs against the hierarchy goal: does the title read first, and do the descriptive words carry meaning? Write the ranking as a short list with one sentence per design.
  2. Revise all eight based on your ranking and the critique. Never stretch or distort type.
  3. Submit one PDF, High Quality Print, eight 8 by 8 pages, in order: pages 1 and 2 placement only; 3 and 4 weight and case; 5 and 6 scale; 7 and 8 image (color only in the image, never the type).

AI: No AI use for the designs or the ranking. Log “No AI used.”

First keep the content: put the site’s text in a Google Doc and its images in your course folder, then open each published page and save it as a PDF (File, then Print, then Save as PDF). Then try a direct copy: in the site editor, choose Share and add your personal email as an editor. Sign in with your personal account, open the site, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing outside Maricopa is blocked, rebuild the site in your personal account at sites.google.com with the same content and publish it again. The web address changes, so update it on your resume and profiles.
  5. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • If you already have a Behance portfolio, it uses your school Adobe ID. Before your school account closes, move your Behance profile to a free personal Adobe account.
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
