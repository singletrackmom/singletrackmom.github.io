How this course works

AVC 248 is the capstone career course for graduating Digital Media Arts, Animation, Photography, Graphic Design and Fine Arts students. It is fully online and 3 credits. There are no required meeting times, and you never need to come to campus. Plan on about 6.5 to 9.5 hours a week for 15 weeks plus finals week.

You will make a resume (four drafts and a final), a cover letter, an identity package with a business card and leave-behind, an updated portfolio, a LinkedIn profile, recorded interview practice, and a career agent in Gemini that keeps working after the course ends. A leave-behind is a small printed or digital piece you give an employer so they remember you.

Every module has the same parts: Objectives, Overview, Watch, Read, Practice, Assessment and Critique. Each graded item names the objective it measures. Modules open at least one week before their first deadline. Optional Zoom check-ins are announced in Canvas and Discord, and each one is recorded and captioned.

This course expects you to already have work to build a portfolio from. Not sure it fits your program plan? Talk with Academic Advisement before the first deadline.

Accessibility of your work

Everything you make must work for people who cannot see it or hear it. Media rubrics give about 10% of the points to this. Alt text is a short written description of an image for people who use screen readers.

  • Video and audio: automatic captions (YouTube Studio, Premiere Pro Speech to Text, Google Vids or Meet), then every line fixed by hand. Audio gets a corrected transcript.
  • Images: alt text on every image that matters, including on Discord. Write one or two sentences on what matters. Never start with “image of.”
  • Documents: real heading styles, readable contrast, alt text on every image or logo, and a tagged PDF.
  • Portfolio and Google Sites pages: alt text, captions on video, headings in order, link text that says where it goes, and a WAVE check with every error fixed.
  • Outside tools: Discord (privacy, accessibility); Adobe (privacy, accessibility conformance reports); Google Workspace for Education (accessibility); Canvas (privacy, accessibility). If a tool does not work with your assistive technology, contact your instructor and Disability Resources and Services.

AI in This Course

This course uses generative AI (Google Gemini) in assignments.

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account. That is Gemini for Education, the educational version of Gemini. Google states that what you enter there is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models (Gemini for Education; Google’s education FAQ). Your artwork and writing are not used to teach the AI. A personal Google account is different: depending on its settings, what you enter there may be used to improve Google’s AI. That is why every assignment uses your Maricopa account.

Industry expects it. In the 2024 Microsoft and LinkedIn Work Trend Index, 66% of business leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills, and 71% said they would rather hire a less experienced candidate with AI skills than a more experienced candidate without them (Work Trend Index). Our industry advisory board recommends that we teach these tools.

  • Using AI here is not cheating. Cheating is turning in work you did not do as if you did. Here, AI use is assigned and recorded in your AI-use log, and you are graded on your own thinking, research, choices and design work. Other courses may have different rules.
  • Privacy. Your instructor never puts your work into a public AI tool. Never enter personal information, like your student ID or phone number, into any AI tool.
  • AI does not make your work. Gemini helps with research, writing, feedback and planning. It never designs. Every mark, layout, color and type choice is yours, and you show your process.
  • You write first. Your first resume draft and your Personal Goals Statement notes are your own. Later drafts may use Gemini feedback, with every kept change tracked and logged. Never submit a first AI draft.
  • You will learn to question it. AI makes confident mistakes and repeats bias from its training data. It is trained on artists’ work, often without permission, and it uses a lot of energy (International Energy Agency, Energy and AI). You learn to check every answer against a source you open yourself.
  • Tools: the Gemini app (the AI Primer, feedback on drafts, company research, the Post-Graduation Action Plan, the career agent); Gemini Live (the AI Mock Interview, where Gemini plays the hiring manager); Gemini Notebook (work from your own sources); and Gemini skills, scheduled actions and Gemini Canvas (the career agent in Module 8).
  • The AI-use log: for each assignment, one row with the tool, what you asked, and what you kept, changed or rejected. “No AI used” is a complete entry.
  • Students under 18: some Gemini features are blocked on your account (Google, Gemini feature access by age). No grade depends on a blocked feature. Type your answers instead of using Gemini Live for the AI Mock Interview. Search job boards and library databases yourself instead of using Deep Research.

It keeps working for you after the course ends. Avery, the student in the worked example, is fictional, and so is every studio on that page.

Assessment

Step 1: Set Your Agent’s Goal (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objective 8.1, which supports Course Competency 1.

  1. Choose one goal type from the table. You may combine two (for example, freelance work while you job search), but name which one comes first.
  2. Write your goal in one or two sentences that include what, for whom, where, and by when. Example: “Find entry-level motion design jobs in the Phoenix area or remote that I can land by August, at studios that make work for brands.”
  3. Write three must-haves: things every result has to have, or the agent drops it. Example: entry level; paid; Phoenix or remote.
  4. Write two to four deal-breakers: things that remove a result right away. Example: unpaid; requires a bachelor’s degree; more than 25 percent travel.
  5. Find two real examples of what you want (two job postings, two freelance calls, two courses, two organizations) and explain in two sentences how each one fits your goal.
  6. Name the five parts of your agent (goal, where it looks, rules, what it reports, when it runs) in one line each, using What Is an AI Agent?
  7. Read the example goal in Example Agent: Avery’s Animation Job Agent to compare your level of detail.

Submit: One Google Doc with your goal type, goal statement, the five parts, must-haves, deal-breakers and two examples. Post it to the class Discord the day before the deadline and comment on three classmates’ goals: is the goal specific enough for an agent to act on?

Rubric (10): goal states what, for whom, where and by when 2; the five parts of the agent named for the chosen goal 1; must-haves and deal-breakers are specific enough for an agent to apply 3; two real examples, each tied to the goal 2; three critique comments on classmates’ goals 1; accessibility: heading styles in the Doc and alt text on every image 1.

Step 2: Plan Your Agent on Paper (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objective 8.2, which supports Course Competency 5.

Work through these in order. Each one becomes a section of your rules file, the single document your agent will read every time it runs. Open Avery’s Animation Job Agent beside you and match its level of detail.

  1. Brainstorm by hand. Spend 20 minutes on paper or a whiteboard listing everything you want the agent to find, every place it could look, and every reason you would throw a result away. Photograph the page.
  2. Your starting point. Paste your current resume (or a skills and portfolio summary) at the bottom of the file. The agent rates every result against it.
  3. Goal and must-haves. Copy your goal, three must-haves and deal-breakers from Step 1.
  4. Rating. Write what a 5-star, 4-star and 3-star result means for your goal. Only 4 and 5 stars reach the report.
  5. Your top 20. Research the twenty organizations that matter most for your goal (studios, agencies, clients, schools, programs). For each, find its own careers or opportunities page, record the exact link, and add one note (city, remote policy, what it makes). Sort them into Tier 1 (check hardest), Tier 2 (check every run) and Tier 3 (never search, with the reason). Gemini may help you brainstorm names; you confirm every organization and link is real.
  6. Boards and sources. List three to five job boards, freelance boards, calendars or catalogs your field actually uses, for discovery only.
  7. Titles and keywords. List every name the thing you want goes by (for example, Junior Animator, Associate Animator, Motion Designer).
  8. Location and ranking. Write the order results appear in: which locations first, then what breaks a tie.
  9. What gets cut. List the rules that remove a result, including pay and anything on your tracker.
  10. The checking rule. Write how the agent proves each result is real and current (for example, open the organization’s own page and confirm the posting is there; drop it if not).
  11. The report format. Sketch one result card, field by field, and say what the report shows when nothing new turns up. No introduction paragraph.
  12. The tracker. Create a Google Sheet with two tabs, Applied and Not applied, so the agent never shows you the same result twice.
  13. The schedule. How often should it run, and why? Match how often your sources change.

Submit: The photo of your brainstorm, your rules file (Google Doc) and a link to your tracker sheet. Post the rules file to Discord the day before the deadline and comment on three classmates’ files: could an agent follow these rules without guessing?

Rubric (10): top 20 organizations in three tiers, each with a verified link and a note 2; rating, ranking, cut rules and checking rule an agent can follow without guessing 3; boards, titles, report format, tracker and schedule are all decided 2; hand brainstorm and starting-point resume or summary included 1; three critique comments on classmates’ rules files 1; accessibility: alt text on the brainstorm photo and heading styles in the rules file 1.

Step 3: Build and Test Your Agent (10 points, Process Work)

This assignment measures Module Objective 8.3, which supports Course Competency 1.

  1. Write the instructions. Open your rules file from Step 2 and add, at the top, the opening line from the example: “You are my career agent. Follow every rule below exactly. Never include a result you have not checked.” Keep this Doc; it is your master copy.
  2. Save it as a Gemini skill. In the Gemini app, create a new skill, name it (for example, “Avery’s Job Agent”), and paste your instructions. See Google’s help page on skills. If skills are not on your account yet, paste the Doc into a new chat each time; it works the same way.
  3. Test run 1. Run the agent. For every result, open the link yourself and check it against your rules. Record each result in a test log: kept, should have been cut, or broken link.
  4. Revise. For every miss, change one rule in your Doc so the miss cannot happen again. Write what you changed and why. Update the skill.
  5. Test runs 2 and 3. Run it twice more, revising after each. By run 3, every result should pass your own rules.
  6. Check privacy. Your instructions should not contain your home address, phone number, student ID or anything you would not put on a public resume.

Submit: The Google Doc with your final instructions, the test log for all three runs, and the list of changes. Post your run 3 results to Discord the day before the deadline and comment on three classmates’ results: would you trust this agent?

Rubric (10): instructions saved as a skill or master Doc, matching the Step 2 plan 2; test log covers three runs, with every result checked by hand 2; each miss led to a specific rule change, with the reason 3; run 3 results pass the agent’s own rules 1; three critique comments on classmates’ results 1; accessibility: heading styles in the Doc and alt text on images posted to Discord 1.

Step 4: Schedule and Launch Your Agent (30 points, Projects)

This assignment measures Module Objective 8.4, which supports Course Competencies 1 and 5.

  1. Choose option A or B and write two sentences on why it fits your goal.
  2. Option A: in the Gemini app, start a chat that uses your skill, then ask Gemini to schedule it (for example, “Run this every Monday at 7 a.m.”). Confirm it in Settings under Scheduled actions. See Google’s help page on scheduled actions. Turn on Gemini notifications on your phone. You can have up to ten scheduled actions at a time. If scheduled actions are not on your account yet, set a repeating Google Calendar reminder for the same days and run the skill when it fires.
  3. Option B: run your skill, then ask Gemini to “Turn these results into a one-page dashboard in Canvas, using my report format.” Publish or share the page, and set a repeating Google Calendar reminder to rerun and refresh it.
  4. Let the agent run at least twice on schedule before the deadline. Keep both outputs.
  5. Record a three-minute OBS presentation: your goal, your plan, one rule you changed in testing and why, and one scheduled output. End with what you will change next.

Take Your Work With You (ungraded, Week 15)

Your Maricopa Google account may close after you leave the college. Do this before the course ends, so you keep everything you made even if you stop out, transfer or graduate. It takes about 45 minutes.

  1. Save your files in one place. Make one Google Drive folder for this course. Put in it your resume, cover letters, portfolio files, interview recordings and agent files (rules file, tracker sheet and test log) and your AI-use log. Videos you posted to YouTube with your Maricopa account are not copied, so download your own YouTube videos into the folder too.
  2. Copy everything to a personal Google account. Make a personal Google account if you do not have one. First make your own copy (File, then Make a copy) of any file someone shared with you, because files you do not own are not copied. Then, signed in to your Maricopa account, open Google Takeout Transfer and copy your Drive and Gmail to your personal account (see Google’s help page). It can take up to a week, and the copies arrive unshared. If Transfer is not available on your account, use Google Takeout to download your Drive as .zip files and save them on your computer.
  3. Take your AI work with you. Export every Gemini skill or Gem you made: your career agent, and any others such as a Career Counselor, Mock Hiring Panel, Mentor Finder or Job Search agent. The instructions and source files are what rebuild it, so keep each one’s instructions in a Google Doc and its sources in your course folder. To keep your chats, use Google Takeout: choose My Activity, then Gemini Apps. After you leave, rebuild it in Gemini with your personal account: make a new one, paste the instructions and upload the sources. Scheduled actions stop when your Maricopa account closes, so set the schedule again in your personal account. If scheduled actions are not available there, use a repeating calendar reminder, as in Step 4.
    • Know the privacy difference. Your Maricopa account keeps your Gemini work out of AI training (see AI in This Course in Module 0). A personal account does not have the same protections, and Google may use your activity to improve its AI, depending on your settings. In your personal account, open Gemini, then Settings & help, then Activity (Gemini Apps Activity), and decide whether to turn off Keep Activity. Never put anyone else’s private information into a personal account.
  4. Move your Google Site. If you already moved your portfolio site to a personal account, it stays yours. Takeout Transfer copies Drive, Gmail and Photos. Do not count on it to move a Google Site you made with your Maricopa account, such as your portfolio site or a profile page. First keep the content: put the site’s text in a Google Doc and its images in your course folder, then open each published page and save it as a PDF (File, then Print, then Save as PDF). Then try a direct copy: in the site editor, choose Share and add your personal email as an editor. Sign in with your personal account, open the site, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing outside Maricopa is blocked, rebuild the site in your personal account at sites.google.com with the same content and publish it again. The web address changes, so update it on your resume and profiles.
  5. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
