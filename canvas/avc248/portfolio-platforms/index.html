Where to Put Your Portfolio

Every option, what it really costs, what happens when you stop paying, and how much code you need to know. Ranked in the order I recommend them.

Goal

Help you choose where your work lives after this class, with honest tradeoffs instead of a sales pitch.

Audience

You. A graduating designer who needs a portfolio a hiring manager can actually find and open.

Process

Read the comparison. Start with Behance. Then decide if you want a site of your own, and pick from the ranked list.

Start here: the two-part answer

Almost everyone should end up with two things and they do different jobs.

1. A Behance profile, because that is where employers go looking. Recruiters and creative directors search Behance to find people to hire. Nothing else on this page has that. It is a discovery engine, and it is free.

2. A real website of your own, because that is what goes on your résumé. It is the link you control, the one that says you and not a platform.

These are not competitors. Behance is how people find you. Your site is where you send them. Most working designers have both, and the same projects (and the same walkthrough videos) live on both.

If you only do one thing, do Behance. And if you would rather not put your work on Behance at all, that is a legitimate choice. Skip it and build a site. Nothing below requires it.

Do you need to buy a domain name?

No. You can graduate with a great portfolio and never spend a dollar. Every option below has a free address.

But you have the option, and it is not hard. A domain like yourname.com costs about $12 a year and takes about twenty minutes to point at a site you already built. It looks sharper on a résumé than a platform URL, and it is yours.

The one thing to understand before you buy one: your domain is portable, your host is not. You own yourname.com forever as long as you renew it. You can point it at a free site today, and re‑point it somewhere else in five years when your needs change. The domain follows you. The platform does not. That is exactly why it is worth the $12: it is the one piece of your professional identity that no company can take away or start charging you for.

Ranked, with the real tradeoffs

Recommendation 1  ·  Everyone

Behance

The one I have always recommended first. Free. Do this even if you do nothing else.

Pros
  • Employers search it to find people to hire. This is the only platform here with that. It is the whole reason it is number one.
  • Completely free, forever. No plan to lapse.
  • You already have an account through your Adobe school login.
  • Zero technical skill. Upload images, write a description, publish.
  • Built-in audience, likes, and appreciation from other designers.
Cons
  • No custom domain, ever. Your address is always behance.net/yourname.
  • Your work sits inside Adobe's layout next to everyone else's. Limited control over how it looks.
  • It is a profile, not a website. It cannot be an "About me" plus "Services" plus "Contact" site.
  • If you do not want your work public on a social platform, this is not for you, and that is fine.

Code needed: none.  ·  Cost: $0.  ·  Best for: literally everyone, as a starting point.

Recommendation 2  ·  The easy real website

Adobe Portfolio

Free right now because you are a student. Read the warning before you commit to it.

Pros
  • Free while you are enrolled. It comes with the Creative Cloud you already have through Maricopa.
  • It syncs with Behance, so projects you already published can flow straight in. Least duplicate work of anything here.
  • Real custom domain support.
  • Genuinely good-looking templates, built for designers by a design company.
  • No code at all.
Cons
  • It is rented, not owned. It only stays online while somebody is paying for Creative Cloud.
  • When your student access ends, you have 14 days, then the public site goes dark.
  • To keep it, you pay Adobe every month for the rest of your career.
  • Less layout control than building it yourself.

Read this before you point a domain at it. If you buy yourname.com point it at Adobe Portfolio, put it on your résumé, and then graduate and lose Creative Cloud, your portfolio goes offline exactly when you need it most. Your files are not deleted, but the site stops loading for anyone who clicks your résumé link.

You have two honest ways out. Pay for it forever (the cheapest Creative Cloud plan that includes Portfolio runs roughly $10 to $15 a month, and if you are freelancing you probably want Creative Cloud anyway, so this may cost you nothing extra). Or move it, rebuild somewhere free, and re-point your domain. That takes an afternoon, and it is only possible because you own the domain.

Code needed: none.  ·  Cost: $0 now, then ~$10-15/month, forever, to stay online.  ·  Best for: designers who are going to keep paying for Creative Cloud anyway, especially freelancers.

Recommendation 3  ·  The one you own outright

Build it yourself with AI, host it free on GitHub Pages

This is what I did for my own site in July 2026. It is free forever, and it is more within reach than it sounds.

Pros
  • Free. Forever. No plan to lapse, nothing to cancel, no company that can turn it off.
  • Custom domain support, free.
  • Total control. It looks exactly like you want, because you made it.
  • You can describe what you want to an AI assistant and it will write the HTML with you. You do not have to already know how to code. You have to be willing to look at code.
  • "I built my own portfolio site" is a real thing to say in an interview.
  • The skill transfers. Understanding a little HTML makes you better at every digital job.
Cons
  • The steepest learning curve here. There is no drag-and-drop editor. This is the honest cost.
  • You will meet unfamiliar words: repository, commit, deploy. None are hard, all are new.
  • Updating it means editing a file, not clicking a button.
  • If something breaks, there is no support line. There is you, the internet, and your AI assistant.
  • AI writes code that looks right and is sometimes wrong. You still have to look at the result and judge it. That is a design skill, not a coding one, and you have it.

Code needed: some HTML, but you do not have to write it from a blank page.  ·  Cost: $0, plus ~$12/yr if you want a domain.  ·  Best for: anyone who wants to actually own their site, and anyone curious enough to try.

Recommendation 4  ·  If you are going into games, 3D, or concept art

ArtStation

The Behance of the games industry. If that is your field, this is not optional.

Pros
  • This is where game studios actually recruit. Character artists, environment artists, concept artists, and animators are hired off ArtStation.
  • Free profile.
  • Built-in job board aimed at exactly these roles.
  • The audience is other game artists, which is good for feedback and good for networking.
Cons
  • Custom domain requires ArtStation Pro, which is paid.
  • Very field-specific. If you are doing branding or print design, your work will look out of place.
  • The bar for posted work is high. That is motivating for some people and demoralizing for others.

Code needed: none.  ·  Cost: free profile, Pro is paid for a custom domain.  ·  Best for: games, 3D, animation, concept art. Skip it if that is not you.

Recommendation 5  ·  Free and fast, if looks are not the priority

Google Sites

The easiest free site you can make. Be honest with yourself about what it looks like.

Pros
  • Free forever, with free custom domain support. That combination is rare.
  • You already have a Google account.
  • Easiest builder on this page. You can have a site up in under an hour.
  • Never goes offline, because there is nothing to pay.
Cons
  • It will not look as good as the others. Say that out loud. The templates are plain and there is very little you can do about it.
  • You are a designer. A hiring manager will notice that your own site looks generic, and it is fair for them to notice.
  • Almost no layout control, no custom typography worth the name.
  • It reads as a school project, because it is what schools use.

Code needed: none.  ·  Cost: $0.  ·  Best for: getting something live this week, or as a temporary home while you build something better. Not a great final answer for a design portfolio.

Recommendation 6  ·  Polished, but you rent it

Squarespace and Wix

Beautiful, easy, and you will pay for them every month for as long as the site exists.

Pros
  • The best-looking templates you can get without writing code.
  • Truly no code. Drag, drop, done.
  • Real customer support, which nothing free on this page has.
  • Handles the boring parts (mobile layouts, contact forms, e‑commerce if you sell prints).
  • Squarespace often has a student discount. Check before you pay full price.
Cons
  • Squarespace has no free tier at all. The trial ends and you pay or the site goes away.
  • Wix's free tier puts their ads on your portfolio and gives you a wixsite.com address. Not acceptable for a professional portfolio.
  • Stop paying and your site is gone. This is a subscription you carry for your whole career.
  • Over several years, this is by far the most expensive option here.

Code needed: none.  ·  Cost: paid, ongoing, indefinitely.  ·  Best for: people who want it to look great, want zero technical friction, and are fine paying for that. That is a completely valid tradeoff. Just make it on purpose.

Recommendation 7  ·  Powerful, and more work than you want

WordPress

Runs a huge share of the web. I have always found it harder than it should be, and I am being straight with you: it has not gotten much easier.

Pros
  • Enormously powerful. It can do anything, eventually.
  • Huge plugin ecosystem, endless themes.
  • A real professional skill. Plenty of agencies still run client sites on it.
  • Self-hosted WordPress is genuinely yours, with no platform that can shut you off.
Cons
  • The confusing part nobody explains: there are two WordPresses. wordpress.com is a hosted service with paid plans. wordpress.org is free software you have to host yourself. People mix these up constantly and it is the source of most WordPress frustration.
  • Themes, plugins, updates, hosting, and security are all your problem.
  • The learning curve is real and it buys you very little that you need for a portfolio.
  • A custom domain still costs money on wordpress.com.

Code needed: not officially, but you will hit it.  ·  Cost: paid for anything real.  ·  Best for: honestly? Not a student design portfolio. It is on this list because you will hear about it, and you deserve to know why I am not recommending it.

If you want me to just tell you what to do

1. Put your work on Behance. Free, and it is where employers look.
2. Build a real site. Use Adobe Portfolio if you want it easy and you know you will keep paying for Creative Cloud. Build it yourself on GitHub Pages if you want to own it outright and never pay again.
3. Optionally, buy yourname.com for about $12 and point it at whichever one you built. You do not have to. But it is yours, and it moves with you.
4. Put your project walkthrough videos on both.

Pricing and features Platforms change their plans constantly, so confirm current pricing before you pay for anything. If you find something on this page that is out of date, tell me and I will fix it.
