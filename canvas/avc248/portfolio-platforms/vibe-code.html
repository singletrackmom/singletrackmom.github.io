Build Your Own Site with AI

Free forever, fully yours, and closer to your reach than you think. This is how I built my own site.

Cost: $0 foreverCode needed: Some HTML (AI helps)Custom domain: Yes, freeSetup: a weekend

Goal

Own your portfolio outright. No subscription, no platform, nothing that can be turned off or start charging you.

Audience

Anyone willing to look at code, even if they cannot write it yet. You do not need to be technical. You need to be curious.

Process

Describe the site you want to an AI assistant. It writes the HTML. You review it, fix what looks wrong, and publish it free on GitHub Pages.

Why I am recommending this at all

I built my own site this way in July 2026. I am a graphic designer, not a developer. I described what I wanted to an AI assistant, it wrote the HTML, and I told it what to change until it looked right. Then I put it on GitHub Pages, which hosts it for free, and pointed my own domain at it.

Total ongoing cost: about $12 a year for the domain. That is it. There is nothing else to pay, ever.

Two years ago I would not have put this on a list for a 100-level design student. The AI assistants changed that. You still have to think, and you still have to judge whether the result is any good. But you are a designer. Judging whether something looks right is the thing you are already trained to do.

What it looks like: exactly what you tell it to. That is the entire point, and it is also the catch. There is no template protecting you from your own choices. If your site looks generic, that is a design decision you made. If it looks incredible, that is yours too. For a designer, that is the most honest possible portfolio: the site itself is a work sample.

The honest cost

This is the hardest option on the list, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. There is no drag-and-drop editor. You will meet words you have never seen: repository, commit, deploy. None of them are hard, but all of them are new, and new is tiring when you are also job hunting.

And AI writes code that looks confident and is sometimes wrong. It will hand you something broken with total conviction. You have to look at what came back and judge it, which means the work never fully leaves your hands. That is a feature, not a bug, but know it going in.

Set it up

  1. Sketch first, on paper. What pages? What goes on the homepage? Do not skip this. “Make me a portfolio” produces slop. A clear description produces a good site.
  2. Decide your design system before you type a word. Pick your typefaces. Pick three to five solid colors and write down the hex codes. You already know how to do this. Hand these to the AI as rules, not suggestions.
  3. Open an AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, or whatever you have) and describe the site. Be specific. Tell it: a single HTML file, my exact colors, my exact fonts, no gradients, these sections, this content.
  4. Read what it gives you. Ask it to explain any part you do not understand. It will. This is how you learn, and you will pick up more HTML in a weekend than you expect.
  5. Iterate like a designer. “The headline is too heavy.” “Tighten the space above the project grid.” “This does not work on a phone.” You already know how to give this kind of feedback. Give it.
  6. Save the file as index.html.
  7. Make a free GitHub account at github.com.
  8. Create a repository named yourusername.github.io. The name matters, that exact pattern is what makes it a website.
  9. Upload your index.html to that repository. There is a plain upload button. You do not have to learn git commands to do this.
  10. Turn on GitHub Pages in the repository's Settings, under Pages. Your site is now live at yourusername.github.io free, forever.
  11. Optional: point your own domain at it. GitHub Pages supports custom domains for free. See the domain page.

Pros and cons

Pros
  • Free forever. No subscription, no lapse, no company that can shut you off.
  • Custom domain support, free.
  • Total design control. It looks like you, because it is you.
  • “I built my own portfolio site” is a real thing to say in an interview, and people remember it.
  • You learn a little HTML, and that skill transfers to every digital design job.
  • The site itself becomes a work sample.
  • Nobody can put ads on it, change the terms, or raise the price.
Cons
  • The steepest learning curve here. Real, and worth naming.
  • No visual editor. Updates mean editing a file.
  • New vocabulary that has nothing to do with design.
  • No support line. Just you, the internet, and the AI.
  • The AI will confidently give you broken code sometimes. You have to check.
  • You can absolutely build something ugly. The template was not there to save you.

The reason this is worth the trouble: every other option on this list is a place you are staying. This is the only one you own. Ten years from now, Adobe Portfolio might not exist, Squarespace might have tripled its price, and Behance might have redesigned into something you hate. A folder with an index.html in it will still open and still work.
