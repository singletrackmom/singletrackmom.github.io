What Employers Actually Do With Your Portfolio

How little time they spend, what they open first, and why your weakest visible piece matters more than your best one.

← Back to all platforms
Read this before you buildThen read it again before you cut

Goal

Replace what you imagine happens when someone opens your portfolio with what actually happens.

Audience

You, before you decide what goes in and what gets cut.

Process

Read it. Then go look at your own portfolio the way a stranger with 30 seconds would.

The uncomfortable truth about how long they look

You will spend a semester on your portfolio. A hiring manager with sixty applications will give it somewhere between thirty seconds and two minutes on the first pass.

They are not reading. They are scanning to decide whether to keep reading. Everything below follows from that one fact.

Design for the first ten seconds, then design for the person who stays. Those are two different jobs. Most student portfolios are built entirely for the second one and never survive the first.

The rule that will hurt: you are judged by your weakest visible piece

Not your best piece. Not your average. Your weakest one.

Here is why. When a reviewer sees one great piece and one weak piece, they do not think “nice range.” They think “I am not sure this person knows which one is which.” And that is a much worse problem than being inexperienced, because it means they cannot trust your judgment on their project either.

A portfolio of six strong pieces beats a portfolio of six strong pieces plus four okay ones. Adding work made it worse. Sit with that.

Hierarchy: the mistake almost everyone makes

You have two or three years of work. Some of it is from your first semester. Some of it might be from high school. You are proud of it, and you should be, it is how you got here.

It does not go first.

Your portfolio is not a timeline and it is not a scrapbook. It is an argument, and the argument is “I can do the job you are hiring for.” Every piece either supports that argument or dilutes it.

  • Strongest piece first. Always. Not your favorite. Not the one that took longest. The one that best proves you can do the job you are applying for.
  • Second strongest last. People remember the first thing and the last thing. Put your two best there.
  • Older or weaker work is secondary, which means deeper in, smaller, or on a second page. Not gone necessarily. Just not the thing that greets a stranger.
  • High school work is not disqualifying. But it should never be the first thing a hiring manager sees, and if it is not carrying weight, it is costing you.
  • If you are not sure whether a piece is strong enough: cut it. The hesitation is the answer.

What they are actually looking for

  • Can this person solve a problem, or just make things pretty? This is the big one. Show the brief, the constraint, the decision. Pretty is table stakes.
  • Can they think? A portfolio with no writing is a folder of images. Two sentences of real reasoning per project changes everything.
  • Do they fit the job? A studio hiring a packaging designer wants to see packaging. Range is nice. Relevance beats range.
  • Is the work finished? Typos, misaligned type, low-res exports, and placeholder text kill you instantly. It says you do not sweat details, and the whole job is details.
  • Does the portfolio itself look designed? The site is a work sample whether you intended it or not. A sloppy portfolio site about beautiful work is a contradiction they will notice.

Which projects get a walkthrough video

Only your strongest projects get one. Not the old ones. Not a personal project, unless it is directly relevant to the industry you are targeting.

The video is a signal not a requirement. When you make a walkthrough for a project, you are telling the viewer “this one is my best, spend your time here.” If you make one for everything, you have said nothing.

Three great walkthroughs beat eight mediocre ones. Choosing which projects deserve one is itself the skill, and it is the exact skill most students are missing.

What goes in a walkthrough, and what kills one

Two to three minutes. Three beats:

  • The problem. What was the brief, the constraint, the audience?
  • The decision. One real fork in the road, and why you went the way you went. Not “then I picked blue.” Why blue.
  • The result. What the finished thing does.

The failure mode: narrating a tour of your own screen. “Then I opened Illustrator, and I made a shape, and then I made it blue.”

Nobody hires that. They can see what you made. They are watching to hear you think. The whole value of a walkthrough is that it is the one place a portfolio can show reasoning instead of results.

Go look at real portfolios

Before you build anything, go find five portfolios from people doing the job you want. Not famous designers. People one or two years ahead of you.

  • What did they lead with?
  • How many projects? (It is probably fewer than you think.)
  • How much writing per project?
  • What did they leave out? What would you have cut?
  • How long did it take you to figure out what they do? If it was more than five seconds, that is a failure, and it is the most common one.

Now open your own portfolio and ask the same five questions. Be as hard on yourself as you were on strangers.
