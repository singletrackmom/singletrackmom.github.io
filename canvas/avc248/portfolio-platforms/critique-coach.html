The Portfolio Critique Coach

An AI side-coach that helps you write a real critique instead of “looks good!” It does not grade you, and it does not replace your classmates.

Goal

Help you give specific, useful, kind feedback on a classmate’s portfolio, using real vocabulary instead of vague praise.

Audience

You, when you are staring at a classmate’s portfolio with nothing to say but “this is nice.”

Process

Answer the coach’s questions about what you actually see. It helps you turn those observations into a critique worth reading.

Why this exists

“Looks good!” is not a critique. It is politeness wearing a critique costume. It helps nobody, and it is what most peer feedback in most classes actually is.

The problem usually is not that you have nothing to say. It is that you do not have the words yet. You can feel that something is off in a layout and not know it is a hierarchy problem. The coach exists to hand you the vocabulary and push you to be specific.

What this is not.

It does not critique your classmate’s portfolio for you. It coaches you to write the critique.

It does not grade anything, and I do not see what you type into it.

Do not paste a classmate’s personal information, résumé, or contact details into any AI tool. Public portfolio work is fine. Their private materials are not yours to hand to a company.

And it does not replace your classmates. Their eyes are the point. This just helps you use yours better.

The vocabulary it will push you toward

  • Hierarchy · what you see first, second, third. Is the strongest work first? Does your eye land where the designer wanted?
  • Curation · what is in and what is out. What would you cut? What is the weakest visible piece?
  • Relevance · does this work argue for the job this person says they want?
  • Range vs. depth · a little of everything, or real command of one thing? Both are strategies. Which is this, and is it on purpose?
  • Narrative · does each project explain the problem, or just show the result?
  • Craft · typos, alignment, image resolution, consistent margins. The unglamorous stuff that gets people rejected.
  • Voice · can you tell who made this? Or could it be anyone?
  • Scan time · how long before you knew what this person does? Over five seconds is a problem.

How to use it

  1. Look at the portfolio yourself first, cold. Time it. Note what you saw in the first ten seconds, before you form an opinion. Do not skip this, it is the most valuable data you will produce.
  2. Open the coach and tell it what you actually observed. Not what you think you should say. What you saw.
  3. Let it push you. If you say “the layout feels off,” it will ask you where and why. That is the whole job.
  4. Write the critique in your own words. Do not paste its output. Your classmate wants a person’s reaction, not a machine’s.
  5. Lead with what is working, and be as specific about that as you are about the problems. “The first project is the right one to lead with, it is clearly your strongest” is worth more than three paragraphs of criticism.
  6. Give one thing to change, not ten. One actionable note beats a list nobody can act on.

The prompt behind the coach

No mystery here. This is what it is told to do, and you should know:

You are a portfolio critique coach for graduating design students. You never write the critique. You help the student write their own.

When a student describes what they saw in a classmate’s portfolio, ask questions that push them from vague to specific. If they say “it looks unprofessional,” ask what specifically: is it the craft, the curation, the hierarchy, the writing?

Teach the vocabulary in context: hierarchy, curation, relevance, range vs. depth, narrative, craft, voice, scan time.

Push on these in particular: Is the strongest work first? What is the weakest visible piece, and should it be cut? Does the work argue for the job this person says they want? How long before you could tell what they do?

Insist on kindness and specificity together. Vague praise is not kind, it is just easy. Refuse to help them write cruelty, and refuse to help them write nothing.

Never evaluate a student’s worth or talent. Evaluate choices, which can be changed.

The honest note about using AI here

An AI can hand you vocabulary. It cannot tell you whether a piece of work moved it, because it was not moved.

The most valuable thing in a critique is a real human’s real reaction: what you noticed first, what confused you, what you would have cut. No tool can produce that, and it is exactly what your classmate needs.

Use the coach to get sharper. Do not use it to get out of looking.
