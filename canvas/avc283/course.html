How This Course Works

AVC 283 is fully online and asynchronous: there are no live class meetings, and you work on your own time each week. You build one client’s brand identity all semester. You should already know the basics of Illustrator, Photoshop and InDesign from AVC 183 Graphic Design.

Plan on about 8 hours a week, or 120 hours in all. A new module opens each Monday. Every module has the same parts in the same order: Objectives, Overview, Watch, Read, Practice, Assessment and Critique. Each graded item has its own page with objectives, instructions and a rubric (the scoring guide). Every video has captions and a transcript. Read AI in This Course in Module 0 during Week 1.

Deadlines

Work is due at noon Arizona time every Thursday and Sunday; Week 1 has one deadline, on Thursday. For process steps and projects, post in the AVC 283 Discord channel by noon the day before. Work up to 24 hours late loses 10 percent. After that cutoff, called the hard close, nothing is accepted.

No work is due when the college is closed, and Canvas shows any moved deadline. If you can, comment on classmates who post late. It earns no points, but it helps the class.

Critique

Every critique comment has three parts and is two to four sentences long:

  1. Strength: one specific thing that works, and why.
  2. Issue: one specific problem and where it is (for example, “the tagline in the stacked lockup”).
  3. Suggestion: one fix tied to a design principle: hierarchy, contrast, alignment, proximity, legibility, consistency or accessibility.

Comment on three different classmates. Start with posts that have the fewest comments. Critique the work, not the person.

If you cannot use Discord, post and comment in the matching Canvas discussion instead. Tell the instructor through Canvas Inbox.

Accessibility of Your Work

Everything you make must work for people who cannot see it or cannot hear it. About 10 percent of the points on each media assignment go to accessibility. These rules apply to every assignment:

  • Video: add captions. Start with automatic captions (YouTube Studio, Premiere Pro Speech to Text or Google Meet). Then read every line and fix names, terms and timing by hand.
  • Images: add alt text to every image that carries meaning. Alt text is a short written description that a screen reader reads aloud. Write one or two sentences on what matters. Mark decorative images as decorative.
  • Documents and PDFs: use real headings, readable color contrast and alt text. Export PDFs with tags, the hidden labels that tell a screen reader what each part is.
  • Web pages: headings in order, link text that says where the link goes, alt text, and captions on video. Check the page with WAVE.
  • Discord: alt text on every image; captions or a transcript with every audio or video post.

AI in This Course

This course uses Google Gemini, a generative AI tool, in some assignments. You use it for research, writing and feedback. You make all of the design work yourself.

Industry expects it. In the 2024 Microsoft and LinkedIn Work Trend Index, 66% of business leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills, and 71% said they would rather hire a less experienced candidate with AI skills than a more experienced candidate without them (Work Trend Index). Our industry advisory board recommends that we teach these tools.

Using AI here is not cheating. Cheating is turning in work you did not do as if you did. Here, AI use is assigned, and you record it in an AI-use log with every assignment. You are graded on your own thinking: your research, your decisions and your design work. Other courses may have different AI rules. Follow those rules in those courses.

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account. That is Gemini for Education, the educational version of Gemini. Google states that what you enter there is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models (Gemini for Education; Google’s education FAQ). Your artwork and writing are not used to teach the AI. A personal Google account is different: depending on its settings, what you enter there may be used to improve Google’s AI. That is why every assignment uses your Maricopa account.

Always sign in to Gemini with your Maricopa account, never a personal one. Your instructor will not put your work into any public AI tool. The only AI tools in this course are Gemini and Gemini Notebook.

Gemini writes; it never designs. Gemini helps with research, writing, critique questions and checklists. It never makes images, mockups or any other visual. Every logo, layout, image and visual choice is yours, and you show your process. You draw every logo yourself in vector (shapes built from points and paths, so they stay sharp at any size), and you submit the construction file.

You will learn to question it. AI makes confident mistakes and repeats bias from the data it learned from. You will learn to check every answer and to think about what AI gets wrong and what it costs.

AI has real costs. AI is trained on artists’ work, often without permission. Data centers used about 1.5% of the world’s electricity in 2024, and the International Energy Agency expects that to more than double by 2030.

AI Primer

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account. That is Gemini for Education, the educational version of Gemini. Google states that what you enter there is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models (Gemini for Education; Google’s education FAQ). That is why every assignment uses your Maricopa account.

You are here to build a career in graphic design, and employers already expect AI skills (see Industry expects it, above). Three out of four knowledge workers (people who work with information) already use AI at work. Without these skills, you would fall behind the people you compete with for jobs.

So why learn it? Refusing AI does not slow it down. It only takes away your voice in how it is used. The designers who shape how a studio uses AI are the ones who understand it. Employers also say creativity, communication and critical thinking matter more than ever, because AI cannot do those for you. In this course, the creative work stays in your hands. You learn to use AI for what it does well and to catch it when it is wrong. You can leave as an informed critic, but only if you know how it works.

E1 Figma Warm-Up: A Three-Page Site

Objectives

  1. 3.5 Produce a simple three-page website layout in Figma with consistent navigation, readable text and named layers (Course Competency 3)

10 points, Exercises. This assignment measures Module Objective 3.5, which supports Course Competency 3.

Overview

Part of Module 3: Logo Construction and Refinement. You make three desktop pages in Figma for Sidewalk Sips, a made-up neighborhood coffee cart: Home, About and Contact. Your instructor gives you all the words and photos, so you can focus on learning the tool. Plan on one sitting of about 2.5 hours. You do not need components, auto layout or prototyping; those come in Module 14. This is an exercise, so there is no Discord critique. If you already did this warm-up in AVC 190, build it again with a new layout; it goes faster the second time.

AI: none. This is hands-on practice in Figma. Do not use Figma’s AI features. Your AI-use log for E1 says “No AI used.”

Instructions

  1. Make your Figma account. Go to Figma for Education and sign up with your Maricopa email (MEID@maricopa.edu). Figma asks college students to confirm they are 18 or older for the education plan. If you cannot get it, sign up for the free Starter plan instead; it has everything this exercise needs. Still stuck? Your instructor can share a starter file for you to copy.
  2. Get the content. Open the Figma Warm-Up Content Pack from the link in the Canvas assignment. It has the words for each page, the nav labels and six photos with credits and alt text. Download the photos to one folder. Use the words as they are; this exercise is about layout, not writing.
  3. Start a file. In Figma, choose Create (or the plus sign), then Design. Name the file “Your Last Name Figma Warm-Up.”
  4. Make three frames. A frame is one page, like an artboard in Illustrator. Press F for the Frame tool, then choose Desktop in the panel on the right. Make three desktop frames side by side. Double-click each frame’s name and rename them Home, About and Contact.
  5. Build the nav bar on Home. Press R for the Rectangle tool and draw a bar across the top of the frame. Press T for the Text tool and type the business name on the left and the nav labels Home, About and Contact on the right.
  6. Add the words. With the Text tool, place the headline and page copy on Home, the About copy on About, and the contact details on Contact. Make headlines large and body text at least 16 pixels. Use dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark one, so it is easy to read.
  7. Place the photos. Drag photos from your computer into a frame. Put at least one photo on each page. To resize a photo without stretching it, hold Shift while you drag a corner. Put the photo credits from the pack in small text at the bottom of the Contact page.
  8. Line things up. Select two or more layers, then use the alignment buttons at the top of the right panel (align left, align center, align top). Keep the same space at the left edge of every page.
  9. Group and copy the nav bar. Select the bar and its text and press Command + G (Mac) or Ctrl + G (Windows) to group them. Name the group Nav. Copy it, select the About frame and paste, then do the same on Contact. Check that the nav bar sits in the same spot on all three pages.
  10. Name your layers. In the Layers panel on the left, double-click each layer and give it a plain name, such as Headline, Hero photo or Contact email. No “Rectangle 4.”
  11. Share a view link. Click Share, set the link so anyone with the link can view, and copy it. Open the link in a private browser window to make sure it works.
  12. Export each page. Select a frame, find Export at the bottom of the right panel, choose PNG and click Export. Do this for all three frames.
  13. Submit in Canvas: the view link, the three PNG files, and alt text for each PNG: one or two sentences that say what the page shows.
  • If you get stuck, post a screenshot in the AVC 283 Discord channel with the step number.
  • Screen-reader users: Figma’s canvas is hard to use with a screen reader. Send a Canvas Inbox message, and an equivalent path will be set up.
  • Follow the accessibility rules: check that your text is easy to read against its background, and write alt text for every PNG you submit.

Building Your Image Bank

A generator needs pictures to work with. Your image bank is one organized folder of photos and video clips you are allowed to use, with a record of where each one came from. You build it once and use it for all three applications.

What goes in the bank

Getting Started with Figma

Figma is a design tool that runs in a web browser (or as a desktop app). Designers use it for screens, websites, social posts and design systems, and teams work in the same file at once. You will use it to build your brand as a set of reusable parts, then turn those parts into a generator.

Three words you will see a lot:

  • Frame: a box that holds a design, like an artboard in Illustrator. Each post or screen is one frame.
  • Layer: each thing inside a frame (a text box, a photo, the logo). The Layers panel lists them.
  • Component: a reusable part. Change the main component and every copy of it updates.

You already know frames, text and images from E1 Figma Warm-Up in Module 3. Start at step 1; steps 3 to 5 are quick review, and the new skills start at step 6.

Part 1: Check your account

  1. Sign in at figma.com with the Maricopa account you made in the warm-up. Education status lasts for a set term. If Figma asks you to reapply, follow Verify your education status. If you have education status, create an education team named “AVC 283 your name.”

If you are on the free Starter plan (for example, you are under 18 or verification failed), Starter allows three files in your team and does not publish libraries (Figma plans and features). That is enough for this module: build your whole system and your templates in one file, and skip the “publish the library” step. Tell your instructor through Canvas Inbox so the rubric is applied the same way.

Part 2: Start your brand file

  1. Create a new Design file. Name it “Client name Brand System.”

Part 3: Make your first template frame

  1. Press F for the Frame tool. In the right sidebar, type 1080 for W (width) and 1350 for H (height). This is one feed post. Rename the frame “Feed post.” Name every layer you make; the generator finds layers by name.
  2. Press R and drag a rectangle across the top half of the frame. Rename it “Photo.” Drag a photo from your image bank onto the rectangle to fill it. The photo becomes the rectangle’s fill, so it stays inside the shape.
  3. Press T, click under the photo and type a headline. Rename the layer “Headline.” Add a second text layer named “Body.”
  4. Bring in your logo. In Illustrator, choose File, Export, Export As, and save the logo as SVG. Drag the SVG file into Figma and place it in the frame. Rename it “Logo.”
  5. If your brand typeface is not in Figma’s font list, install the Figma desktop app, which can use fonts installed on your computer. If it still does not appear, use the closest Google Font and write the swap into your rules.

Part 4: Build the brand system

Now turn your guidelines book into parts Figma can reuse. Keep your book open beside you and copy values exactly.

  1. Color variables. A variable is a named value you can reuse, like “Brand Navy = #1D2B4F.” Click an empty spot on the canvas so nothing is selected. In the right sidebar, open Variables (under Local variables). Create a collection named “Brand.” Add one color variable for each color in your book, named the way your book names them (for example, color/primary, color/accent, color/paper). Type the HEX value from the book. Guide: Guide to variables in Figma.
  2. Number variables. In the same collection, add number variables for spacing from your grid (for example, space/margin = 64, space/gap = 24).
  3. Text styles. Select your headline. Set the font, size, line height and letter spacing from your book. In the Typography section of the right sidebar, click the styles icon, then the plus sign, and name the style (for example, Headline/H1). Repeat for each level in your book’s hierarchy: H1, H2, body, caption, button. Guide: Guide to styles in Figma.
  4. Apply variables. Select each layer and change its fill to a variable instead of a typed color: click the fill, open the libraries icon, and pick the variable. From now on, if a color changes, it changes everywhere.
  5. Logo component. Select the logo and press Command, Option and K (Mac) or Control, Alt and K (Windows) to make it a component. Duplicate it twice. Make one reversed (white) and one single color. Make each a component, select all three, and click Combine as variants in the right sidebar. Name the property “Version” with the values Full color, Reversed and One color. Guides: Guide to components and Create and use variants.
  6. Auto layout. Select the Headline and Body layers and press Shift and A. Auto layout stacks layers and spaces them by rule, so a longer headline pushes the body down instead of overlapping it. Set the gap and padding with your number variables. Rename the group “Text block.” Guide: Guide to auto layout.
  7. Template component. Select the whole Feed post frame and make it a component. Build the other sizes you need (1080 by 1920 and 1080 by 1080) the same way, reusing the logo component, the text styles and the variables.
  8. Write the rules into the parts. Select each component and type its rules into the description field (for example, “Logo: bottom right, 96 pixels wide, clear space all around equal to the height of the x”). Anyone who uses the part sees the rule. Guide: Add descriptions to styles, components, and variables.
  9. Publish the library (education plan only). Open the Assets tab in the left sidebar, open the library settings, and publish. Your components, styles and variables can now be used in other files, including Figma Buzz. Guide: Publish a library.

How the Generator Works

The generator has four parts. You make all four.

PartWhat it isWho makes it
RulesYour brand rules from the guidelines book, rewritten so each one can be checked yes or noYou. Gemini may help draft the wording; you test and decide every rule.
TemplatesFigma components built from your variables and styles, with every changing layer namedYou
DataA Google Sheet: one row per piece, one column per changing layerYou. Gemini may draft caption variations; you choose and edit them.
The runFigma Buzz reads the sheet and fills one copy of the template per rowThe machine. It applies your system; it does not design.

Step 1: Write rules a machine can follow

A rule like “keep it clean” cannot be checked. A rule like “the headline is 6 words or fewer” can. Write every rule with a measurable value. Examples:

  • Headline: Headline/H1 style, 6 words or fewer, top third of the frame.
  • Logo: Full color version on light backgrounds, Reversed on dark; bottom right; 96 pixels wide; clear space equal to the height of the x.
  • Color pairs allowed: Navy text on Paper, White text on Navy. Never Accent text on Paper (fails contrast).
  • Photos: one subject, natural light, subject on the left half so the text block sits on the right.
  • Never: stretched logo, more than two typefaces, text over a busy part of a photo.

Check every allowed color pair with the WebAIM Contrast Checker before it goes in the rules.

Step 2: Put the rules into the spreadsheet

  1. Make a Google Sheet named “Campaign Data.” Row 1 holds the column names. Use the exact layer names from your template: Headline, Body, CTA, Photo. Add columns for Phase, Format, Caption and Alt text.
  2. Each row after that is one piece. Fifteen posts means fifteen rows.
  3. Make the sheet enforce your rules. In a helper column, use =LEN(B2) to count the characters in a headline and highlight any that run long. Use Data, then Data validation, to give the Phase column a dropdown (Teaser, Launch, Sustain).
  4. In the Photo column, put a link to each image in your bank.
  5. Make one tab per format (feed, story, square), because each format has its own template.
  6. Download each tab: File, then Download, then Microsoft Excel (.xlsx) or Comma-separated values (.csv).

Step 3: Run it in Figma Buzz

Figma Buzz is Figma’s tool for making marketing pieces in batches. While it is in beta, Figma lists it as available on all plans.

  1. Go to figma.com/buzz/new to start a Buzz file.
  2. In your Figma Design file, select your feed post template, copy it, and paste it into a Buzz asset. Check that the layer names came with it.
  3. Open Bulk create from the left navigation and upload the feed tab file.
  4. Select a text or image layer, then choose the column that fills it. Text layers take text columns; image layers take image columns. Repeat for each changing layer.
  5. Click Create assets. Buzz makes one piece per row. Switch to Grid view to see the whole batch.
  6. Repeat for the story and square templates.
  7. Export the batch from the right panel as image files.

Guide: Bulk create assets in Figma Buzz. If photos from Drive links do not load (district sharing settings can block them), place those photos by hand in Grid view and note it. That is a generator flaw; put it in your batch critique.

Do not use Buzz’s image generation or any Figma AI feature. Every image comes from your bank.

Step 4: Critique, revise, run again

This is the assignment. Score every piece in a batch critique sheet with these columns: Piece number; Rules broken (list them by name); Usable at a job without a designer fixing it (yes, fix, or no); What went wrong; Rule or template change.

Count the “yes” pieces. That is your usable rate. Then change the rules, the sheet or the template, and run the batch again. Project 2 compares the first run and the second.

It is slower, but the rules, the critique and the rerun work the same way. Data plugins exist in the Figma Community; check the cost, and note that many need a public sheet, which district sharing settings may block.
  • Website: build the pages from your components and repeat the cards from the sheet. Publish with Figma Sites (Publish a site; the education plan includes one published site) or with Google Sites.
  • Advanced, optional: ask Gemini to write a small web page that builds posts from your rules, the same way as From Gemini to a Web Page. Give it your rules document, your HEX values, your type sizes and your logo file. Gemini writes the code; every visual value comes from your rules. From Gemini to a Web Page

You can turn something you make in Gemini into a real web page, with no code. You publish your Final Project Part B case study on Google Sites the same way.

Part 1: Make the App

  1. Open your web browser (Chrome works best).
  2. Go to gemini.google.com.
  3. Look at the top right corner. Make sure it shows your Maricopa account (your MEID@maricopa.edu). If it shows a personal account, click the picture and switch to your Maricopa account.
  4. Click in the box at the bottom that says “Ask Gemini.”
  5. Type this, then press Enter: “Make an interactive quiz with 5 questions about brand identity design. Make it a working app I can click.”
  6. Wait. Gemini builds the app in a panel on the right side of the screen.
  7. Click through your quiz to make sure it works.
  8. If something is wrong, type what to fix in the box (for example, “Make the buttons bigger”) and press Enter.

Part 2: Send It to Google Sites

  1. Look at the top of the app panel for the Export button. Click Export.
  2. Click Google Sites.
  3. A new tab opens with a Google Sites draft. Your app is already on the page. This draft is private; only you can see it.
  4. Click the title at the top of the page and type a name for your page.
  5. Click below the app and type one sentence about what your app does.

Part 3: Publish and Share

  1. Click the blue Publish button in the top right corner.
  2. Type a short web address for your site (for example, your-name-quiz). Click Publish.
  3. Click Share. Next to Published site, choose Restricted, so only people you add can see it. Add your instructor’s email.
  4. Click the arrow next to Publish and click Copy published site link.
  5. Go to the AVC 283 Discord channel. Paste the link with a screenshot of the page, and press Enter. Classmates you have not added see the screenshot.

If you get stuck: post a screenshot in the AVC 283 Discord channel with the step number. Screen-reader users: the Export menu and the Publish button are labeled buttons. If a step does not read clearly, post in Discord or send a Canvas Inbox message, and another way will be set up.

Good to know: your site follows the district’s sharing rules, so people outside Maricopa may not be able to open it. Gemini may build how the app works, but every design choice on your page is yours: words, colors and images. Take Your Work With You

Ungraded. Before the course ends.

Your Maricopa Google account may close after you leave the college. Do this before the course ends, so you keep everything you made even if you stop out, transfer or graduate. It takes about 45 minutes.

  1. Save your files in one place. Make one Google Drive folder for this course. Put in it your InDesign guidelines book and its package, Illustrator and Photoshop files, Figma templates and spreadsheet, exported PDFs and images, the Acrobat reports and your presentation and your AI-use log. For each Figma file, choose File, then Save local copy, to download a .fig copy.
  2. Copy everything to a personal Google account. Make a personal Google account if you do not have one. First make your own copy (File, then Make a copy) of any file someone shared with you, because files you do not own are not copied. Then, signed in to your Maricopa account, open Google Takeout Transfer and copy your Drive and Gmail to your personal account (see Google’s help page). It can take up to a week, and the copies arrive unshared. If Transfer is not available on your account, use Google Takeout to download your Drive as .zip files and save them on your computer.
  3. Take your AI work with you. Save anything you made with Gemini: the prompts from your AI-use log, the case-study edits Gemini suggested, and the code of any Gemini Canvas app. The instructions and source files are what rebuild it, so keep each one’s instructions in a Google Doc and its sources in your course folder. To keep your chats, use Google Takeout: choose My Activity, then Gemini Apps. After you leave, rebuild it in Gemini with your personal account: make a new one, paste the instructions and upload the sources.
    • Know the privacy difference. Your Maricopa account keeps your Gemini work out of AI training (see Start Here). A personal account does not have the same protections, and Google may use your activity to improve its AI, depending on your settings. In your personal account, open Gemini, then Settings & help, then Activity (Gemini Apps Activity), and decide whether to turn off Keep Activity. Never put anyone else’s private information into a personal account.
  4. Move your Google Site. Takeout Transfer copies Drive, Gmail and Photos. Do not count on it to move your case-study site. First keep the content: put the site’s text in a Google Doc and its images in your course folder, then open each published page and save it as a PDF (File, then Print, then Save as PDF). Then try a direct copy: in the site editor, choose Share and add your personal email as an editor. Sign in with your personal account, open the site, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing outside Maricopa is blocked, rebuild the site in your personal account at sites.google.com with the same content and publish it again. The web address changes, so update it on your resume and profiles.
  5. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
