AI Primer (10 points, Process Work)

What this is. Your first time using Gemini in this course, and it takes about an hour. No experience with AI is needed. Complete Technology Setup first.

What Gemini is. Gemini is an AI chat tool from Google. You type a request, called a prompt, and it writes an answer. It learned from an enormous amount of text written by people, so it can sound knowledgeable about almost anything. It does not know you, and it has never been where you have been.

Why this assignment. Some assignments in this course use Gemini for research and feedback. Before you use it for that, you will see for yourself what it does well, where it falls short, and what you bring that it cannot.

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account, so what you enter is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models. See AI in This Course for Google’s exact wording.

This assignment measures Module Objective 0.2, which supports Course Competency 3.

Part 1: Sign in

  1. Open Chrome and go to gemini.google.com.
  2. If you see Sign in in the top right corner, click it. Sign in with your Maricopa account: your MEID followed by @maricopa.edu, then your MEID password on the Maricopa sign-in page.
  3. Check the top right corner. It should show your Maricopa account. If it shows a personal Gmail account, click the picture and switch to your Maricopa account.

If Gemini does not open or says it is not available for your account (this can happen for students under 18), message your instructor in Canvas Inbox. You will receive an equal option with the same grade.

Part 2: Ask the question

  1. Click the box at the bottom that says Ask Gemini.
  2. Type Do I need to learn AI to get a good job in the digital media industry? and press Enter.
  3. Copy your prompt and Gemini’s answer into a Google Doc.

Part 3: Read it like a critic

  1. In your Doc, mark:
    • one point you agree with;
    • one point that sounds generic, overstated or like a sales pitch;
    • one question you would rather ask a working professional in the field.
  2. Gemini is made by Google, a company that sells AI. Write one sentence on how that might shape its answer.

Part 4: Give it better directions

A stronger prompt tells Gemini four things: a role (who it should act as), a task (what to do), context (who it is for and why) and a format (how the answer should look). Example: You are a hiring manager at a small design studio in Phoenix. List the skills you look for in a new junior designer, and say honestly where AI skills rank among them. I am a first-year community college student. Use a short ranked list.

  1. Rewrite your prompt using all four parts, and add one detail about yourself: the kind of work you want to do. Press Enter.
  2. Copy the new prompt and answer into your Doc. Write one sentence on how the answer changed.

Part 5: Reflect

  1. In four to six sentences: Did either answer change how you think about AI and your career? Where might a tool like this help you in your own work, and where would you not want to use it?

Part 6: Start your AI-use log

  1. At the bottom of your Doc, add a table with three columns: Tool, What I asked, and What I kept, changed or rejected. Add one row for this assignment. Every assignment in this course has a row like this, so your instructor can see how you used AI and what was your own.

Submit the link to your Google Doc.

Grading (10 points): critic’s mark-up 3; revised prompt with your own detail 3; reflection 2; AI-use log row 2.

Overview

A brand is what people expect from an organization. An identity system is the set of visuals and words that shapes those expectations. Designers do not start with a logo. They start with questions. In this module, you see how professionals document an identity. Then you research your client and write a creative brief: a short plan that every later design choice is checked against.

Client Packet A: Saguaro Ridge Coffee Roasters

Fictitious client. This business was created for the course. For the Client Research Notebook, research real competitors in the same field.
ClientSaguaro Ridge Coffee Roasters. Owner: Dana Ruiz.
BusinessA small-batch coffee roaster in Glendale, Arizona, founded six years ago. Sells whole-bean coffee online and at two farmers markets, and supplies four local cafés. Plans to open a walk-up tasting bar next year.
AudiencePrimary: local coffee drinkers aged 25 to 45 who buy specialty coffee and care where it comes from. Secondary: café owners who buy wholesale.
GoalsLook like a serious specialty roaster rather than a hobby; build recognition before the tasting bar opens; work as well on a bag label as on a phone screen.
ConstraintsTwo-color printing on kraft paper bags to keep cost down; small labels (2 by 3 inches); no images of cacti or sunsets, which every local competitor uses.
Drone imagery locationThe future walk-up tasting bar. The instructor names an open area on campus as the stand-in for supervised drone flights.
DeliverablesLogo and lockups, color palette and type, bag label system for six coffees, business card, and a brand guidelines book for the staff and the label printer.
Brand notesWarm, knowledgeable, unpretentious. The owner likes hand-drawn letterforms and the color of roasted beans. The current logo is a clip-art coffee cup.

Step 1: Write rules a machine can follow

A rule like “keep it clean” cannot be checked. A rule like “the headline is 6 words or fewer” can. Write every rule with a measurable value. Examples:

  • Headline: Headline/H1 style, 6 words or fewer, top third of the frame.
  • Logo: Full color version on light backgrounds, Reversed on dark; bottom right; 96 pixels wide; clear space equal to the height of the x.
  • Color pairs allowed: Navy text on Paper, White text on Navy. Never Accent text on Paper (fails contrast).
  • Photos: one subject, natural light, subject on the left half so the text block sits on the right.
  • Voice: plain, warm words; no exclamation points; say “sliding-scale fee,” never “cheap.”
  • Copy: caption of one to three sentences; one call to action per piece; every launch post uses one of the key messages.
  • Never: stretched logo, more than two typefaces, text over a busy part of a photo.

Check every allowed color pair with the WebAIM Contrast Checker before it goes in the rules.

Step 2: Put the rules into the spreadsheet

  1. Make a Google Sheet for your content. Row 1 holds the column names. If you use Figma or Buzz, use the exact layer names from your template, such as Headline, Body, CTA and Photo. Add columns for Caption, Alt text, Image credit and License.

  • At least 5 of the photos are yours: you shot them with a phone or camera.
  • Everything matches the imagery section of your guidelines book (subject, light, color, mood).

Route 2: a free stock photo site

  1. Pick one free site from the table below and read its license.
  2. Write five search terms from your book’s imagery section (subject, light, color, mood).
  3. Search the site yourself, or ask your image scout agent for candidate links. Gemini can give wrong or broken links, so open every one.
  4. Skip anything labeled AI-generated, anything that shows another business’s logo, and any use of a recognizable person that the license does not allow.
  5. Download the photo, or copy its direct image link for Figma Buzz, and add a log row.
  6. Your brand checker reviews every chosen photo against your photo rules.

Figma Community also has plugins that place photos from free sites into a frame. If you use one, check that it is free and log every photo the same way.

Your own shoot (Route 1)

  1. Write a shot list before you shoot: a table with shot number, subject, framing (wide, medium or close), orientation (vertical or horizontal), and which piece it is for. Plan vertical shots for stories and the motion piece.
  2. Shoot in good light, usually daylight from a window or outdoors in shade. Hold the phone steady or lean on something. Shoot each item vertical and horizontal.
  3. For video, record 10 to 20 seconds of each shot so you can trim to the best 5 to 15. Keep the phone still or move slowly.
  4. If a person can be recognized, get a signed release before you use the image: a form in which the person agrees to the use. A person under 18 needs a parent or guardian to sign. Use the release form in Canvas. If you cannot get a release, shoot hands, backs, silhouettes or objects instead. Background: Model release.

Folders and the log

  1. In your Maricopa Google Drive, make a folder named “AVC 283 Image Bank.” Inside it, make five folders: 01 Own Photos, 02 Licensed Photos, 03 Video, 04 Logo and Graphics, 05 Releases.
  2. Name every file the same way: client, subject, orientation, number. For example: desertdash_trail-start_v_01.jpg (v for vertical, h for horizontal).
  3. Make a Google Sheet named “Image Bank Log” in the same folder. Use these columns: File name; Source (own, Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay, Adobe Stock, client); Creator (photographer); Link to the source page; License; Recognizable people (yes or no); Release on file (yes, no, not needed); Alt text; Orientation; Size in pixels; Used in.
  4. Fill one row for every file the day you add it, including stock photos your system picked. A file with no row does not go in any design. Copy the source, creator and license into your content sheet’s Image credit and License columns.

Image specifications

  • Print: 300 pixels per inch at the size the image prints. A photo printed 8 inches wide needs to be at least 2400 pixels wide.
  • Social and screens: at least the size of the frame. A 1080 by 1920 story needs a photo at least 1080 by 1920 pixels.
  • Formats: JPG for photos; PNG for graphics that need a transparent background; SVG for the logo in Figma; MP4 for video.
  • Keep the original file. Crop and color-correct copies, never the original.

Scheduling Your Posts

A scheduled campaign posts itself on the dates in your calendar. You can schedule for real, or hand in a calendar that is ready to schedule. Both earn full credit.

  1. Make the content calendar in a Google Sheet, one row per post, with these columns: Post number; Date; Time; Platform; Phase; Format; Headline; Body; CTA (call to action); Caption; Hashtags; Image (Drive link or direct link); Image credit; License; Alt text; Status.
  2. Pick the dates: three weeks to three months, at a steady rhythm (for example, three posts a week).
  3. Choose how to schedule:
    • Meta Business Suite (free) for a Facebook Page and a connected Instagram account. Facebook posts can be scheduled up to about 29 days ahead, so schedule the first few weeks and keep the rest in the calendar.
    • Buffer, free plan: up to three channels and ten scheduled posts per channel at a time.
    • No public posting: set every row’s Status to “Ready to schedule” and hand in the calendar.
  4. Only schedule to an account you own, or one the client has said yes to in writing. If you make a test page for a fictitious client, say in its bio that it is a class project, and remove it after grading.
  5. When a post is scheduled, paste its alt text into the platform’s alt text field and set its Status to “Scheduled.”

Sizes: feed posts 1080 by 1350 pixels; stories 1080 by 1920 (keep text and the logo away from the top and bottom edges, where app buttons cover the screen); optional square posts 1080 by 1080. Platforms change their sizes, so check each platform’s help page before you export. Alt text for social posts describes what matters in one or two sentences and includes any words in the image.

Getting Started with Figma

Figma is a design tool that runs in a web browser (or as a desktop app). Designers use it for screens, websites, social posts and design systems, and teams work in the same file at once. If you choose a Figma or Figma Buzz approach, you build your brand as a set of reusable parts, then turn those parts into a generator.

Three words you will see a lot:

  • Frame: a box that holds a design, like an artboard in Illustrator. Each post or screen is one frame.
  • Layer: each thing inside a frame (a text box, a photo, the logo). The Layers panel lists them.
  • Component: a reusable part. Change the main component and every copy of it updates.

You already know frames, text and images from the Figma Warm-Up in Module 3. Start at step 1; steps 3 to 5 are quick review, and the new skills start at step 6.

Part 1: Check your account

  1. Sign in at figma.com with the Maricopa account you made in the warm-up. Education status lasts for a set term. If Figma asks you to reapply, follow Verify your education status. If you have education status, create an education team named “AVC 283 your name.”

If you are on the free Starter plan (for example, you are under 18 or verification failed), Starter allows three files in your team and does not publish libraries (Figma plans and features). That is enough for the brand generator: build your whole system and your templates in one file, and skip the “publish the library” step. Tell your instructor through Canvas Inbox so the rubric is applied the same way.

Part 2: Start your brand file

  1. Create a new Design file. Name it “Client name Brand System.”

Part 3: Make your first template frame

  1. Press F for the Frame tool. In the right sidebar, type 1080 for W (width) and 1350 for H (height). This is one feed post. Rename the frame “Feed post.” For a website, make the frame 1440 wide instead, and a second one 390 wide for phones. Name every layer you make; the generator finds layers by name.
  2. Press R and drag a rectangle across the top half of the frame. Rename it “Photo.” Drag a photo from your image bank, or a stock photo you logged, onto the rectangle to fill it. The photo becomes the rectangle’s fill, so it stays inside the shape.
  3. Press T, click under the photo and type a headline. Rename the layer “Headline.” Add a second text layer named “Body.”
  4. Bring in your logo. In Illustrator, choose File, Export, Export As, and save the logo as SVG. Drag the SVG file into Figma and place it in the frame. Rename it “Logo.”
  5. If your brand typeface is not in Figma’s font list, install the Figma desktop app, which can use fonts installed on your computer. If it still does not appear, use the closest Google Font and write the swap into your rules.

Part 4: Build the brand system

Now turn your guidelines book into parts Figma can reuse. Keep your book open beside you and copy values exactly.

  1. Color variables. A variable is a named value you can reuse, like “Brand Navy = #1D2B4F.” Click an empty spot on the canvas so nothing is selected. In the right sidebar, open Variables (under Local variables). Create a collection named “Brand.” Add one color variable for each color in your book, named the way your book names them (for example, color/primary, color/accent, color/paper). Type the HEX value from the book. Guide: Guide to variables in Figma.
  2. Number variables. In the same collection, add number variables for spacing from your grid (for example, space/margin = 64, space/gap = 24).
  3. Text styles. Select your headline. Set the font, size, line height and letter spacing from your book. In the Typography section of the right sidebar, click the styles icon, then the plus sign, and name the style (for example, Headline/H1). Repeat for each level in your book’s hierarchy: H1, H2, body, caption, button. Guide: Guide to styles in Figma.
  4. Apply variables. Select each layer and change its fill to a variable instead of a typed color: click the fill, open the libraries icon, and pick the variable. From now on, if a color changes, it changes everywhere.
  5. Logo component. Select the logo and press Command, Option and K (Mac) or Control, Alt and K (Windows) to make it a component. Duplicate it twice. Make one reversed (white) and one single color. Make each a component, select all three, and click Combine as variants in the right sidebar. Name the property “Version” with the values Full color, Reversed and One color. Guides: Guide to components and Create and use variants.
  6. Auto layout. Select the Headline and Body layers and press Shift and A. Auto layout stacks layers and spaces them by rule, so a longer headline pushes the body down instead of overlapping it. Set the gap and padding with your number variables. Rename the group “Text block.” Guide: Guide to auto layout.
  7. Template component. Select the whole Feed post frame and make it a component. Build the other sizes you need (1080 by 1920 and 1080 by 1080) the same way, reusing the logo component, the text styles and the variables.
  8. Write the rules into the parts. Select each component and type its rules into the description field (for example, “Logo: bottom right, 96 pixels wide, clear space all around equal to the height of the x”). Anyone who uses the part sees the rule. Guide: Add descriptions to styles, components, and variables.
  9. Publish the library (education plan only). Open the Assets tab in the left sidebar, open the library settings, and publish. Your components, styles and variables can now be used in other files, including Figma Buzz. Guide: Publish a library.

If you get stuck: post a screenshot in the AVC 283 Discord channel with the step number. Screen-reader users: Figma’s canvas is hard to use with a screen reader. Send a Canvas Inbox message in Week 13. Vibe coding with Gemini or InDesign Data Merge are good approaches for you, and your instructor will help set one up.

AI-Assisted Coding with Gemini

“Vibe coding” means you describe what you want in plain words, and the AI writes the code. You stay the designer: you write the spec and the rules, Gemini writes the code, and you test and fix it. Use your Maricopa account.

  1. Write a one-page spec in a Google Doc: what the generator makes; the pages or piece sizes; the sections; and your brand rules as exact values (HEX colors, Google Fonts names, type sizes, spacing, the logo as an SVG file, and image links).
  2. Build the code helper agent from Planning Your Agents, with your spec and rules document attached.
  3. Ask it for one page, or one piece, first. Gemini shows the result in a Canvas panel beside the chat.
  4. Test it: check it at phone width, check every color pair with the WebAIM Contrast Checker, and click every link. Write each problem in your process log.
  5. Tell Gemini one fix at a time, in plain words, for example: “The headline must use 48 pixels, Lora, color #1D2B4F.” Keep a short change log.
  6. Ask Gemini to build the repeating part from a data list at the top of the code. Paste rows from your sheet into the list. Adding a row should add a card, a post or a page section.
  7. To publish a website: from the Gemini Canvas panel, choose Export, then Google Sites, as in From Gemini to a Web Page. Or, in Google Sites, choose Insert, then Embed, then Embed code, and paste the code (Google Sites Help). Publish as Restricted and add your instructor’s email.
  8. Check each published page with the WAVE browser extension.

Gemini can write code that looks right and still breaks. Test everything, and keep every version of your spec so you can explain your choices.

From Gemini to a Web Page

You can turn something you make in Gemini into a real web page, with no code. You publish your Final Project Part B case study on Google Sites the same way.

Part 1: Make the App

  1. Open your web browser (Chrome works best).
  2. Go to gemini.google.com.
  3. Look at the top right corner. Make sure it shows your Maricopa account (your MEID@maricopa.edu). If it shows a personal account, click the picture and switch to your Maricopa account.
  4. Click in the box at the bottom that says “Ask Gemini.”
  5. Type this, then press Enter: “Make an interactive quiz with 5 questions about brand identity design. Make it a working app I can click.”
  6. Wait. Gemini builds the app in a panel on the right side of the screen.
  7. Click through your quiz to make sure it works.
  8. If something is wrong, type what to fix in the box (for example, “Make the buttons bigger”) and press Enter.

Part 2: Send It to Google Sites

  1. Look at the top of the app panel for the Export button. Click Export.
  2. Click Google Sites.
  3. A new tab opens with a Google Sites draft. Your app is already on the page. This draft is private; only you can see it.
  4. Click the title at the top of the page and type a name for your page.
  5. Click below the app and type one sentence about what your app does.

Part 3: Publish and Share

  1. Click the blue Publish button in the top right corner.
  2. Type a short web address for your site (for example, your-name-quiz). Click Publish.
  3. Click Share. Next to Published site, choose Restricted, so only people you add can see it. Add your instructor’s email.
  4. Click the arrow next to Publish and click Copy published site link.
  5. Go to the AVC 283 Discord channel. Paste the link with a screenshot of the page, and press Enter. Classmates you have not added see the screenshot.

If you get stuck: post a screenshot in the AVC 283 Discord channel with the step number. Screen-reader users: the Export menu and the Publish button are labeled buttons. If a step does not read clearly, post in Discord or send a Canvas Inbox message, and another way will be set up.

Good to know: your site follows the district’s sharing rules, so people outside Maricopa may not be able to open it. Gemini may build how the app works, but every design choice on your page is yours: words, colors and images. Take Your Work With You

Ungraded. Before the course ends.

Your Maricopa Google account may close after you leave the college. Do this before the course ends, so you keep everything you made even if you stop out, transfer or graduate. It takes about 45 minutes.

  1. Save your files in one place. Make one Google Drive folder for this course. Put in it your InDesign guidelines book and its package, Illustrator and Photoshop files, your generator files (Figma files, code, sheets and process log), exported PDFs and images, the Acrobat reports and your presentation and your AI-use log. For each Figma file, choose File, then Save local copy, to download a .fig copy.
  2. Copy everything to a personal Google account. Make a personal Google account if you do not have one. First make your own copy (File, then Make a copy) of any file someone shared with you, because files you do not own are not copied. Then, signed in to your Maricopa account, open Google Takeout Transfer and copy your Drive and Gmail to your personal account (see Google’s help page). It can take up to a week, and the copies arrive unshared. If Transfer is not available on your account, use Google Takeout to download your Drive as .zip files and save them on your computer.
  3. Take your AI work with you. Save anything you made with Gemini: your Agent Instructions Doc, the prompts from your AI-use log, the case-study edits Gemini suggested, and the code of any Gemini Canvas app. The instructions and source files are what rebuild it, so keep each one’s instructions in a Google Doc and its sources in your course folder. To keep your chats, use Google Takeout: choose My Activity, then Gemini Apps. After you leave, rebuild it in Gemini with your personal account: make a new one, paste the instructions and upload the sources.
    • Know the privacy difference. Your Maricopa account keeps your Gemini work out of AI training (see Module 0). A personal account does not have the same protections, and Google may use your activity to improve its AI, depending on your settings. In your personal account, open Gemini, then Settings & help, then Activity (Gemini Apps Activity), and decide whether to turn off Keep Activity. Never put anyone else’s private information into a personal account.
  4. Move your Google Site. Takeout Transfer copies Drive, Gmail and Photos. Do not count on it to move your case-study site. First keep the content: put the site’s text in a Google Doc and its images in your course folder, then open each published page and save it as a PDF (File, then Print, then Save as PDF). Then try a direct copy: in the site editor, choose Share and add your personal email as an editor. Sign in with your personal account, open the site, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing outside Maricopa is blocked, rebuild the site in your personal account at sites.google.com with the same content and publish it again. The web address changes, so update it on your resume and profiles.
  5. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
