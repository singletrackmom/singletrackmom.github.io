AVC 283 Graphic Design and Digital Publishing II

Courses and Programs · Case study

DesignCourse

The second course in the graphic design sequence. Open the full student course. Students build a visual identity system for a client, document it in an accessible brand guidelines book, design and test a brand generator that applies the brand, and present the work as a portfolio case study.

Goal

Move students from a single logo to a governed brand system, from a single layout to a long, styled, accessible publication, and from a finished brand to a system that applies it. The brand projects build on each other: the brief becomes the mark, the mark becomes the identity system, the system becomes the brand guidelines book, and the book drives a brand generator. Typography, layout and production exercises run alongside.

The course is AI-forward. Gemini supports research, writing and critique throughout, plays the client’s marketing team in the brand generator, and works inside agents students define themselves. AI never designs the brand and never makes images; every mark is drawn by the student in vector.

Audience

Second-year Digital Media Arts students who have completed AVC 183 Graphic Design and are building toward a professional portfolio.

Process

Students choose one of three fictional client packets (a coffee roaster, a trail running club, and a bilingual community health clinic), each with an audience, goals, real production constraints, deliverables and an email from the client. An instructor-arranged real client is an optional alternative, with a no-penalty return to a packet if the client goes silent. Constraints such as two-color kraft labels, one-color screen printing and photocopied bilingual flyers make the design problem authentic rather than open-ended.

Production skills employers test for (styles, parent pages, anchored objects, accessible tagging) are taught on a document the student authored, not on isolated drills, and each phase ends in a presentation.

Outcomes

Six course competencies, each mapped to module objectives written with the revised Bloom’s taxonomy and to criteria in the projects. A student can:

  1. Develop visual identity systems through logo design and brand applications.
  2. Apply advanced typography, composition, and color techniques to graphic design projects.
  3. Use advanced digital design and publishing tools for complex layouts, styles, and document organization.
  4. Produce professional graphic design materials for print and digital applications.
  5. Create multi-page publications incorporating advanced layout techniques, technical document features, and interactive elements.
  6. Evaluate graphic design projects for visual effectiveness, accessibility, technical quality, and portfolio presentation.

Deliverable options in development with the STEAMworks XR lab include an NFC brand object, a laser-cut packaging prototype, an AR layer on the guidelines book and drone imagery of the client’s location, each with an equal path for online students and the same rubric.

Pacing and interaction

About eight hours a week, about 123.5 hours in all, across sixteen modules (Module 0 to Module 15). Two noon deadlines a week; process steps and projects are posted to Discord the day before for critique in a fixed three-part format (a strength, an issue and its location, a suggestion tied to a design principle). Process-step feedback returns within two to three days, and any process step may be resubmitted once within seven days of feedback.

AI in This Course

Gemini and Gemini Notebook are the only AI tools, used with the Maricopa (Google Workspace for Education) account, under which student data is not reviewed by people, not used to train models and not used for ads. Gemini writes, questions and checks; it never makes a mark, image, moodboard image, mockup or any other visual. In the brand generator, students build Gemini skills (saved instructions with files) as agents with one job each.

How AI Supports Learning Here

  • Grounded research with verification. A source-grounded notebook speeds discovery, and checking its answers against the originals builds source evaluation into the brief.
  • AI as critic, designer as decision-maker. Gemini’s small-size critique is one input; the log records which suggestions the student accepted and which were rejected, making design judgment visible.
  • AI as tutor for technical production. Explanations of GREP and text patterns support the transfer of InDesign skills, with the student’s own test as the evidence.
  • Agents students define. In the brand generator, each student writes an agent plan (role, inputs, outputs, what the agent must never do, and how its work is checked) and builds at least a client content agent and a brand checker.
  • Verify and revise. The AI Primer in Module 0 has students question a Gemini answer like a critic, rewrite the prompt with role, task, context and format, and start an AI-use log. AI in This Course covers the costs of AI, including energy use (data centers drew about 1.5% of global electricity in 2024, per the International Energy Agency).
  • AI-use log. One log all semester, one row per work session (date, tool, prompt, what it produced, what was kept or changed and why); “No AI used” is a complete entry.

Guardrails

  • Account and data. Maricopa account only, under the district’s Gemini for Education terms. The instructor never places student work in a public AI tool.
  • Never AI-made. Every logo is drawn by the student in vector, with the construction file submitted. Every image is a client photograph, the student’s own photograph or illustration, or an openly licensed image, credited. In the generator, AI may select real photos from a free stock site; it never makes them.
  • Fictional means fictional. Each client packet is labeled as fictitious; students research real competitors only for analysis.
  • Students under 18. Where a feature is blocked, each assignment names an equivalent (for example, research in a Google Doc with GCC Library databases in place of Gemini Notebook), and no grade depends on access to a specific AI feature.

What Students Build

  • A client research notebook. A Gemini Notebook grounded in the client packet and competitor sources, used to inform the creative brief.
  • A brand generator. A small brand website (3 to 5 pages), a scheduled social media campaign (a few weeks to three months), or an approved idea of the same size, built from the student’s own rules, content sheet and agents, with a demo video and a process log.
  • A brand guidelines book. A styled, interactive and tagged InDesign publication, exported as a print PDF/X and an accessible interactive PDF, written so that a printer, staff member or future volunteer could apply the brand.

The Brand Generator

In Weeks 13 to 15, students build a brand generator: a system that applies their brand to new content. This is where experimentation comes in. Each student sets a challenge (a small brand website, a scheduled social campaign, or an approved idea of their own), defines the agents it needs, and prototypes at least two approaches on a small test batch, choosing from Figma templates with a sheet, Figma Buzz, a generator vibe-coded with Gemini, a scheduled social calendar, InDesign Data Merge or their own method. They pick one direction with a rationale, build it, run it, critique every piece against their own rules and against “usable at a job without a designer fixing it,” revise the rules and run it again.

The inputs are the brand book’s visual rules, the brand’s written side (audience, key messages, voice, tagline) and real images from the student’s own bank or a free stock site. A client content agent writes the copy and a brand checker reviews each piece; the student edits every word and scores every piece. Grading weights the process (prototypes, tests, rationale, iteration) over polish, and the brand book stays the center of the course.

How to Adapt This

  1. Write realistic client packets. Fictional clients with real production constraints give every student a bounded problem, and the packet becomes the source of truth for the client content agent.
  2. Make AI critique accountable. Require the student to log which AI suggestions were rejected and why, not only which were used.
  3. Grade the generator’s process. Weight prototypes, test results, the rationale for the chosen direction and the second run more than the polish of the final batch.

  • Gemini skills and Gemini Canvas: the agents students define for the generator, and code written from a student’s spec.
  • Google Sheets: the generator’s content sheet or posting calendar.
  • Meta Business Suite or Buffer (optional): scheduling a social campaign; a calendar marked ready to schedule earns full credit.
  • Google Slides or Adobe Express: the client presentation deck.
  • Google Docs and Drive: the creative brief, kickoff email and the semester-long AI-use log.
  • Google Sites: the published portfolio case study, Restricted by default (only invited people can see it) and public when the student chooses. A student who already has a Behance portfolio may use it instead.

  • Discord: the course channel on the Digital Media Arts server for questions and critique.

How the Course Was Tested

Before launch, the course went through synthetic student walk-throughs, a quality-rubric review, and an alignment and AI-exposure audit. No real students were involved in testing. Every course in the program goes through the same build and review method.

Synthetic Student Walk-Throughs

The testers are synthetic students: AI agents, each built from one persona in the Student Journey Barriers Study. Each agent worked through the course module by module as that student and logged every point where it got stuck or could not finish. A fixer pass applied the findings, and a second round re-ran all eight agents against the revised course.

Quality and Alignment Reviews

The course was reviewed against the SUNY OSCQR online course quality rubric, and every objective was audited against the revised Bloom’s taxonomy (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001) so that each sits at or below its parent competency and is measured by a graded item. Evaluation, the highest competency, is practiced in every critique and assessed in Project 1 and both parts of the final project.

Every Assessment Checked for AI Resistance

Every graded assessment was audited for AI exposure, asking whether a student could hand the task to an AI and pass. The design answers are process evidence (sketches, construction files and screenshots on every step), work tied to the student’s own client and brand, recorded explanation, peer critique on Discord, the AI-use log, and the rule that Gemini never designs.

Accessibility

Designed with Universal Design for Learning to WCAG 2.1 AA: captioned instructor video with corrected captions, accessible color in every palette, and laptop and hotspot loans for any student without a computer or reliable internet. Student work carries hand-corrected captions, corrected transcripts, alt text and tagged PDFs, worth about 10 percent of each media rubric, and the guidelines book itself must pass an accessibility check, so students learn tagging and reading order on their own work.

Status

Designed and fully built: sixteen modules (Module 0 to Module 15), nineteen process steps, two graded discussions, two projects, a two-part final project and three client packets.
