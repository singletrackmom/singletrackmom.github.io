AVC 283 Graphic Design and Digital Publishing II

Courses and Programs · Case study

Course designThe full course

The second course in the graphic design sequence. Open the full student course. Students build a complete visual identity system for a client, document it in an accessible brand guidelines book, and present the whole process as a portfolio case study.

Goal

Take students from a single logo to a governed brand system, and from a single layout to a long, styled, accessible publication: the two skills a working designer needs most.

Audience

Students enrolled in AVC 283, usually in their second year of Digital Media Arts, who have taken AVC 183 Graphic Design and are building toward a professional portfolio.

Process

One client carries the whole term, in three phases. Every step is posted for Discord critique before it is due, and each phase ends in a presentation.

PhaseWhat students build
IdentityResearch and a creative brief, sixty thumbnails, three logo directions, a refined mark, an accessible palette and type system, stationery and collateral, and a client presentation
PublicationA brand guidelines book in InDesign: type specimen, grids and parent pages, paragraph and character styles, images and anchored objects, interactive features, and accessibility tagging
PortfolioA portfolio case study and recorded presentation, followed by a final revision

One client across the whole term means every phase builds on the last: the identity becomes the content of the guidelines book, and the book becomes the centerpiece of the portfolio case study. The publication phase teaches the production skills employers test for (styles, parent pages, anchored objects, accessible tagging) on a document the student cares about, rather than on drills alone. The course ends where professional practice begins: a presentation to the client and a case study written for a hiring manager.

Outcomes

A student can:

  1. Develop visual identity systems through logo design and brand applications.
  2. Apply advanced typography, composition, and color techniques to graphic design projects.
  3. Use advanced digital design and publishing tools for complex layouts, styles, and document organization.
  4. Produce professional graphic design materials for print and digital applications.
  5. Create multi-page publications incorporating advanced layout techniques, technical document features, and interactive elements.
  6. Evaluate graphic design projects for visual effectiveness, accessibility, technical quality, and portfolio presentation.

Pacing and interaction

Two deadlines a week. Everything is due at noon, Arizona time, never midnight; late work is accepted for 24 hours at 10% off, and nothing after noon the day after. Projects and project steps are posted to the class Discord by noon the day before they are due, with three critique comments by the due time; exercises, quizzes, readings and reflections have no Discord step.

How success is measured

Objectives are written with the revised Bloom’s taxonomy (Anderson and Krathwohl), and every assessment was checked for AI exposure so the graded evidence is process, critique, recorded explanation or design rationale. Sixteen process steps carry the process; the identity system, the guidelines book and the portfolio case study carry the outcome, each with one rubric criterion per competency it evidences. Students submit construction files for every mark and an AI-use log with every assignment, and the final presentation asks them to defend the system, not just show it.

Before launch, the course was checked against the SUNY OSCQR online course quality rubric and walked through by eight synthetic student agents, built from the Student Journey Barriers Study personas, in two rounds of testing.

Access

Designed for learner variability with Universal Design for Learning and built to WCAG 2.1 AA: captioned video, accessible color in every palette, and a district device request for any student without a computer. Every piece of student work is accessible too: captions on video (automatic captions, then corrected by hand), corrected transcripts for audio, alt text on images, and tagged documents, all graded at about 10 percent of each media rubric. Every instructor video is captioned and corrected the same way. The guidelines book itself must pass an accessibility check, so students learn tagging and reading order on their own work.

Technology

  • Adobe Creative Cloud. Illustrator for the mark and identity, InDesign for the guidelines book, Photoshop for imagery, and Acrobat for accessibility tagging.
  • AI as a studio partner. Gemini, with the Maricopa account, writes and never designs. Gemini Notebook holds the client research; Gemini critiques a mark at small sizes, drafts brand voice options, explains InDesign text patterns, and drafts alt text the student checks. Students make every image, moodboard and mockup themselves. Every use goes in a short AI-use log.
  • Marks are drawn by hand. Every logo is student-drawn vector work with its construction file as evidence, because AI-generated marks cannot be owned or defended by a client.

Where the course is going

The next step is a brand generator. Students build the brand, write its rules into a generator (Gemini, a Figma component library, or a small web tool), let it produce a batch of posters or social posts, and critique the batch against their own rules. The designer’s job moves toward making the brand and judging what a machine produces.

Status

Offered every spring.
