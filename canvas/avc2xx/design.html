UX Design for Interactive Media

Courses and Programs · Case study

Course designThe course

One UX course for multiple disciplines. Students from game design, animation, sound design, graphic design, and other creative programs learn the same UX method, then apply it to an original capstone in their own field.

Goal

Give second-year students from very different programs a shared UX practice: research the people they design for, structure and prototype an experience, test it with real users, and revise. The competencies name abilities, not software or devices, so a game designer, an animator, and a sound designer can each meet them with the tools of their own field.

Audience

Second-year students across the district’s creative programs. Most arrive with strong skills in their own medium and no UX background, so the first half of the course teaches the fundamentals before anyone starts the capstone. Prerequisites in design, web, game, programming, or music keep first-semester students out.

Process

I wrote the first draft by backward design (Wiggins and McTighe), starting from what a second-year student should be able to make at the end of the term. A cross-discipline curriculum team then rebuilt it so it would serve every program equally: the original eleven competencies, written for one digital media audience, became seven written around abilities, and emerging technology moved into the outline so it shapes the content without becoming the only way to pass.

How success is measured

Every graded task is authentic and anchored to the student’s own work. Points sit on the research, the reasoning, and the response to testing, not only on the finished piece. The capstone ends in a defense: a model can generate a screen or a sound, but it cannot account for a term of the student’s own design decisions.

Access

Designed for learner variability from the start with Universal Design for Learning, and to WCAG 2.1 AA. Accessibility is also course content: every student reviews their own work for inclusive design before it is tested. Every assignment has a sound path and a non-visual path, so a blind, low-vision, Deaf or hard-of-hearing student can complete the whole course; rubrics grade reasoning, research and testing, not visual polish, and accommodations are arranged with Disability Resources and Services before the first project.

Technology

Students use the tools of their own field. The district’s Gemini and Adobe Creative Cloud come first; Figma, game engines such as Unity and Unreal, Blender, audio tools such as Audition, Reaper and Audacity, and browser-based AR are all in bounds. AI-assisted development lets a student who has never coded build a functional prototype, and every student reviews AI output for accuracy, accessibility, and bias before it ships.

I build the course with my own course-builder skill, and a panel of agents reviews each draft from a different seat, so no single discipline’s view takes over. I direct the work and own every decision (AI-assisted course design).

Subject-matter advisors

UX, game design, and sound design faculty

Pressure-test each assignment against current practice in their field and flag anything that only works for screen-based or visual students.

Learning-design advisor

Instructional Designer, 20 years

Checks backward design and alignment, seat time against the credit hours, Regular and Substantive Interaction, and UDL.

Student tester

A second-year game or sound design student

Moves through each module as a learner from one of the disciplines most likely to be underserved, and flags where a step assumes a visual or web background.

No single open textbook spans these competencies, so the course uses a vetted mix, with licensing stated on every resource. It is built to the SUNY OSCQR online-course-quality rubric alongside the department’s alignment and workload criteria, with estimated seat hours on every module held to the credit-hour budget.
