UX Design for Interactive Media

Courses and Programs · Case study

Course designThe full course

One UX course for multiple disciplines. Students from game design, animation, sound design, graphic design, and other creative programs learn the same UX method, then apply it to an original capstone in their own field.

Goal

Give second-year students from very different programs a shared UX practice: research the people they design for, structure and prototype an experience, test it with real users, and iterate. The competencies name abilities, not software or devices, so a game designer, an animator and a sound designer each meet them with the tools of their own field.

Two commitments distinguish the course. Every assignment has a sound path and a full non-visual path graded on the same UX reasoning, so a blind, low-vision, Deaf or hard-of-hearing student can complete the entire course. And AI-assisted development lets a student who has never coded build a functional prototype, because Gemini may write code from the student’s own specification while every design decision stays with the student.

Audience

Second-year students across the district’s creative programs, most with strong skills in their own medium and no UX background. Prerequisites in design, web, game, programming or music keep first-semester students out.

Process

The course is backward-designed (Wiggins and McTighe) from what a second-year student should be able to make at the end of the term. A cross-discipline curriculum revision replaced eleven competencies written for one audience with seven written around abilities, and moved emerging technology into the outline so it shapes the content without becoming the only way to pass. The first half scaffolds the method through a small Studio Project; the second half transfers it to one original capstone in the student’s own discipline, tested twice with real people and defended.

Levels follow the revised Bloom’s taxonomy (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001); every module objective names its level and sits at or below its competency.

Pacing and interaction

Sixteen modules of about eight hours a week, each in the same order. Two noon deadlines a week, with process steps and projects posted to Discord the day before for peer critique. Office hours and capstone check-ins are optional live sessions, always recorded.

AI in This Course

Students use Gemini chat, Gemini Canvas, Gemini Live, Gemini Notebook and Gemini in Google Docs and Sheets, only with the Maricopa (Google Workspace for Education) account, under which their data is not reviewed by people, not used to train models and not used for ads. Gemini helps with research, writing, feedback, planning and code from the student’s own specification. It never designs, and no AI image, layout, audio or voice generation is used for design work.

How AI Supports Learning Here

  • AI as critic after the thinking. Students write first, then invite critique, then document which suggestions they rejected and why, citing a participant code for each. The sequence protects generative thinking and makes evaluative judgment assessable.
  • AI as developer, student as designer. A student-made specification (a style tile or comp, or on the non-visual path a text spec of structure, labels and focus order) must exist before any build. Gemini implements; the student tests.
  • AI as an object of evaluation. When a student chooses an AI-driven interaction method, they test it like any other technology, logging every trial and judging it for accuracy, accessibility, bias and ethics.
  • Rehearsal without risk. Gemini Live as a practice participant builds moderation skill before real sessions.
  • Verify and revise. The AI Primer in Module 0 has students prompt with role, task, context and format, revise twice, fact-check three claims against sources, and take a position on AI in their discipline, including its costs (data centers drew about 1.5% of global electricity in 2024, per the International Energy Agency). Sound design students verify claims about loudness targets or adaptive audio against documentation.
  • AI-use log. Every submission logs the tool, purpose, what was kept and what was changed; the final reflection asks where the student used AI and where they chose not to.

Guardrails

  • Account and data. Maricopa account only, under the district’s Gemini for Education terms. Saved custom versions of Gemini are not used. The instructor never places student work in a public AI tool.
  • Research ethics. Adult participants, a consent script before every session, de-identified notes (P1 to P3), and recordings deleted at the end of the course. Identifiable participant data never enters an AI tool, and AI never generates participants, quotes or results.
  • Never AI-made. Marks, layouts, sounds and visual decisions are the student’s; concept sketches are hand-made, and Gemini critiques the written concept only.
  • Students under 18. No assignment depends on features restricted for those accounts; a blocked step is replaced with an equivalent at no loss of points. A supervised research path (instructor-paired classmates, approved participants with a guardian present, or recorded public sessions) counts the same for every grade.

What Students Build

  • A functional capstone. Original, functional for the discipline, and integrating research, two test rounds and an emerging technology or interaction method. Browser builds from Gemini Canvas are published to Google Sites for testers.
  • Non-visual builds. On the sound and non-visual paths, a Web Audio build with code Gemini writes from the student’s text specification, a sonic AR walk, or a Twine audio flow stands in for visual tools.

How to Adapt This

  1. Require a specification before any AI build. A comp, style tile or text spec makes the student the designer and the AI the implementer, in any medium.
  2. Sequence AI critique after authorship. Have students submit the original, the AI critique, the revision and the rejected suggestions with reasons.
  3. Write a non-visual path for every task. Grade the reasoning, research and decisions, so a text, audio or tactile artifact can earn full credit.

How the Course Was Tested

Before launch, the course went through synthetic student walk-throughs, a quality-rubric review, and an alignment and AI-exposure audit. A synthetic review panel (UX, game design, sound design, animation and XR experts, an instructional designer, and second-year students in game, sound and graphic design) also reviewed the draft. No real students were involved in testing.

Synthetic Student Walk-Throughs

The testers are synthetic students: AI agents, each built from one persona in the Student Journey Barriers Study. Each agent worked through the course module by module as that student and logged every point where it got stuck or could not finish. A fixer pass applied the findings, and a second round re-ran all eight agents against the revised course.

Quality and Alignment Reviews

The course was built to the SUNY OSCQR online course quality rubric, with estimated seat hours on every module held to the credit-hour budget, and every objective was audited against the revised Bloom’s taxonomy so that each sits at or below its parent competency.

Every Assessment Checked for AI Resistance

Every graded assessment was audited for AI exposure with Oregon State University Ecampus’s Bloom’s Taxonomy Revisited, asking whether a student could hand the task to an AI and pass. The design answers are tests with real people, the student’s own captures, process evidence, peer critique on Discord, the AI-use log, the rule that Gemini never designs, and judgment defended aloud.

Accessibility

Designed with Universal Design for Learning to WCAG 2.1 AA. Every assignment names a sound path and a non-visual path that students may use without a special request, and rubrics on those paths grade UX reasoning, research, testing and decisions rather than visual polish. Instructor videos are captioned and audio described. Student work carries hand-corrected captions, transcripts or cue sheets, alt text and tagged documents, worth about 10 percent of each media rubric.

Status

Designed and fully built: sixteen modules, a Studio Project and a defended capstone, sound and non-visual paths on every assignment, and a Canvas import package.
