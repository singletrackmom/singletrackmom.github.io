UX Design for Interactive Media

Case study · Learning design · AI for learning

Competencies Course design The course Instructor kit

A new Digital Media Arts course, designed backward from industry competencies with an agent review panel, and built with the same course-build method behind the data-science demonstration.

Goal

Design an online UX Design course that takes a Digital Media Arts student from no UX background to a defensible portfolio case study, built to a formal quality standard and resistant to generative substitution, so the competency, not the artifact, is what gets assessed. The course is designed first at the competency level from advisory-board input, then built module by module through a review panel of synthetic experts before any real student sees it.

Audience

Community college Digital Media Arts students, many first-generation and new to college at a Hispanic-Serving Institution, most with a graphic-design or visual foundation and no prior UX. The course assumes design sensibility but no research, prototyping, or systems experience, and it is built so a student can carry the work straight into the portfolio they keep building across the program.

Process

The eleven course competencies come from the industry advisory board, which named UX design as the skill area where student preparation matters most. From those competencies the course is designed backward: outcomes, then the evidence that would prove them, then the activities that build toward that evidence. The build runs through a three-agent review panel, and every module is checked against a fixed quality standard and re-tested with a synthetic student before it is considered done. The advisors review the design several times as it develops, so it stays validated against what employers actually want.

The build panel

Three synthetic experts review every module from a different seat. None of them replace a human. They surface problems early so the human reviewer inherits a stronger draft, and the design line holds: nothing ships that a person has not reviewed.

Subject-matter expert

Senior UX Designer, 20 years at a Fortune 100 company

Owns current professional practice: real tool workflows, design systems at production scale, mobile and responsive conventions, how AI actually fits a working designer’s day, and what a hiring portfolio genuinely needs. Its job is to keep every module true to the profession and to flag anything that is academic but not real, so students learn the craft as it is practiced, not as a textbook imagines it.

Instructional designer

Instructional Designer, 20 years at a top-tier art and design university

Owns the learning design: backward design and constructive alignment, scaffolding, critique pedagogy, assessment that measures the stated competency, and accessibility and UDL. Its job is to make sure every outcome is actually taught and evidenced, that the workload fits the credit, and that a true beginner can follow the path without the tool becoming the reason they quit.

Student tester

GCC Digital Media student

A synthetic student grounded in the real population: often first-generation, balancing work and school, first exposure to UX. Its job is to move through each module as a learner and surface where instructions confuse, where cognitive load spikes, and where a step quietly assumes knowledge the student does not have yet. What it flags becomes the next revision.

How it is built

  1. Backward design and alignment. Start from the eleven competencies and the evidence that would prove each one, then design activities back from there. An alignment matrix is built as the course goes: every competency tied to graded evidence, nothing claimed that is never measured.
  2. Getting started. A Welcome and Getting Started module: how to move through the course, a findable syllabus with every required element, a real instructor introduction so the course does not read as authored by no one, and clear expectations in writing.
  3. Can a student use it. UDL and accessibility checked at build time: more than one way to engage and to demonstrate learning, content in more than one modality, real alt text, navigable headings, readable contrast, and a privacy link for every third-party tool.
  4. Presence and interaction. Regular and Substantive Interaction built into every week, not bolted on: an announcement and check-in rhythm, a Discord critique loop with post-by and reply-by cadence, and a stated feedback timeline.
  5. Assessment and AI-resistance. Every graded item faces one question, could a model do this correctly in under a minute, and if so it is rebuilt at the same cognitive level around the student’s own work. Interactive simulations carry the practice; a defended case study carries the summative.
  6. Finish and hand off. One solid-color palette and one typeface applied across the course so it reads as designed, an instructor prep kit with recording guides and the weekly RSI rhythm, an end-of-course survey and seat-time validation, and a reference library kept with the course.

Resources and licensing

There is no single UX open textbook that spans these competencies, so the course uses a vetted mix: openly-licensed and public-domain materials students can rely on freely, free-to-cite professional authorities for currency, and course-specific materials authored in-house. Licensing is stated on every resource, and the course-authored pieces are the connective tissue no open resource covers.

Assessment, and the interactive simulations

Assessment is authentic and resistant to generative substitution by design. The through-line project is the student’s own product, on a subject they choose, so no two are the same and a pasted model answer does not fit. Points sit on the process and the decisions, the research, the rationale, the response to critique, not only on the finished file.

Practice is carried by interactive simulations built into the modules, so students rehearse the judgment before it is graded:

  • Heuristic-evaluation simulator. Students inspect a working mock interface and flag issues against Nielsen’s ten heuristics, then compare their findings to an expert pass.
  • Usability-test role-play. Students run a task-based test against a synthetic participant who behaves like a real, sometimes confused, user, then synthesize what they observed.
  • Affinity-mapping and card-sort. Students cluster real research notes into themes to practice synthesis, the step beginners skip.
  • Spot-the-barrier accessibility check. Students find and name accessibility failures on a sample screen and state the fix.

The summative is a defended case study: the student presents their process and product in a recorded walkthrough, then answers live questions about why they made the decisions they made. A model can generate a screen; it cannot sit in the room and defend fifteen weeks of a student’s own choices.

Workload, accessibility, and standard

  • Seat time. Each module estimates its direct instruction, practice, and project hours, written into the module intro and held to the course credit-hour budget.
  • Accessibility and UDL. WCAG 2.1 AA from the first draft, not a retrofit; more than one way to engage and to demonstrate learning; video captioned and scripted; the recorded-presentation format so no student has to perform live in front of the whole class.
  • Quality standard. Built to the SUNY OSCQR rubric alongside the department’s alignment and workload criteria, the same standard applied across the portfolio. Not affiliated with or endorsed by SUNY.

Status

In design. The competencies are written from advisory-board input and are in the curriculum-proposal process. The course itself is being built through the method on this page. Everything here is honest about that: it is a design in progress, not a delivered course.
