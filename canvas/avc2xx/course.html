Course designThe course

UX Design for Interactive Media

AVC 2xx

One UX method, many disciplines. Students from game design, animation, AR/VR, sound design, graphic design and other creative programs learn the same UX method and practice it in their own medium. Module 1 is a short AI primer. Modules 2 to 8 build a small Studio Project, one step at a time: analyze, research, brief, flow, prototype, evaluate technology, test and revise. Modules 9 to 16 are one original, functional capstone in the student’s own discipline, tested twice with real people, presented to the class and defended.

Sound experience path: every assignment and the capstone can be done as a sound experience: an audio-first app or game interface, a sonic identity and notification (earcon) system, a voice or audio-guided tour, an accessible audio navigation experience, or spatial audio for AR or VR. Each module names what a sound student makes and submits at that step.

Seat time: M0 3h · M1 6h · M2 4h · M3 6.5h · M4 8h · M5 8h · M6 8h · M7 8h · M8 8h · M9 7.5h · M10 7.5h · M11 8h · M12 8h · M13 8h · M14 7.5h · M15 7.5h · M16 6.5h · Total 120 hours (3 credits: about 40 direct, 80 outside). Fifteen weeks, fully online.

Every week: two noon deadlines, Arizona time, never midnight. Late work is accepted for 24 hours at 10% off; nothing is accepted after noon the day after. Projects and project steps (Process Work and Projects) are posted to the Discord critique channel by noon the day before (Wednesday or Saturday), submitted in Canvas Thursday or Sunday with three critique comments, and close Friday or Monday; exercises, quizzes, readings and reflections have no Discord step and follow only the noon due time and the late rule. Week 1 holds Module 0 (Thursday) and Module 2 (Sunday); the AI Primer runs across Weeks 1 and 2 and is due Thursday of Week 2.

Resources. Every module has a short, captioned, course-authored video with worked examples from more than one discipline. Readings are open or free to read, with the license noted. Starter kits for Unity with FMOD, Web Audio (keyboard-driven, tested with screen readers), WebAR image tracking, model-viewer and Twine, plus a Gemini Canvas prompt guide, are course-authored and posted in Module 10.
Access. The course is designed so a blind, low-vision, Deaf or hard-of-hearing student, or one who works by keyboard, switch or voice, can complete every assignment and be graded on the same criteria. Each visual task names a non-visual equivalent (text or audio wireframes, structured outlines, audio flows, tactile mock-ups, prototypes tested with VoiceOver or NVDA, recorded verbal walkthroughs), and each sound task carries a cue sheet or transcript. Course videos are captioned and audio described, with transcripts. Critique posts carry alt text, transcripts and captions. On the sound and non-visual paths, rubrics grade the UX reasoning, research, testing and decisions, never visual polish. Accommodations are arranged with Disability Resources and Services before Module 2, and Access in This Course (Module 0) lists which tools work with a screen reader and the alternative for each.

AI in This Course course page

This course uses generative AI as part of the assignments. Our industry advisory board encourages us to teach current industry practice, and employers now expect graduates to work with AI. In Microsoft and LinkedIn’s Work Trend Index, 66% of business leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills.

Using AI here is not cheating. Cheating is turning in work you did not do as if you did. In this course, AI use is assigned, it is disclosed in an AI-use log with every assignment, and you are graded on your own thinking: your research, your decisions and your design work. Other courses may set different rules, and those rules apply in those courses.

Your work is not used to train AI. You will use Gemini with your Maricopa account. Under the district’s Google for Education agreement, what you enter is not reviewed by people, not used to train AI models, and not used for ads (Gemini for Education; Gemini for Google Workspace education FAQ). Always sign in with your Maricopa account, never a personal one. Your instructor will not put your work into any public AI tool.

AI does not make your work. Gemini helps with research, writing, feedback, planning and code from your own spec. It never designs: your marks, layouts, sounds and visual decisions are your own, and you will show your process. No AI image, layout, audio or voice generation for design work. Every submission carries a short AI-use log (tool, purpose, what you kept, what you changed).

You will learn to question it. AI makes confident mistakes and reflects bias in its training data. This course teaches you to check every answer and to think critically about what AI gets wrong and what it costs.

Overview

You are here because you want a career doing this work. The people who will hire you have already decided AI skills matter: in Microsoft and LinkedIn’s Work Trend Index, 66% of business leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills, and 71% said they would rather hire a less experienced candidate who has them. Three out of four knowledge workers already use AI on the job. Our industry advisors asked us to teach it. Leaving you without these skills would put you behind the people you will compete with.

Many of you have serious doubts about AI, and some of those doubts are right. AI is trained on artists’ work, often without permission. It makes confident mistakes. It reflects the biases in its training data. Data centers already use about 1.5% of the world’s electricity, and the International Energy Agency expects that to more than double by the end of the decade. This course does not hide those problems.

Here is the case for learning it anyway. Refusing AI does not slow it down; it only removes your voice from how it gets used. The designers who decide how AI is used in their studios are the ones who understand it well enough to be trusted. Employers also say creativity, communication and critical thinking matter more than ever, because AI cannot do those for you. This course keeps the creative work in your hands, teaches you to use AI for what it does well, and to catch it when it is wrong. You can leave as an informed critic; you cannot be one without knowing how it works.

Is this cheating? No. AI use here is assigned, it is disclosed in your AI-use log, and you are graded on your own thinking.

How your work is protected: use your Maricopa account only. What you enter is not reviewed by people, not used to train models and not used for ads (Gemini for Education; Workspace with Gemini FAQ for Education). The instructor never puts your work into a public AI tool.

Overview

Visual design asks how something looks. User experience design asks what it is like to use: whether people can understand it, do what they came to do, and want to come back. Every interactive medium has users. A player learns a control scheme, a viewer chooses a branch in an animated story, a listener recognizes a notification sound without looking, a shopper scans an AR poster, a visitor steps into a VR scene. This week you take apart one experience from your own field to see the decisions behind it. If you have already taken a web design and UX course, choose an experience that is not a website.

This week you learn the ten heuristics and how people perceive and process information (grouping, hierarchy, attention, memory limits), then apply both to your own field.

Heuristics in sound (course page)
HeuristicWhat it means for audio
1. Visibility of system statusEvery input gets an audible confirmation; states such as loading or low health have distinct cues
2. Match with the real worldSounds map to familiar meanings (rising for success, falling for error) or real sources
3. User control and freedomListeners can skip, mute or replay; separate volume for music, effects and voice
4. Consistency and standardsThe same action always makes the same sound family
5. Error preventionWarning cues before irreversible actions, distinct enough not to be confused
6. Recognition rather than recallEarcons are few, distinct and learnable, not twenty similar beeps
7. Flexibility and efficiencyExperienced users can shorten or turn off tutorial narration
8. Aesthetic and minimalist designOnly meaningful sounds play; the mix leaves room for the important cue
9. Recover from errorsError sounds are paired with a spoken or visual explanation
10. Help and documentationCues are explained once, in context, with a visual alternative

From Gemini to a web page

Setup, about 20 minutes, before the starter kit walkthrough below. You can turn something you make in Gemini into a real web page. You do not need to write any code.

Part 1: Make the app

  1. Open your web browser (Chrome works best).
  2. Go to gemini.google.com.
  3. Look at the top right corner. Make sure it shows your Maricopa account (your MEID@maricopa.edu). If it shows a personal account, click the picture and switch to your Maricopa account.
  4. Click in the box at the bottom that says “Ask Gemini.”
  5. Type this, then press Enter: “Make an interactive card sort with 8 cards about a campus app. Make it a working app I can click.”
  6. Wait. Gemini builds the app in a panel on the right side of the screen.
  7. Click through your app to make sure it works.
  8. If something is wrong, type what to fix in the box (for example, “Make the buttons bigger”) and press Enter.

Part 2: Send it to Google Sites

  1. Look at the top of the app panel for the Export button. Click Export.
  2. Click Google Sites.
  3. A new tab opens with a Google Sites draft. Your app is already on the page. This draft is private; only you can see it.
  4. Click the title at the top of the page and type a name for your page.
  5. Click below the app and type one sentence about what your app does.

Part 3: Publish and share

  1. Click the blue Publish button in the top right corner.
  2. Type a short web address for your site (for example, your-name-card-sort). Click Publish.
  3. Click the arrow next to Publish and click Copy published site link.
  4. Go to the class Discord channel. Paste the link and press Enter.

If you get stuck: take a screenshot and post it in the class Discord channel with the step number you were on. Screen-reader users: the Export menu and the Sites Publish button are labeled buttons; if a step does not read clearly, post in Discord or contact the instructor and an equivalent will be set up. Game designA playable build (desktop, web or headset) in which a first-time player completes one core loop without help
AnimationAn interactive animated piece in which every choice point works and leads to a complete path
Sound designAn audio system that responds to input or state in real time (middleware in an engine, a Web Audio build, or a sonic AR walk); a linear mix alone does not count
Graphic designA working interactive layer, such as an image-tracked AR trigger or live web experience, connected to the printed or spatial pieces
AR/VRAn AR or VR experience a first-time user can start, complete and exit without the designer’s help
App design and digital media artsAn app that completes the core task end to end; an installation or kiosk that responds to visitors without the designer operating it

A Wizard-of-Oz prototype is a valid prototype in Modules 11 and 12, but not a functional final unless the instructor approved it as your fallback.

Sound experience capstone

Any student may build the capstone as a sound experience: an audio-first app or game interface, a sonic identity and notification system, a voice or audio-guided tour, an accessible audio navigation experience, or spatial audio for AR or VR. Functional is defined in the table above. Recorded voice and sound are your own, reused or licensed; AI never generates audio or voice. It is graded with the same Capstone Final and Defense rubric, points and objectives:

CriterionWhat a sound capstone shows
Competency 1, analysis (16.3)Audio heuristic and accessibility analysis of the final build: a caption or cue sheet entry for every meaningful cue, volume, mute and replay controls
Competency 2, research and brief (9.2)Listening context, devices and participant evidence trace to the brief
Competency 3, technology evaluation (10.2)The audio technology is justified against alternatives, including device access and listener comfort
Competency 4, concept and pre-production (10.3)Sonic palette, earcon library, cue sheet and state table show how the concept developed
Competency 5, prototyping (11.1, 12.1)Prototypes rise from soundboard to responsive build, each answering a stated question
Competency 6, synthesis and iteration (14.1)Two rounds of listening tests drive changes, shown in before and after clips at matched loudness, cue identification rates and loudness readings
Competency 7, original functional project (16.1)The sound experience works for a first-time listener and is defended clearly

Defense for an audio project: the presentation is a captioned recording with system audio, or an audio piece with a transcript. Demonstrate the build responding live, with a cue sheet so Deaf and hard-of-hearing classmates can follow; play before and after clips at matched loudness; answer defense questions in audio or writing, citing cue identification data and listening-test findings.
