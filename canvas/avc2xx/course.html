How This Course Works

Welcome to UX Design for Interactive Media (AVC 2xx, 3 credits). UX means user experience: designing an experience around the people who use it. You learn one UX method and use it in your own medium, such as games, animation, sound, graphic design, AR/VR or apps.

The course is fully online and asynchronous: there are no required live sessions. Office hours and capstone check-ins are optional and recorded. Plan on about 8 hours a week, or 120 hours in all.

Module 0 sets you up and includes a short AI Primer. In Modules 1 to 7, you build a small Studio Project one step at a time. In Modules 8 to 14, you build one original, working capstone, test it twice with real people, then present and defend it.

Every module has the same parts in the same order: Objectives, Overview, Watch, Read, Practice, Assessment, and Critique on Discord. Every video has captions, audio description and a corrected transcript. Read AI in This Course in Module 0 during Week 1.

Before this course: a grade of C or better in AVC183, AVC184, AVC190, AVC200, CIS151 or MUC122, or permission of the instructor. You should be able to manage files and folders, make a screen or audio recording, and share an unlisted link.

Deadlines

Work is due at noon Arizona time every Thursday and Sunday; Week 1 has one deadline, on Thursday. For projects and project steps, post in the Discord course channel by noon the day before, then leave three critique comments by the due time. Work up to 24 hours late loses 10%. After that cutoff, called the hard close, nothing is accepted.

No work is due on a college holiday or during a break, and Canvas shows any moved deadline. You are welcome to comment on classmates who post late; it is not graded.

Sound and Non-Visual Paths

Every assignment, and the capstone, can be done as a sound experience. Examples: an audio-first app or game; a set of earcons, which are short sounds that stand for an action, such as send or error; a voice-guided tour; or spatial audio for AR or VR. Each module says what a sound student makes.

A blind, low-vision, Deaf or hard-of-hearing student, or one who works by keyboard, switch or voice, can complete every assignment. Each visual task names a non-visual option, and each sound task has a cue sheet or transcript. On these paths, rubrics grade your UX reasoning, research, testing and decisions, never visual polish.

Critique

Every critique comment has three parts:

  1. I notice: what you saw, heard or read.
  2. Because: the principle or evidence behind it, such as a heuristic.
  3. What if: one specific suggestion.

Critique what you can perceive, and say how: by ear for sound work, or from the description and alt text when you cannot see an image.

Access in This Course

Request accommodations through Disability Resources and Services. Tell the instructor privately in Week 1 how you work best. You may use the sound path or the non-visual path on any assignment without a special request.

These rules apply to every submission and every Discord post:

  • Video: accurate captions. Start with automatic captions (YouTube Studio, Premiere Pro Speech to Text, Google Vids or Meet), then read every line and fix names, terms and timing by hand. Automatic captions alone are not enough.
  • Audio: a transcript (auto-transcribe, then correct by hand) or a cue sheet, and for sound pieces a short text description of the important non-speech sounds.
  • Images: alt text on every meaningful image, one or two sentences on what matters (never “image of”); decorative images marked decorative.
  • Documents and slides: real headings, readable contrast and alt text (in Google Docs and Slides, right-click an image, then Alt text); PDFs exported with tags.
  • Web pages and prototypes: headings in order, link text that says where it goes, alt text, captions on video, and labeled controls. Check the page with WAVE or a screen reader.
  • Discord: the same rule; add a description (alt text) to every image you upload.

Captions or a transcript and alt text are about 10 percent of the points on every media rubric. Free screen readers for testing your own work: NVDA for Windows, VoiceOver on Mac and iPhone, and TalkBack on Android.

Research Ethics and Consent

  • Who takes part: participants are 18 or older and are not enrolled in your section, unless the instructor assigns classmates for a supervised session. Returning participants and students from other sections or courses may take part.
  • Consent every time: read or send the course consent script before every session, and stop when a participant asks.
  • Privacy: notes are de-identified (P1, P2, P3). Recordings stay in your Maricopa Google account and are deleted at the end of the course. Consent records go only to the instructor. Nothing identifying is posted to Discord.
  • Observation: observing in a store, venue or other private space needs the manager’s permission, and no photos of people without consent.
  • AI and research data: identifiable participant data never goes into any AI tool, and AI never generates participants, quotes or results.
  • Students under 18: you do not recruit, interview or test with adults you do not know, and you do not run video sessions with them. The supervised path counts the same for every grade. Use one of these:
    1. Classmates the instructor pairs you with, in a session the instructor can see (a recorded Google Meet in the course account, or a shared document).
    2. Participants the instructor approves, such as family members. A parent or guardian must be present for any session outside class, and their written okay is kept with your consent record.
    3. Recorded public usability sessions the instructor provides, which you analyze in place of live sessions.
    Tell the instructor privately in Week 1 if you are under 18; the instructor sets your path before Module 4.

Course consent script (read aloud or send in writing before every session, and adapt the bracketed parts):

  1. “My name is [name]. I am a student in a user experience design course, and I am studying [topic] for a class project.”
  2. “The session takes about [20 to 30] minutes. I will ask about things you have done, and I may ask you to try [an app, game, sound or prototype].”
  3. “With your permission, I will [take notes, record audio, record the screen]. Recordings stay in my school Google account and are deleted at the end of the course. Your name will not appear in my work; you will be called P1, P2 or P3.”
  4. “You may skip any question or stop at any time, with no reason needed.”
  5. “Do you agree to take part, and do you agree to [each kind of recording]?”

Record the date and the answer to each question in your private consent record, which goes only to the instructor.

AI in This Course

This course uses generative AI (Google Gemini) in assignments.

Your work stays yours. You use Gemini through your Maricopa account. That is Gemini for Education, the educational version of Gemini. Google states that what you enter there is not reviewed by people and is not used to train AI models (Gemini for Education; Google’s education FAQ). Your artwork and writing are not used to teach the AI. A personal Google account is different: depending on its settings, what you enter there may be used to improve Google’s AI. That is why every assignment uses your Maricopa account.

Industry expects it. In the 2024 Microsoft and LinkedIn Work Trend Index, 66% of business leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills, and 71% said they would rather hire a less experienced candidate with AI skills than a more experienced candidate without them (Work Trend Index). Our industry advisory board recommends that we teach these tools.

Using AI here is not cheating. Cheating is turning in work you did not do as if you did. Here, AI use is assigned, and you record it in an AI-use log with every assignment. You are graded on your own thinking: your research, your decisions and your design work. Other courses may have different AI rules. Follow those rules in those courses.

Privacy. Your instructor never puts your work into a public AI tool.

AI does not make your work. Gemini helps with research, writing, feedback, planning, and code built from your own plan. It never designs: your layouts, sounds and visual choices are your own, and you show your process. No AI images, layouts, audio or voices for design work.

You will learn to question it. AI makes confident mistakes and repeats bias from the data it learned from. You will learn to check every answer and to think about what AI gets wrong and what it costs.

AI has real costs. AI is trained on artists’ work, often without permission. Data centers already use about 1.5% of the world’s electricity, and the International Energy Agency expects that to more than double by the end of the decade.

Students under 18. Maricopa Gemini accounts for students under 18 leave out a few features, such as Deep Research and music generation, and decline some topics. Heuristics in sound
HeuristicWhat it means for audio
1. Visibility of system statusEvery input gets an audible confirmation; states such as loading or low health have distinct cues

From Gemini to a web page

Setup, about 20 minutes, before the Practice below.

You can turn something you make in Gemini into a real web page. You do not need to write any code. Each step names the control the way a screen reader announces it.

Part 1: Make the app

  1. Open your web browser (Chrome works best).
  2. Go to Gemini.
  3. Check the account: select the button named “Google Account” followed by your name. It must show your Maricopa address (MEID@maricopa.edu). If it shows a personal account, choose your Maricopa account from that menu.
  4. Move to the text box named “Enter a prompt here” (it reads “Ask Gemini”).
  5. Type this, then press Enter: “Make an interactive card sort with 8 cards about a campus app. Make it a working app I can click.”
  6. Wait. Gemini builds the app in a separate panel beside the chat, labeled with the app’s name.
  7. Use your app to make sure it works.
  8. If something is wrong, type what to fix in the same text box (for example, “Make the buttons bigger”) and press Enter.

Part 2: Send it to Google Sites

  1. In the app panel, select the button named “Export.”
  2. In the menu that opens, select “Google Sites.”
  3. A new tab opens with a Google Sites draft. Your app is already on the page. This draft is private; only you can see it.
  4. Select the page title text box (it reads “Your page title”) and type a name for your page.
  5. Move below the app, select the page, and type one sentence about what your app does.

Part 3: Publish and share

  1. Select the button named “Publish.”
  2. In the dialog, type a short web address in the field named “Web address” (for example, your-name-card-sort). Select “Publish.”
  3. Select the button named “Publish options” (next to Publish), then “Copy published site link.”
  4. Go to the class Discord channel. Paste the link and press Enter.

If you get stuck: post in the class Discord channel with the step number you were on, plus a screenshot or, for screen reader users, the last thing your screen reader announced. Google sometimes renames these controls; if a name does not match, tell the instructor and the steps will be updated. Test your published page with your screen reader before you share it.

Sound design: in step 5, ask instead for an audio player page or a listening quiz for your own recorded clips, and add the sound files yourself. Gemini builds how the page works; it never makes the sounds.

Good to know: your site follows the district’s sharing rules, so people outside Maricopa may not be able to open it. Gemini may build how the app works, but every design choice on your page (words, colors, images, sounds) is yours. Take Your Work With You

Ungraded · Week 15

Your Maricopa Google account may close after you leave the college. Do this before the course ends, so you keep everything you made even if you stop out, transfer or graduate. It takes about 45 minutes.

  1. Save your files in one place. Make one Google Drive folder for this course. Put in it your native project files (engine, Figma, audio and video projects), the final build with its run instructions, your process book, recordings and asset and license list and your AI-use log. For each Figma file, choose File, then Save local copy, to download a .fig copy. Videos you posted to YouTube with your Maricopa account are not copied, so download your own YouTube videos into the folder too.
  2. Copy everything to a personal Google account. Make a personal Google account if you do not have one. First make your own copy (File, then Make a copy) of any file someone shared with you, because files you do not own are not copied. Then, signed in to your Maricopa account, open Google Takeout Transfer and copy your Drive and Gmail to your personal account (see Google’s help page). It can take up to a week, and the copies arrive unshared. If Transfer is not available on your account, use Google Takeout to download your Drive as .zip files and save them on your computer.
  3. Take your AI work with you. Save every prototype and all the code Gemini wrote for you: copy each Gemini Canvas app’s code into a file in your folder, and keep the spec you gave it. The instructions and source files are what rebuild it, so keep each one’s instructions in a Google Doc and its sources in your course folder. To keep your chats, use Google Takeout: choose My Activity, then Gemini Apps. After you leave, rebuild it in Gemini with your personal account: make a new one, paste the instructions and upload the sources. If you used a Gem or Gemini Notebook, keep its instructions and sources the same way.
    • Know the privacy difference. Your Maricopa account keeps your Gemini work out of AI training (see Start Here). A personal account does not have the same protections, and Google may use your activity to improve its AI, depending on your settings. In your personal account, open Gemini, then Settings & help, then Activity (Gemini Apps Activity), and decide whether to turn off Keep Activity. Never put anyone else’s private information into a personal account.
  4. Move your Google Site. Takeout Transfer copies Drive, Gmail and Photos. Do not count on it to move your Google Site, if your build or process book lives on one. First keep the content: put the site’s text in a Google Doc and its images in your course folder, then open each published page and save it as a PDF (File, then Print, then Save as PDF). Then try a direct copy: in the site editor, choose Share and add your personal email as an editor. Sign in with your personal account, open the site, and choose More (the three dots), then Make a copy. If sharing outside Maricopa is blocked, rebuild the site in your personal account at sites.google.com with the same content and publish it again. The web address changes, so update it on your resume and profiles.
  5. Protect your artwork online. No website can fully stop AI companies from copying public images. These steps make it harder and show the work is yours:
    • Post screen-size images, not your full-size print files.
    • Add your name and a copyright line (© Your Name) to the description or the image.
    • In Adobe apps or Adobe Content Authenticity, attach Content Credentials and choose “I request that generative AI models not train on or use my content.” It is a request, not a lock: Adobe Firefly honors it, and other AI companies may not (see Adobe’s help page).
    • Optional: Glaze, a free tool from a University of Chicago research team, adds hidden changes that make it harder for AI to copy your style. It works best on detailed artwork and is not a guarantee.
    • Keep your full-size master files private in your Drive folder. Your portfolio site shows only screen-size images and stays Restricted until you choose to make it public.
