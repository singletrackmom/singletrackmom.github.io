UX Design for Interactive Media

AVC 2xx

One UX method, many disciplines. Students from game design, animation, AR/VR, sound design, graphic design and other creative programs learn the same UX method and practice it in their own medium. You meet the modules in order. Module 0 sets you up and Module 1 is a short AI primer, both in Weeks 1 and 2. Modules 2 to 8 build a small Studio Project one step at a time: analyze, research, brief, flow, prototype, evaluate technology, test and revise. Modules 9 to 15 are one original, functional capstone in your own discipline, tested twice with real people, presented to the class and defended.

Sound experience path: every assignment and the capstone can be done as a sound experience: an audio-first app or game interface, a sonic identity and notification system built from earcons (short sounds that stand for an action or event, such as a send or an error), a voice or audio-guided tour, an accessible audio navigation experience, or spatial audio for AR or VR. Each module names what a sound student makes and submits at that step.

Access: the course is designed so a blind, low-vision, Deaf or hard-of-hearing student, or one who works by keyboard, switch or voice, can complete every assignment and be graded on the same criteria. Each visual task names a non-visual equivalent, each sound task carries a cue sheet or transcript, and rubrics on the sound and non-visual paths grade the UX reasoning, research, testing and decisions, never visual polish. Access in This Course, in the Syllabus, lists which tools work with a screen reader.

Every week: two noon deadlines, Arizona time; Week 1 has one, on Thursday, August 26, 2027. About 8 hours a week, 120 hours in all. Start with Start Here and the Syllabus.

Overview

Welcome to UX Design for Interactive Media. Students come to this course from game design, animation, sound design, graphic design, app design, digital media arts and other creative programs. You learn one user experience (UX) method, the practice of designing an experience around the people who use it, and you apply it in your own medium. The course is fully online and asynchronous: there are no required live sessions. Office hours and capstone check-ins are optional live sessions, always recorded.

How a module works

There are 16 modules, Module 0 to Module 15, and you meet them in order. Every module follows the same order: Objectives, Overview, Watch, Read, Practice, Assessment, Critique on Discord, and Due. Each assignment states the objective it measures, how it is graded, and its deadlines. Module 1, the AI Primer, opens in Week 1 and is due in Week 2.

Critique protocol

Every critique comment has three parts: I notice (what you saw, heard or read), Because (the principle or evidence behind it, such as a heuristic), and What if (one specific suggestion). Critique what you can perceive and say how: by ear for sound work, from the description and alt text when you cannot see an image.

Overview

AVC 2xx, UX Design for Interactive Media. 3 credits, lecture and lab. Principles and methods of user experience design applied to interactive media across disciplines, including usability, accessibility, user research, prototyping and user testing; investigation of emerging technologies and interaction methods, including technology-assisted development and augmented, virtual and spatial interfaces. The course culminates in an original, interactive project in your discipline. Fully online and asynchronous, 15 weeks, about 120 hours of work (about 8 hours a week).

Prerequisites: a grade of C or better in AVC183, AVC184, AVC190, AVC200, CIS151 or MUC122, or permission of the instructor. The course assumes you can manage files and folders, make a screen or audio recording, and share an unlisted link.

AI in This Course

This course uses generative AI as part of the assignments. Our industry advisory board encourages us to teach current industry practice, and employers now expect graduates to work with AI. In Microsoft and LinkedIn’s Work Trend Index, 66% of business leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills.

Using AI here is not cheating. Cheating is turning in work you did not do as if you did. In this course, AI use is assigned, it is disclosed in an AI-use log with every assignment, and you are graded on your own thinking: your research, your decisions and your design work. Other courses may set different rules, and those rules apply in those courses.

Your work is not used to train AI. You will use Gemini with your Maricopa account. Under the district’s Google for Education agreement, what you enter is not reviewed by people, not used to train AI models, and not used for ads (Gemini for Education; Gemini for Google Workspace education FAQ). Always sign in with your Maricopa account, never a personal one. Your instructor will not put your work into any public AI tool.

AI does not make your work. Gemini helps with research, writing, feedback, planning and code from your own spec. It never designs: your marks, layouts, sounds and visual decisions are your own, and you will show your process. No AI image, layout, audio or voice generation for design work. Every submission carries a short AI-use log (tool, purpose, what you kept, what you changed).

You will learn to question it. AI makes confident mistakes and reflects bias in its training data. This course teaches you to check every answer and to think critically about what AI gets wrong and what it costs.

Students under 18. Maricopa Gemini accounts for students under 18 leave out a few features (for example Deep Research and music generation) and decline some topics. No assignment in this course depends on those features. If Gemini blocks a step, note it in your AI-use log, tell the instructor, and you receive an equivalent step with no loss of points. Google lists the differences in Turn the Gemini app on or off: education offerings by age.

Which Gemini tools. Gemini chat, Gemini Canvas (a side panel in Gemini for drafting code or a small web page; not the Canvas learning management system) and Gemini in Google Docs and Sheets. Saved custom versions of Gemini are not used; for a reusable set of sources, use Gemini Notebook. Gemini writes; it never designs.

Read the sources yourself: Gemini for Education; Google Workspace with Gemini FAQ for Education; Microsoft and LinkedIn 2024 Work Trend Index; International Energy Agency, Energy and AI.

Access in This Course

Request accommodations through Disability Resources and Services, and tell the instructor privately in Week 1 how you work best. Every assignment names a sound path and a non-visual path; you may use either without a special request. Post for everyone: alt text on every image (the description field on each Discord upload), a transcript or cue sheet with every audio post, captions on every video. Free screen readers for testing your own work: NVDA for Windows, VoiceOver on Mac and iPhone, and TalkBack on Android.

Accessibility of your work. Required on every submission and every Discord post:

  • Video: accurate captions. Start with automatic captions (YouTube Studio, Premiere Pro Speech to Text, Google Vids or Meet), then read every line and fix names, terms and timing by hand. Automatic captions alone are not enough.
  • Audio: a transcript (auto-transcribe, then correct by hand) or a cue sheet, and for sound pieces a short text description of the important non-speech sounds.
  • Images: alt text on every meaningful image, one or two sentences on what matters (never “image of”); decorative images marked decorative.
  • Documents and slides: real headings, readable contrast and alt text (in Google Docs and Slides, right-click an image, then Alt text); PDFs exported with tags.
  • Web pages and prototypes: in Google Sites, headings in order, link text that says where it goes, alt text, captions on embedded video, labeled controls, and a check with WAVE or a screen reader.
  • Discord: the same rule; add a description (alt text) to every image you upload.

Every rubric for media work includes captions or a transcript and alt text, about 10 percent of the points (1 of 10, 3 of 30).

Research ethics and consent

  • Who takes part: participants are 18 or older and are not enrolled in your section, unless the instructor assigns classmates for a supervised session. Returning participants and students from other sections or courses may take part.
  • Consent every time: read or send the course consent script before every session, and stop when a participant asks.
  • Privacy: notes are de-identified (P1, P2, P3). Recordings stay in your Maricopa Google account and are deleted at the end of the course. Consent records go only to the instructor. Nothing identifying is posted to Discord.
  • Observation: observing in a store, venue or other private space needs the manager’s permission, and no photos of people without consent.
  • AI and research data: identifiable participant data never goes into any AI tool, and AI never generates participants, quotes or results.
  • Students under 18: you do not recruit, interview or test with adults you do not know, and you do not run video sessions with them. The supervised path counts the same for every grade: (1) classmates the instructor pairs you with, in a session the instructor can see (a recorded Google Meet in the course account, or a shared document); (2) participants the instructor approves, such as family members, with your parent or guardian present for any session outside class and the guardian’s acknowledgment kept with your consent record; or (3) analysis of recorded public usability sessions the instructor provides, in place of live sessions. Tell the instructor privately in Week 1 if you are under 18; the instructor sets your path before Module 5.

Course consent script (read aloud or send in writing before every session, and adapt the bracketed parts):

  1. “My name is [name]. I am a student in a user experience design course, and I am studying [topic] for a class project.”
  2. “The session takes about [20 to 30] minutes. I will ask about things you have done, and I may ask you to try [an app, game, sound or prototype].”
  3. “With your permission, I will [take notes, record audio, record the screen]. Recordings stay in my school Google account and are deleted at the end of the course. Your name will not appear in my work; you will be called P1, P2 or P3.”
  4. “You may skip any question or stop at any time, with no reason needed.”
  5. “Do you agree to take part, and do you agree to [each kind of recording]?”

Record the date and the answer to each question in your private consent record, which goes only to the instructor.

From Gemini to a web page

Setup, about 20 minutes, before the Practice below.

You can turn something you make in Gemini into a real web page. You do not need to write any code. Each step names the control the way a screen reader announces it.

Part 1: Make the app

  1. Open your web browser (Chrome works best).
  2. Go to Gemini.
  3. Check the account: select the button named “Google Account” followed by your name. It must show your Maricopa address (MEID@maricopa.edu). If it shows a personal account, choose your Maricopa account from that menu.
  4. Move to the text box named “Enter a prompt here” (it reads “Ask Gemini”).
  5. Type this, then press Enter: “Make an interactive card sort with 8 cards about a campus app. Make it a working app I can click.”
  6. Wait. Gemini builds the app in a separate panel beside the chat, labeled with the app’s name.
  7. Use your app to make sure it works.
  8. If something is wrong, type what to fix in the same text box (for example, “Make the buttons bigger”) and press Enter.

Part 2: Send it to Google Sites

  1. In the app panel, select the button named “Export.”
  2. In the menu that opens, select “Google Sites.”
  3. A new tab opens with a Google Sites draft. Your app is already on the page. This draft is private; only you can see it.
  4. Select the page title text box (it reads “Your page title”) and type a name for your page.
  5. Move below the app, select the page, and type one sentence about what your app does.

Part 3: Publish and share

  1. Select the button named “Publish.”
  2. In the dialog, type a short web address in the field named “Web address” (for example, your-name-card-sort). Select “Publish.”
  3. Select the button named “Publish options” (next to Publish), then “Copy published site link.”
  4. Go to the class Discord channel. Paste the link and press Enter.

If you get stuck: post in the class Discord channel with the step number you were on, plus a screenshot or, for screen reader users, the last thing your screen reader announced. Google sometimes renames these controls; if a name does not match, tell the instructor and the steps will be updated. Test your published page with your screen reader before you share it.

Sound design: in step 5, ask instead for an audio player page or a listening quiz for your own recorded clips, and add the sound files yourself. Gemini builds how the page works; it never makes the sounds.

Good to know: your site follows the district’s sharing rules, so people outside Maricopa may not be able to open it. Gemini may build how the app works, but every design choice on your page (words, colors, images, sounds) is yours. Source: Google Workspace Updates, Publish interactive apps from the Gemini app to Google Sites.

Overview

You now have two test rounds, a peer review and weeks of critique, and a revision plan. In this module you make the planned changes, freeze features, check your own build one last time with the heuristics and accessibility guidelines, and rehearse the recorded presentation. Your presentation is both a class presentation and a portfolio piece for employers: the problem, the people you designed for, what you tried, what testing taught you, and what you built. After Thursday you only fix bugs, record and document.

Module 15: The Capstone: Presentation and Defense

, Week 15 · about 8 hours

Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  1. 15.1 Present and defend an original, functional capstone project to peers using research and test evidence (Course Competency 7; Create)
  2. 15.2 Analyze classmates’ capstone projects using UX principles and pose evidence-based defense questions (Course Competency 1; Analyze)

Overview

This week you present your capstone to the class and defend it. A model can generate code or a sentence, but it cannot account for a term of your own design decisions; the defense is where you show that you can. Keep the final recording: it is built to show employers.

The capstone

An original, functional, interactive project in your discipline that integrates user research, two rounds of testing and one emerging technology or interaction method. One primary user, one core task or loop, three to five minutes of experience. No networking, accounts, payments or personal data. Assets are made, reused or licensed, and disclosed.

What functional means, by discipline
DisciplineFunctional means
Game designA playable build (desktop, web or headset) in which a first-time player completes one core loop without help
AnimationAn interactive animated piece in which every choice point works and leads to a complete path
Sound designAn audio system that responds to input or state in real time (middleware in an engine, a Web Audio build, or a sonic AR walk); a linear mix alone does not count
Graphic designA working interactive layer, such as an image-tracked AR trigger or live web experience, connected to the printed or spatial pieces
AR/VRAn AR or VR experience a first-time user can start, complete and exit without the designer’s help
App design and digital media artsAn app that completes the core task end to end; an installation or kiosk that responds to visitors without the designer operating it

A Wizard-of-Oz prototype is a valid prototype in Modules 11 and 12, but not a functional final unless the instructor approved it as your fallback.

Sound experience capstone

Any student may build the capstone as a sound experience: an audio-first app or game interface, a sonic identity and notification system, a voice or audio-guided tour, an accessible audio navigation experience, or spatial audio for AR or VR. Functional is defined in the table above. Recorded voice and sound are your own, reused or licensed; AI never generates audio or voice. It is graded with the same Capstone Final and Defense rubric, points and objectives:
