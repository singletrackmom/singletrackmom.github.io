UX Design for Interactive Media

The course, module by module. Backward-designed from the competencies, one project carried the whole way, assessed on the reasoning as much as the artifact.

Course design The course

One project, built in parts. Every student designs a product experience for the brand they built in Graphic Design 1, so each project starts from the student’s own identity work, no two are alike, and the courses connect. Each module adds one scaffolded piece, research, prototype, component library, evaluation, and the pieces assemble into a single defended case study at the end. Practice is carried by four interactive simulations; the summative is the defense.

0. Getting Started

Seat time: about 4 hours

Objectives

  • Locate the syllabus, module map, and grading, and restate how the one project builds across the course (C2)
  • Set up the Discord critique space and the Miro planning board (C10)
  • Describe the difference between formative practice and the summative case study (C2)

Content

  • How the course works: backward design and constructive alignment in plain terms
  • The project: design a product experience for your Graphic Design 1 brand, carried from research to a defended case study
  • Where each tool lives, and the weekly rhythm (post-by, reply-by, captions, glossary)

Resources

  • Course-authored orientation demo video and syllabus (my OER, captioned)
  • Course-authored key-terms glossary, added to each module

Formative practice

  • A low-stakes orientation self-check, so students confirm what they know before Week 1 (self-regulated learning)

Interaction

  • Instructor welcome and check-in; students post an intro and their Graphic Design 1 brand, reply to two peers

Assessment

  • Formative, completion. AI-resistance: the intro names their own brand and a personal reason. Deliverable: the project’s starting brand is set.

1. Foundations & HCI

Seat time: about 14 hours

Objectives

  • Describe UX principles and how UX differs from graphic design (C1)
  • Identify cognitive load and at least three Laws of UX in a real interface (C1)
  • Connect one idea from HCI history to a design decision (C1)

Content

  • What UX is and is not: problem framing, usability, user goals versus visual polish
  • Cognitive load, affordances and signifiers, feedback, consistency, a first look at heuristics
  • A short arc of HCI history and why human-centered thinking emerged

Resources

  • Laws of UX (CC BY-NC-SA), principles and cognitive load
  • Interaction Design Foundation open literature, HCI history
  • Nielsen Norman Group, UX fundamentals (cite, do not copy)

Activities

  • Interface teardown in Miro: three principles at work, one violated
  • One paragraph distinguishing a UX decision from a graphic-design decision

Formative practice

  • A principle-spotting self-check that maps examples to the right Law of UX, scaffolding the vocabulary

Interaction

  • Discord: post the teardown by Wednesday, feedback to four classmates by Sunday, log it

Assessment

  • Formative. AI-resistance: tied to the student’s own annotated screenshots. Deliverable: name the user and product need for their brand.

2. Human-Centered Process & User Research

Seat time: about 18 hours

Objectives

  • Outline a human-centered process and place the project within it (C2)
  • Conduct a short interview or lightweight survey and record findings (C3)
  • Synthesize findings into an affinity map and translate three insights into decisions (C3)

Content

  • The design process: research, synthesis, ideation, delivery
  • Methods: interviews, lightweight surveys, observation, with an ethics and consent note
  • Affinity mapping, working personas, problem statements, insight to decision

Resources

  • 18F Methods and digital.gov (public domain), research and synthesis guides
  • Nielsen Norman Group, user research and personas (cite)

Activities

  • Two short interviews or a small survey on the brand’s users, capture notes
  • Affinity map in Miro, a one-sentence problem statement, three supported decisions

Formative practice

  • Affinity-mapping and card-sort simulation: cluster a provided set of research snippets, compare to model groupings, before doing it on their own data

Interaction

  • Discord: post the problem statement and affinity map by Wednesday, feedback to four by Sunday, log it

Assessment

  • Formative. AI-resistance: graded on the student’s own data and the reasoning linking findings to decisions. Deliverable: validated problem statement, early persona, prioritized decisions.

3. Wireframing, Prototyping, Mobile & Responsive

Seat time: about 22 hours

Objectives

  • Produce wireframes and an interactive prototype in an industry-standard tool (C4)
  • Design a mobile interface to current conventions and a platform guideline (C5)
  • Design a responsive web layout that adapts across three sizes (C6)

Content

  • Fidelity levels, user flows, staying low-fidelity to avoid wasted effort
  • Prototyping mechanics: frames, links, states, clickable flows
  • Mobile conventions and platform guidelines; responsive breakpoints, fluid grids, content priority

Resources

  • Course-authored prototyping demo videos (my OER, captioned)
  • Material Design 3 and Apple Human Interface Guidelines (free, vendor copyright)
  • W3C WAI and WCAG (open), accessible layout and touch targets

Activities

  • Wireframe the core flow, build a clickable prototype of it
  • One mobile screen set and one responsive layout at phone, tablet, and desktop

Formative practice

  • Spot-the-accessibility-barrier simulation: scan sample screens for contrast, touch-target, and reflow issues, building an evaluative eye before critique

Interaction

  • Discord: post the prototype and screen sizes by Wednesday, feedback to four by Sunday, log it; instructor models one strong flow critique

Assessment

  • Formative. AI-resistance: graded on flow logic and how the design answers the student’s own research, with a short rationale per major choice. Deliverable: a working prototype of the core flow, mobile and responsive.

4. AI in the Design Workflow

Seat time: about 16 hours

Objectives

  • Integrate AI tools into ideation, assets, and iteration on the project (C8)
  • Evaluate AI output critically, keeping what serves users and revising what does not, with a rationale (C8)
  • State where AI helped and where human judgment was required (C8)

Content

  • Where AI fits: divergent ideation, placeholder assets, copy drafts, rapid iteration
  • Prompting for design work and the limits of generated output
  • Critical evaluation: bias, accessibility gaps, generic solutions that ignore the research; attribution and honest disclosure

Resources

  • Course-authored AI-in-design lesson with prompts, examples, and a disclosure format (my OER)
  • Laws of UX (CC BY-NC-SA), the yardstick for judging AI suggestions

Activities

  • Use an AI tool to generate alternatives or assets, then select and refine
  • A short before-and-after: one AI suggestion kept, one rejected, and why, judged against UX principles

Formative practice

  • A guided critique comparing AI-generated screens against the project’s research decisions, flagging where output drifts from real user needs

Interaction

  • Discord: post AI-assisted iterations with the disclosure note by Wednesday, feedback to four by Sunday, log it; discussion centers on judgment, not novelty

Assessment

  • Formative. AI-resistance: the task grades critical judgment about AI output on the student’s own project, so uncritical generation scores poorly. Deliverable: a refined iteration with an honest account of AI use.

5. Design Systems & Usability Evaluation

Seat time: about 20 hours

Objectives

  • Build and apply a reusable component library, carrying the Graphic Design 1 brand into the interface (C7)
  • Evaluate a design against Nielsen’s 10 heuristics and record findings (C10)
  • Run a basic usability check and translate results into prioritized fixes (C10)

Content

  • Design systems: tokens, components, states, why reuse reduces inconsistency
  • The brand as the source of the tokens: color, type, and spacing come from their Graphic Design 1 identity
  • Nielsen’s 10 heuristics; basic usability testing: tasks, observation, severity; the prioritized fix list

Resources

  • Course-authored component-library demo video (my OER, captioned)
  • Nielsen Norman Group, the 10 heuristics (cite); Material Design 3, system structure
  • The A11Y Project (CC) and W3C WCAG (open), accessibility checklists

Activities

  • Build a small component library (buttons, inputs, cards, navigation) from the brand and apply it across screens
  • Run a heuristic evaluation and one short usability check, produce a prioritized fix list

Formative practice

  • Heuristic-evaluation simulator (Nielsen’s 10): flag violations on sample screens, compare to an expert pass. Plus the usability-test role-play with a synthetic participant, to practice running and reading a session

Interaction

  • Discord, the deepest critique loop of the term: post the library and heuristic findings by Wednesday, feedback to four by Sunday, log it; instructor summarizes recurring violations

Assessment

  • Formative. AI-resistance: evaluation is performed on the student’s own and a peer’s screens; grades depend on severity reasoning and the resulting revisions. Deliverable: a component-driven, evaluated, revised design.

6. Spatial & AR/VR

Seat time: about 14 hours

Objectives

  • Explain how screen conventions translate, and fail to translate, into spatial and AR contexts (C9)
  • Propose a spatial or AR concept for one part of the project and justify it (C9)
  • Identify new constraints: depth, field of view, comfort, input (C9)

Content

  • From 2D to space: what carries over (hierarchy, affordances, feedback) and what changes
  • Spatial concerns: depth, scale, field of view, comfort and safety, input methods
  • When spatial or AR genuinely serves the user versus when a flat screen is better

Resources

  • Course-authored AR/VR lesson with a screen-to-spatial translation framework (my OER)
  • Apple HIG and Material Design spatial guidance where available (free, vendor copyright)
  • Nielsen Norman Group, spatial and immersive UX (cite)

Activities

  • Storyboard a spatial or AR version of one project moment, noting what changes
  • A short justification of when the spatial version helps the user and when it does not

Formative practice

  • A concept critique in Miro mapping screen conventions to spatial equivalents and flagging what breaks, transferring earlier principles to a new context

Interaction

  • Discord: post the storyboard and justification by Wednesday, feedback to four by Sunday, log it; prompt centers on user benefit and comfort, not spectacle

Assessment

  • Formative. AI-resistance: anchored to the student’s specific project and researched users; grading rewards the reasoning about fit. Deliverable: a spatial concept extension for the case study.

7. Case Study, Portfolio & Presentation

Seat time: about 24 hours · summative

Objectives

  • Assemble the term’s work into one coherent case study, research to final design (C2, C10)
  • Present completed work visually and verbally and defend decisions (C11)
  • Respond to questions about reasoning, research, and response to critique in a live Q&A (C10, C11)

Content

  • Case-study structure: problem, research, decisions, iterations, evaluation, outcome
  • Presenting reasoning, not just screens; portfolio framing; preparing to defend and explain trade-offs

Resources

  • Course-authored case-study brief, portfolio template, and presentation rubric (my OER)
  • Nielsen Norman Group, presenting UX work (cite)

Activities

  • Assemble the case study from the deliverables built in Modules 2 through 6
  • Record a presentation walkthrough (captioned or scripted, so no one presents live to the whole class)

Formative practice

  • A dry-run critique: post a draft walkthrough for peer feedback and self-assess against the rubric before final submission

Interaction

  • Discord: post the draft case study and walkthrough by the posted date, feedback to four, log it; the live Q&A defense is scheduled individually or in small groups

Assessment

  • Summative. The defended case study: a recorded walkthrough plus a live Q&A, graded on process and reasoning as well as the artifact. AI-resistance: the project is on the student’s own brand and the live defense requires explaining their own decisions in real time, which cannot be outsourced. Deliverable: the completed, defended case study and portfolio piece.
