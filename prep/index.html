⭐ The pilot question

Planning and evaluating pilots is in the job description, so he will ask when I ran a pilot and my process. Say the process first, then one story in STAR.

My pilot process, one line:

“I define the number I am moving before I build anything, run it small against a baseline, watch what actually happens instead of what I hoped, and I am willing to kill it if it demoed well but did not move the number.”

Option A · Render usability pilot (recommended)

S Render is a career-launch tool I designed and coded myself, a personal learning environment a student builds across a capstone and keeps. I had a working prototype but no evidence it worked for students rather than for me.

T Before it went near a graded course, I needed to know where real students got stuck. So the task was a structured usability pilot, not a demo.

A I recruited students, gave them real tasks, and watched, no rescuing. I logged every hesitation, scored the friction by severity, and rebuilt the worst offenders. Held the no-PII line the whole way. Then set up a supervised Fall pilot with Career Services with a defined outcome to measure, not “did they like it.”

R The pilot changed the product: the flow that made sense to me confused students, and I cut it. It is honestly still a prototype heading into that Fall pilot, and that is the point. I test before I scale.

Guardrail: do not claim production or a success percentage I cannot verify.

Option B · District Barriers method pilot (scale + rigor)

S A UX study across all ten Maricopa colleges and roughly 140,000 students. That big, it fails if the instrument is wrong.

T So before fieldwork I piloted the method itself, a small run to prove the walkthrough protocol and scoring held before scaling to ten colleges.

A Twelve persona-driven walkthroughs, each barrier scored on a Nielsen severity rating and confirmed by a second rater. I watched whether the protocol produced consistent, decision-useful findings.

R The pilot surfaced its first catastrophic-severity finding and proved the instrument. I earned the right to scale because I tested small first.

Option C · Course rebuild pilot (teaching)

S Commissioned to rebuild my program’s highest-enrollment, lowest-success course, the one students were withdrawing from.

T Rather than redesign on instinct, I treated the rebuild as a pilot: change deliberately, teach it, watch persistence.

A Reworked it module by module from retention research, wove in student-support touchpoints tied to an institutional learning outcome, taught it, watched engagement and completion through Canvas analytics.

R Iterated off the data, not my assumptions, then packaged it as an IMSCC any instructor imports and teaches as built, so the improvement was reusable.

If he pushes “how did you know it worked?” “I set the metric before I built. For a learning pilot that is persistence and completion, not satisfaction. For an assessment pilot it would be inter-rater reliability, whether the reasoning trail predicts competence, and whether completion time stays equitable, that last one is an equity guardrail, not a vanity metric.”

Turn it back: “How does a pilot actually get greenlit here, and what evidence do people need before it scales?”

My pilot method & tools (the Director-era answer)

My strongest pilot credential is not the shiny AI tools, it is running ed-tech evaluation and adoption for the whole campus as Director of Instructional Technology. Name the stages and I sound like someone who has run pilots for fifteen years, because I have.

  • Identify the real problem, from data, not a tool looking for a home.
  • Evaluate options against criteria: accessibility, data privacy, integration, cost, support load.

The hard questions (assessment validity)

My position in three sentences:

1. When a model can produce the artifact, the artifact stops being evidence of who produced it. Construct validity, not academic integrity.

2. Detection is not the answer. Detectors are unreliable and do nothing about the student who used AI well, who is the one we want to graduate.

3. Change what the assessment collects. A model produces a finished thing, not a trail of having thought. Collect the trail: the commitment before knowing, the alternative rejected, the revision under a real consequence, the decision defended live.

1 · Scale (the one that can kill me)

“Your model is one designer with one SME. We have 175,000 students. How does this scale?”

Honest: “It does not scale as a bespoke design engagement, and I won’t pretend otherwise. What scales is the pattern, not the instance. The pairing produces a reusable template: what the scenario has to do, what the rubric collects, what a defensible response looks like. The expensive part is the first one in a discipline; after that it is authoring, not designing. And the evaluator’s job may get easier, reasoning a student can defend is easier to score than an artifact where you’re quietly wondering who wrote it.” Then: “How you get from one to a thousand at your scale is a question I’d want to work on, and I don’t have the answer today.” Turn it back: “How are your evaluators handling the ones they suspect now? What does that cost you?”

2 · Isn’t a defense just another artifact?

“Nothing stops them mechanically. But the questions are generated from what they specifically said, about a scenario they specifically saw, and they’re follow-ups. A student who didn’t do the thinking can’t survive three rounds of ‘why not the other option?’ with a model in the loop, because the model doesn’t know what they chose. It gets slower than just doing the work.” The better answer: “Stop trying to make it impossible. Design the AI use in and grade the disclosure, ask the student to delineate what the model produced from what they produced, and grade the delineation. That works on the student who used AI well.”

3 · Prove it works

“I don’t have that evidence and I won’t claim it. I have a diagnostic in testing and a design not yet built. The first question is only whether it reads as credible to the faculty who teach the subject. Whether it measures better is an empirical claim I haven’t tested, and I’d test it properly before scaling. That’s my whole no-scale-until-proven position, and I won’t abandon it for my own idea.”

4 · Why isn’t proctoring enough?

“Proctoring protects recall and can’t protect the thing you actually certify. You can’t proctor a business analysis into being authentic. And the moment you proctor everything you’ve become a testing center and given up the thing that makes WGU worth more than one.”

5 · The surveillance question

“Against it, hard, and this is a line I hold everywhere. My tools collect no student data: Render stores a first name in the browser, CopaMigo stores nothing, Dial Your Course reads a package with no student data by design. Process evidence doesn’t require surveillance, it requires asking for the process as a deliverable. ‘Submit the working file, the version you rejected, and one paragraph on what the critique changed.’ The student hands you the trail.”

6 · You’re community college faculty

Don’t get defensive, don’t apologize twice. “I haven’t run assessment at 175,000 students and won’t pretend. I have run a study across ten colleges and ~140,000 students, the scale question in another domain, and I hold courses across every discipline to a national standard as a lead reviewer, which is assessment-validity work under another name. What community college gives me is proximity: the frightened faculty walk into my office and I watch what actually happens.”

7 · What did your own tool find?

If it flagged a lot, say so and say what I changed, the credibility is in turning the instrument on myself first. If I haven’t run the full pass, say so and why (Canvas isn’t exporting my learning outcomes, which is itself a finding). Do not invent a number.

8 · What would falsify this?

“If students produce equally strong reasoning trails with a model in the loop and without one, the trail isn’t measuring what I think. If evaluators can’t score reasoning reliably, inter-rater reliability collapses and it’s worse than what it replaced. If it takes so much longer that only students with time can pass, I’ve built an equity problem and should stop.” A futures person respects a falsification condition more than a vision statement.

9 · The actual future (5 years)

Give a validity answer, not a technology one. “Content becomes abundant and nearly free, so the scarce thing is judgment. The artifact stops being the unit of assessment and the trail becomes the unit. Assessment stops being an event at the end and becomes continuous evidence along the way, which CBE is already better positioned for. The institutions that survive are the ones that can still credibly say their credential means the person can do the thing. Validity is about to be the whole competitive advantage.”

10 · Why us?

“Because you have the most to lose and gain. Every institution has this problem; most can hide behind seat time. You can’t, your degree is a claim about competence backed by an assessment and nothing else. You have to solve this first, and properly. It’s the most interesting version of the problem, and the one where getting it right actually matters.”

Futurist / director questions

Reframe, ground in shipped work, land a thesis.

1 · Where is learning headed, and what can an institution do a model company never will?

Content is collapsing to nearly free and infinite, so the scarce thing is judgment and a human who actually became capable. A model company optimizes for capability and engagement. An institution optimizes for what they structurally can’t: whether a real person crossed a threshold and can be trusted by an employer to have done it. Our edge is trust, credentialing of genuine competence, human formation, and a mission pointed at the people the market ignores.

2 · Assessment when the model can do the task

Grading the artifact means grading the machine. Move assessment onto the judgment: can you direct the tool, critique what it gives you, catch it when it’s confidently wrong, make the call it can’t. That’s the competency worth certifying. CBE has an unfair advantage if it shifts from “can you produce X” to “can you make the decisions a professional makes under real conditions.”

3 · Human-in-the-loop at scale

It breaks only if a human touches every interaction, that’s the luxury version. Redefine it: a human present at the moments that decide whether a learner stays, the doubt, the barrier, the quiet “maybe I should quit.” AI absorbs the infinite-scale work; deploy the human surgically. CopaMigo is this made concrete. Scale the machine; concentrate the human.

4 · Rigor vs hype, how do I know a pilot works before you scale it?

I define the outcome before I build and refuse to scale on enthusiasm. Every pilot starts with the number I’m moving and a baseline. Small pilot, measured delta, hard lines. And the tell: I’ll kill a beautiful pilot that demoed brilliantly if it didn’t move the number. Anyone can ship a demo; the strategist says “this one didn’t work” and means it.

5 · Student-owned agent vs accreditation and data

Separate the layers. The learner’s personal environment (tools, sources, agent they keep) and the institutional record of demonstrated competency are different data, and I don’t let them collapse. Render is built this way, no student data by design; the credential lives with the institution, the agent with the student. Certify the competency, don’t own the student’s whole cognitive life.

6 · Biggest way AI makes education worse

Cognitive offloading that looks exactly like learning, excellent work, nothing learned, invisible because the artifact looks like success. Engineer against it: make the thinking visible and assess that, scaffold AI to provoke reasoning not hand over answers, measure whether the human got more capable. After McLuhan: every medium reshapes how we think. If we’re not deliberate, this one teaches learned helplessness beautifully.

7 · Build, buy, or ignore?

Start from the journey and the number I’m moving, never the tool. Filter: does this touch a barrier I’ve measured? Build only what’s core and where owning the pedagogy and data matters (Render). Buy the commodity infrastructure. Ignore the feature masquerading as a strategy and the tool that demos gorgeously but can’t show an outcome.

8 · Does AI widen the gap or close it?

Both, and left alone it widens, the resourced learner gets a superpower, the struggling one gets a fluent wrong answer they can’t challenge. Closing it is a design choice: build for the bottom of the distribution first. CopaMigo aims exactly there. Build for the median and AI will happily widen the gap for you.

9 · A belief I had to abandon

That the tool was the intervention. Cultivate cured me, a nice environment students didn’t adopt because I designed for the idea, not the moment they’d reach for it. Now I start from the barrier and the behavior. Friction and fear do the deciding; design for those and the tool almost disappears.

10 · If AI personalizes the whole path, what’s the institution for? The faculty?

The institution stops delivering content, that role is over. It becomes the thing that certifies a human grew capable, and the community that holds them while they do. The faculty member is less lecturer, more mentor and designer, the person who says “you can do this” at the moment a learner is ready to walk away. Content scales infinitely; belief and belonging don’t. The machine handles the path; the human is why anyone stays on it.

Bonus · What does the future of AI in education look like?

Agentic and adaptive. Agentic: systems that do work on a learner’s behalf, mapping the gap and personalizing in real time. Adaptive: assessment moves to authentic, simulation-based demonstration. Underneath, the non-negotiable: the human stays in the loop and nothing scales until tested for efficacy. Not a prediction I’m waiting on, the thing I’m building toward now.
