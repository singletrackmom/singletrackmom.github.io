The role, decoded

An experienced instructional designer who blends deep learning science, design judgment, and technical fluency to build rigorous, measurable online courses for working professionals. Title is Online Learning Experience Designer (OLED). The work is split between ProEd (professional education, self-paced or facilitated) and 16-week credit-bearing builds alongside a faculty SME.

The unlock in this posting. They wrote: “Some builds will utilize purchased or OER materials without a SME. In these cases, you will work with the content utilizing AI resources and internal expertise to develop a full self-study course.” That is the Course Dialer workflow, in their words. Lead into it: find aligned OER, build with AI, check alignment and AI-resistance, usability test. It is the single strongest bridge from your work to their need. See the OER flagship tab.

Language note. The July notes said never say “instructional design.” The actual posting uses “instructional designer” and “instructional design” repeatedly, and lists an ID master’s as preferred. So do not fear the term. The nuance: the title is Learning Experience Designer, and Sam Spiegel personally draws an OLED-vs-ID distinction (an OLED is educated in both design and learning theory, with outcomes driving the design). Use “learning experience designer” as your title word, speak the learning-theory half fluently, and let ID competencies show in the evidence.

What they will actually screen for

  1. You can build a whole course, not describe one. Outcomes, aligned assessments, scaffolded modules, accessibility, end to end.
  2. Learning-science rigor. Backward design, alignment, adult learning, UDL, a quality standard. Bring the vocabulary.
  3. You work with faculty SMEs (and can also build without one, from OER, using AI).

  5. Design eye and media. Storyboarding, Adobe, scripting. Rare in IDs, and you have a BFA.
  6. Independent project management. Several builds at once on a schedule.
  7. Accessibility and copyright. ADA / WCAG, licensed and OER content with clean attribution.

What Mines Online does, in their words

Engineering Learning, their design framework (know all four)

Their shared language across Mines Online and Trefny. Iterative, not linear. Narrate your own work in these four words.

  1. Design. Measurable learning outcomes, aligned summative assessments, a motivating course description. Together this is the course guide.
  2. Build. Turn the guide into lessons, activities, policies, assessments.
  3. Enact. Teach, gather engagement / performance / survey data, adjust while teaching.
  4. Reflect. Review what worked, consult colleagues, refine. Loops back to Design.

That models good assessment; a guessed number ends the interview.

Do not fabricate a dashboard. If you cannot get a number in time, say “improved” and describe the mechanism, or show the method (how you would measure it). An invented metric is the one thing an assessment scientist will catch and it ends the interview.

How to model good assessment even if you have not run rigorous cycles

Be honest with yourself and strategic with them. The move is not “I have run assessment cycles for years,” it is “I know the model, I have one real close-the-loop example, and I build assessment into the software.”

  • Know the loop: define outcomes → collect evidence (assignment performance, analytics, surveys) → analyze → revise → measure again. That is Reflect in their framework and Evaluation in ADDIE.
  • Show one real close-the-loop: AVC100 vocab quiz → data said it was not discriminating → replaced with applied identification → re-measured.
  • Operationalize it: the Course Dialer bakes assessment in, it checks whether every outcome is actually evidenced by something graded, bands each assessment on AI-resistance, and (the new module) generates an aligned end-of-course survey and course-assessment scaffold.

The Course Dialer assessment feature (your idea, worth building)

Yes, add it, and it is a strong differentiator for this room. It fits the tool’s data-sovereign line because it touches no student data, it is a building and aligning tool. What it can do:

  • Generate an aligned end-of-course survey from the course objectives, ready to drop into the course, so a course that currently collects no feedback can.
  • Course-level assessment scaffold: pick a competency, describe what you are doing and how it is working, then note what to improve, flexible so it follows the college’s evolving model.
  • “Here is how to assess this” output alongside the final package and syllabus: for each outcome, the assessment type that would actually evidence it at the right level.
  • With AI (opt-in, via the bridge): paste back aggregate survey results and it summarizes them into “what to improve” prompts. Aggregate only, never individual student data.
The honest, powerful framing: “Most courses collect no real assessment data, and I have not always either. So I am building the assessment in: my tool checks that every outcome is evidenced, generates the survey, and turns the results into a revision list. I am operationalizing the loop, not just talking about it.” That models good assessment and shows self-aware improvement, which is more credible than claiming a practice you do not have.

Learning-design terminology

The vocabulary to have down cold. Mines calls the role a learning experience designer (LXD), not an instructional designer or an educational technologist, and Spiegel’s distinction is that an LXD is trained in both design and learning theory, so the outcomes drive the design. Each term has a working definition and a quick scenario.

Learning experience design (LXD)

Designing the entire experience a learner moves through, grounded in learning science, so learners reach defined outcomes. Mines separates the LXD from a traditional instructional designer: the ID is “educated in design” and leans on templates and linear flow, while the LXD is educated in design and learning theory and makes the outcomes, not the template, the driver.

In practice: you do not open a course template first, you ask what the learner must be able to do, then design backward to get them there.

Engineering Learning

Mines’ four-phase design framework, shared with the Trefny Center: Design (measurable outcomes, aligned assessments, a motivating course description), Build (the lessons, activities, and assessments), Enact (teach and gather engagement and performance data), Reflect (review and refine). It is iterative, not linear.

In practice: narrate your own work in these four words, “I designed the outcomes, built the modules, and when I enacted it the data told me to change the assessment, so I reflected and revised.”

Backward design (Understanding by Design)

Wiggins and McTighe’s method: start from the desired results (outcomes), then decide what evidence would prove them (assessments), and only then design the learning activities. It prevents activity-for-its-own-sake and unassessed claims.

In practice: before choosing a single reading you write the outcome and the assessment that would demonstrate it, so every activity you add has a job.

Constructive alignment

Biggs’ principle that outcomes, assessments, and activities are mutually aligned: nothing is taught that is not assessed, and nothing is assessed that no outcome claims. The alignment matrix is how you show it.

In practice: you map every outcome to the specific assignment that evidences it; an outcome with no assessment is a gap, an assignment serving no outcome is busywork.

Outcome, objective, competency

Often used loosely, but: a learning outcome is what a learner can do at the end of a course; a learning objective is the smaller, module-level step toward it; a competency is a demonstrable ability, often board- or program-approved, that a course serves. All should be observable and measurable.

In practice: “construct a valid free-body diagram” is an outcome; “identify the forces acting on a body” is an objective under it; the program competency it serves lives on the curriculum site.

Bloom’s Taxonomy (revised)

Anderson and Krathwohl’s six cognitive levels, from lower to higher: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create. Objectives use observable verbs at the intended level; “understand” and “know” are unmeasurable and get replaced.

In practice: you rewrite “students will understand regression” as “students will build and interpret a regression model,” which is observable and sits at Apply.

Scaffolding

Structuring a hard task into supported steps that are gradually removed as the learner gains competence, so they build the skill rather than face it all at once.

In practice: instead of one high-stakes interview at the end of the term, learners answer interview questions in low-stakes reps all semester, so the final is a step, not a cliff.

Formative vs summative assessment

Formative assessment is low-stakes practice that informs learning while it is happening (feedback, self-checks); summative assessment measures achievement at the end (the project, the defense). Formative work should not be graded as if it were a measurement instrument.

In practice: the weekly data-cleaning memo is formative feedback; the capstone report and oral defense are summative.

Authentic assessment

Assessment through a task that mirrors real-world application of the discipline, a real dataset, a client brief, a portfolio, rather than a decontextualized test. Authenticity of task alone does not make an assessment AI-resistant.

In practice: instead of a quiz on statistics, the learner analyzes a real dataset and presents findings to a decision-maker.

AI-resistant assessment / process evidence

An assessment a generative model cannot complete on the learner’s behalf, achieved by moving the evidence from the finished artifact to the record of making it: drafts and version history, response to a specific critique, a live oral defense, a locally-situated prompt, or disclosure-graded AI use. The artifact never protects the assessment; the process does.

In practice: the grade rests on the commit history, a private-test-set prediction, and an oral defense, none of which a model can hand back.

Rubric (analytic vs holistic)

A scoring guide. An analytic rubric scores several criteria separately (each on a scale), which gives targeted feedback; a holistic rubric gives one overall judgment. Competency-based grading sets a threshold (every criterion at proficient or above) rather than averaging to a letter.

In practice: the capstone is scored on eight criteria, and mastery requires each at proficient, so a strong model cannot offset a missing ethics analysis.

Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

The CAST framework: design for variability up front by offering multiple means of engagement (the why), representation (the what), and action and expression (the how). UDL is not accessibility; accessibility asks whether a learner can reach the content, UDL asks whether there is more than one way to engage, perceive, and act on it.

In practice: a deliverable that may be submitted as a written memo or a recorded walkthrough is UDL for action and expression.

Accessibility, WCAG, ADA

Whether every learner can perceive and operate the content. WCAG 2.1 AA is the technical standard (alt text, headings, contrast, captions, keyboard operability); the ADA and Section 508 are the legal drivers. Distinct from UDL, and built in from the first draft, not remediated at the end.

In practice: a figure whose meaning depends on color alone fails WCAG 1.4.1; you add a label so the meaning lives in the words.

ADDIE

The classic instructional-design lifecycle: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation. Systematic and thorough; sometimes criticized as slow or waterfall-like.

In practice: the “Evaluation” phase is where you close the loop with analytics and student data, the step courses most often skip.

SAM (Successive Approximation Model)

Allen’s agile, iterative alternative to ADDIE: prototype, review, revise in rapid cycles rather than one long build.

In practice: “run the course, fix the reds, re-export, run it again” is a SAM loop.

Merrill’s First Principles

Learning is promoted when instruction is problem-centered and cycles through Activation (of prior knowledge), Demonstration, Application, and Integration.

In practice: you flag a module that jumps straight to application with no worked example (no Demonstration) or never asks learners to transfer the skill to their own work (no Integration).

Gagné’s Nine Events of Instruction

A sequenced per-lesson checklist: gain attention, state the objective, recall prior learning, present content, provide guidance, elicit performance, give feedback, assess, enhance retention and transfer.

In practice: a useful audit lens, is the objective visible to students, is there a feedback mechanism, is there a retention or transfer step.

Dick & Carey

A systems model of design in which objectives, assessments, and strategy are all held in mutual alignment; the emphasis is internal consistency among components.

In practice: the lens for catching where one component has drifted out of alignment with another, an assessment that no longer matches its objective.

Connectivism and personal learning environments

Siemens and Downes’ theory that knowledge lives in networks of people and tools and learning is forming and traversing connections; a PLE is the learner’s own assembled set of tools, people, and sources. This is the theoretical floor under your M.Ed.

In practice: you value real audiences, networked tools, and public artifacts, and you flag a closed-loop course with no discipline community or public work.

Seat time and the credit hour (HLC)

The Higher Learning Commission applies the federal credit-hour definition (34 CFR 600.2): one semester credit is about one hour of instruction plus two hours of out-of-class work per week for roughly fifteen weeks, or an equivalent amount of work online, approximately 45 hours per credit. A 3-credit course is about 135 hours of total student engagement.

In practice: you size the workload to the credit and publish an estimated-time note on every assignment so a working adult can plan.

Regular and Substantive Interaction (RSI)

The federal requirement (34 CFR 600.2) that separates distance education from correspondence and protects Title IV eligibility. It must be instructor-initiated and include at least two of five substantive activities: direct instruction, feedback on coursework, answering content questions, facilitating discussion, or an institution-approved activity, on a regular, scheduled basis with proactive outreach.

In practice: a weekly instructor-authored teaching announcement plus timely feedback on graded work satisfies the two-of-five test; an auto-graded quiz does not.

OER and Creative Commons licenses

Open educational resources are teaching materials that are free to use and often to adapt. The license governs reuse: CC BY (adapt with attribution), BY-SA (share alike), BY-NC (non-commercial), BY-ND (no derivatives, link only), public domain (no restrictions). OER is a licensing category, not a quality guarantee, so vet separately.

In practice: you adapt CC BY and public-domain material into course-owned pages, but you only link a no-derivatives text like ISLR, never re-host it.

Quality Matters and OSCQR

The two main online-course-quality rubrics. Quality Matters (QM) is a peer-reviewed standards set with an essential-standards threshold; OSCQR is SUNY’s rubric, now stewarded by OLC, scored on a formative action-plan scale. You are a lead reviewer for both, which is your rigor credential.

In practice: you use the rubric to review a course before formal review, so problems surface as a rehearsal rather than in the evaluation.
