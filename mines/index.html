The role, decoded

An experienced instructional designer who blends deep learning science, design judgment, and technical fluency to build rigorous, measurable online courses for working professionals. Title is Online Learning Experience Designer (OLED). The work is split between ProEd (professional education, self-paced or facilitated) and 16-week credit-bearing builds alongside a faculty SME.

The unlock in this posting. They wrote: “Some builds will utilize purchased or OER materials without a SME. In these cases, you will work with the content utilizing AI resources and internal expertise to develop a full self-study course.” That is the Course Dialer workflow, in their words. Lead into it: find aligned OER, build with AI, check alignment and AI-resistance, usability test. It is the single strongest bridge from your work to their need. See the OER flagship tab.

Language note. The July notes said never say “instructional design.” The actual posting uses “instructional designer” and “instructional design” repeatedly, and lists an ID master’s as preferred. So do not fear the term. The nuance: the title is Learning Experience Designer, and Sam Spiegel personally draws an OLED-vs-ID distinction (an OLED is educated in both design and learning theory, with outcomes driving the design). Use “learning experience designer” as your title word, speak the learning-theory half fluently, and let ID competencies show in the evidence.

What Mines Online does, in their words

Engineering Learning, their design framework (know all four)

Their shared language across Mines Online and Trefny. Iterative, not linear. Narrate your own work in these four words.

  1. Design. Measurable learning outcomes, aligned summative assessments, a motivating course description. Together this is the course guide.
  2. Build. Turn the guide into lessons, activities, policies, assessments.
  3. Enact. Teach, gather engagement / performance / survey data, adjust while teaching.
  4. Reflect. Review what worked, consult colleagues, refine. Loops back to Design.
Your one-to-one hook. This maps onto your AVC100 redesign (outcomes first, aligned low-stakes assessments, revised on Canvas analytics) and onto Render and CopaMigo (built backward from the outcome the learner needs). Say your work in their words without forcing it. Framework page.

The panel (second round)

How would you build a course from OER with no subject-matter expert?

Outcomes first, then find aligned, vetted OER, MIT OpenCourseWare for graduate depth, OpenStax and the Open Textbook Library for peer-reviewed foundations, MERLOT’s peer-reviewed filter, the Mason OER Metafinder to search everything at once. I check the license and attribution, vet quality, and map each asset to an outcome. Then I use AI for the connective tissue: leveling the prose, drafting formative questions and rubrics, alt text. I check alignment and AI-resistance, and I usability test. The one thing AI cannot replace is technical accuracy, so I anchor in pre-vetted graduate sources and budget a short SME review pass as the backstop. I actually built a tool that does the alignment and AI-resistance checks, I can show it.

How do you make an assessment that AI cannot just complete?

You move the evidence from the finished artifact to the record of making it, at the same cognitive level. Process evidence, a decision anchored to something only this learner encountered, a short defense of a choice, disclosure-graded AI use. The artifact never protects the assessment; the record of its making does. For a data-science course that is a real dataset and a walkthrough of the analysis decisions, not a quiz.

How do you use data to improve a course?

Canvas analytics for engagement, on-time submission, and grade distribution, plus outcome attainment and end-of-course surveys. On AVC100 the data flagged an assessment that was not discriminating, so I redesigned it. The principle is the Reflect phase: look at what the evidence says, change one thing, measure again.

What is your experience with STEM content?

My craft is the learning design; the SME owns the discipline. To show it travels, I built a graduate-level module from a real MIT OpenCourseWare course, aligned to one of your online courses, and ran it through my alignment and AI-resistance checks. (Have the flagship ready.) I am comfortable that translating complex material into learning that works is the same job whether it is design or decision analytics.

Tell me about a time you failed or had to change course.

I padded a coverage number once in a tool build to make it look better, and my own rule caught it: never fabricate an outcome. I pulled it back to the honest number. It is a small thing, but it is why the tools report only what they can prove, a check that cannot compute does not fire.

How do you manage several builds at once?

I run concurrent course redesigns, a 10-college study, and tool builds to deadline. I chunk each build into the Design, Build, Enact, Reflect phases, keep a status per module, and protect progress so a long assessment redesign never blocks the rest of a course shipping.

Where is the field going, and how do you stay current?

AI-assisted development that keeps the human in the loop, and assessment that survives generative AI. I am a League for Innovation AI Fellow, I co-chair an AI domain across a ten-college district, and I build the tools rather than just talk about them. The risk is using AI to generate content nobody vetted; the opportunity is using it to do the mechanical work so the designer spends time on judgment.

Metrics and assessment

Assessment is a big part of what they screen for (Megan Sanders is an assessment scientist). You do not need a fabricated dashboard. You need one honest before/after, the vocabulary of good assessment, and proof you build assessment into your tools.

Where your real metrics are

  • Canvas New Analytics, your own sections. As the instructor you have it now: grade distribution, on-time submission, participation, page views, weekly online activity, per-assignment averages. Course → New Analytics → export CSV. This is real, defensible data for AVC100 and AVC248.
  • The before/after you can actually tell. AVC100’s vocab-quiz replacement. Pull the two terms: the old vocab-quiz section and the redesigned applied-identification section, and compare on-time submission or the score distribution. Even a small n is fine if you say so. One real number you can defend beats ten invented ones.
  • Maricopa Institutional Research. Districts usually keep course success (pass) and DFW rates by section, often in a Tableau or institutional dashboard. Request AVC100 and AVC248 success rates by term from IR. Slower, but it is the “before I taught it / after I redesigned it” comparison at the institutional level.
  • Render’s March usability test. Already real data that reshaped the tool. Use it as a “data changed the design” story.
Do not fabricate a dashboard. If you cannot get a number in time, say “improved” and describe the mechanism, or show the method (how you would measure it). An invented metric is the one thing an assessment scientist will catch and it ends the interview.

How to model good assessment even if you have not run rigorous cycles

Be honest with yourself and strategic with them. The move is not “I have run assessment cycles for years,” it is “I know the model, I have one real close-the-loop example, and I build assessment into the software.”

  • Know the loop: define outcomes → collect evidence (assignment performance, analytics, surveys) → analyze → revise → measure again. That is Reflect in their framework and Evaluation in ADDIE.
  • Show one real close-the-loop: AVC100 vocab quiz → data said it was not discriminating → replaced with applied identification → re-measured.
  • Operationalize it: the Course Dialer bakes assessment in, it checks whether every outcome is actually evidenced by something graded, bands each assessment on AI-resistance, and (the new module) generates an aligned end-of-course survey and course-assessment scaffold.

The Course Dialer assessment feature (your idea, worth building)

Yes, add it, and it is a strong differentiator for this room. It fits the tool’s data-sovereign line because it touches no student data, it is a building and aligning tool. What it can do:

  • Generate an aligned end-of-course survey from the course objectives, ready to drop into the course, so a course that currently collects no feedback can.
  • Course-level assessment scaffold: pick a competency, describe what you are doing and how it is working, then note what to improve, flexible so it follows the college’s evolving model.
  • “Here is how to assess this” output alongside the final package and syllabus: for each outcome, the assessment type that would actually evidence it at the right level.
  • With AI (opt-in, via the bridge): paste back aggregate survey results and it summarizes them into “what to improve” prompts. Aggregate only, never individual student data.
The honest, powerful framing: “Most courses collect no real assessment data, and I have not always either. So I am building the assessment in: my tool checks that every outcome is evidenced, generates the survey, and turns the results into a revision list. I am operationalizing the loop, not just talking about it.” That models good assessment and shows self-aware improvement, which is more credible than claiming a practice you do not have.
