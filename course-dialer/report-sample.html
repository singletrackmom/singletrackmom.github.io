Assessment validity under generative AIOSCQR 31, 45
fix

1 graded item could be completed by a generative model

Graded work

Each item below matches a pattern that a current model satisfies in under a minute. This is a construct validity finding, not an integrity accusation. An assessment that something other than the student can satisfy has ceased to measure the student.

Course Reflection (10 pts)
A prose response to a general prompt, submitted as text or a single upload
A model produces a competent version of this from the prompt. Without process evidence, the artifact cannot distinguish the student from the model.

What to do
  1. Move the evidence from the artifact to the process. Require drafts, iteration, and a documented response to critique received. A model can produce the artifact; it cannot produce the student’s revision history in response to a named classmate’s comment.
  2. Situate the prompt. A question answerable without reference to this section, this critique, this local context, is a question answerable without the student. Bind the prompt to something that exists only in this course this term.
  3. Assess the defense, not only the product. A short recorded or live walkthrough of the decisions made converts an artifact into a performance.
  4. Recall quizzes: keep them if they are low-stakes practice, and stop treating the score as evidence of mastery. If the score carries weight, replace it with an application task at the same cognitive level.
  5. Authentic does not mean AI-resistant. A model can write a portfolio case study. The artifact never protects the assessment; the process does.

What to do
  1. Grade the process you already require. Most courses ask for sketches, drafts, and critique and then grade only the final file. The evidence is being produced and discarded. A criterion is all that is needed to capture it.

Can a student use it?

Accessibility, usability, and learner variability.

Accessibility. It verifies the structural properties recoverable from the package: alternative text, descriptive link text, table headers, heading order, typeface, and captions required on video. Contrast is verified against WCAG AA at the moment a palette is chosen, so a failing pair cannot be applied at all.

Limits. It does not open PDFs, and it will not write your alternative text. The instrument locates the images and leaves the sentence to the instructor.

For PDFs, use YuJa Panorama. The district licenses it and it is enabled in Canvas. It appears as a gauge beside every file, performs text recognition on scans, tags headings and reading order, and generates alternative formats a student can download without disclosing a disability to anyone. A scanned PDF is an image of text: a screen reader announces nothing, and the failure is invisible to a sighted instructor because the page looks correct.
Universal Design for LearningCAST framework
pass

Universal Design for Learning is not accessibility

How this section works

Accessibility (WCAG) removes barriers for learners with disabilities. It is a compliance obligation.

Universal Design for Learning designs for learner variability from the outset. It is a pedagogical framework.

A course can be fully WCAG-conformant and still offer a single narrow path through it. Conflating the two is the most common error in the field.

review

Principle 3 · 19 of 27 graded items accept a single submission format

Course-wide

Multiple Means of Action and Expression. Canvas records the submission modes each assessment accepts. Here, 70% of graded items permit exactly one.

A learner who could demonstrate the same competency through a recorded walkthrough, an annotated artifact, or an oral defense has no route to do so. Where submission format is not itself the construct under assessment, it is acting as a barrier.

What to do
  1. For each single-mode item, ask: is the format the competency, or merely the container? A typography assignment must be submitted as a file. A rationale need not be typed.
  2. Where it is the container, admit a second mode. Canvas: open the assignment → Submission Type → Online → check more than one of Text Entry, Website URL, Media Recording, File Upload.
  3. Rubric first. A rubric that says “well-written paragraph” cannot score a spoken defense. Rewrite criteria to describe the competency, not the artifact, and then both modes become gradeable against the same standard.
  4. Start with the reflective and explanatory items. They are where the format is least load-bearing and the barrier is highest.

There is no audio-only pathway. A commuting learner has no way to engage this course without a screen.

What to do
  1. Neither gap is a compliance failure. Both are options a learner does not currently have, and adding one is cheap.
  2. Audio: if your videos already have accurate captions, the transcript is written. Reading it aloud, or exporting the audio track alone, gives you an audio pathway at near-zero cost.
  3. Print: Canvas pages export to PDF from the browser print dialog. One PDF per module, linked in the module overview, covers it.
  4. Do not add modalities for their own sake. The coherence principle (Mayer) holds that extraneous material depresses learning. Add a modality when it opens a path, not when it decorates one.

Principle 1 · 51 expressions of learner choice

Course-wide

Multiple Means of Engagement. Counted across all course text.

Choice language is substantive rather than token. Where learners select their own subject and carry it across the course, this is genuine autonomy, and it is also what makes the work resistant to generative substitution: a model cannot have been to the place the student chose.

What to do
  1. Give learners one durable choice they carry across the whole course rather than many trivial ones. A subject, a client, a destination, a dataset.
  2. The durable choice does the double duty: it drives engagement (self-relevance) and it makes every downstream artifact un-generatable, because the model has no access to the thing they chose.
  3. Distinguish meaningful choice from nominal choice. Picking one of three instructor-supplied prompts is not autonomy. Choosing the thing the work is about is.

What this check cannot evidence

Requires human review

Three UDL indicators are recoverable from a package: accepted submission modes, content modality, and the presence of choice. The remainder are not. Whether options are meaningful rather than nominal, whether scaffolds support self-regulation, and whether the course sustains effort across a term are matters of professional judgment. They are surfaced for review, never scored.

The export stores video references, not video files, so no tool can verify captions from here. Open each one.

The requirement is WCAG 2.1 SC 1.2.2, which mandates captions, not transcripts. Quality is specified by the DCMP Captioning Key. Auto-captions satisfy neither: they run 70 to 90 percent accurate and degrade worst on discipline-specific terminology, which is exactly the content being taught.

What to do
Editing auto-captions IS the standard workflow, and it does satisfy the requirement. You do not need a captioning vendor. You need twenty minutes per video.
First, determine which case you are in. The two cases share no steps, and most faculty guess wrong.
  1. Open the video. Does it play from Canvas Studio, or is it on a YouTube channel you control? Then it is yours, and you can caption it.
  2. Is it embedded or linked from Adobe, a studio, or another creator? Then it is not yours, and you cannot caption it and may not re-upload it.
Case A · Your video, in Canvas Studio
  1. Canvas → Studio in the global (left) navigation → open the video.
  2. Captions tab → select English → Request Captions. Studio generates them in a few minutes.
  3. Now the step that matters, and the step nearly everyone skips. Click Edit and read the track against your audio. Fix the technical vocabulary first: it is what auto-captioning destroys and it is what you are teaching. Keyframe becomes key frame. Bézier becomes anything at all. Bleed, kerning, comp, opacity, alpha, check every one.
  4. Publish. The captions now travel with the video into every course copy.

Yes, this counts. WCAG does not require a human to type from scratch. It requires captions that are accurate and complete. An auto-caption you have corrected is a human caption.

Case A2 · Your video, on YouTube
  1. studio.youtube.com → Content → click the video.
  2. Left sidebar: Subtitles. There will already be a track labelled English (automatic).
  3. Click Duplicate and Edit beside it. This is the whole trick, it converts the auto track into an editable one instead of making you start over.
  4. Play through, correct the terminology, and Publish.
  5. The track is now a real caption track. The “automatic” label is gone, and that label is exactly what a reviewer looks for.
Case B · Someone else’s video. This is where people get stuck.

You cannot add captions to a video you do not own. And you may not download it and re-upload it to caption it, that is a copyright violation, and it is the remedy faculty most often reach for without realising. Your options, best first:

  1. Check whether it already complies. Play it and turn on CC. If the track says English (auto-generated), it does not satisfy the standard. If it says simply English, the owner captioned it and you are done. Many Adobe and major-channel tutorials already have real captions. Check before you do anything else, this resolves it more often than people expect.
  2. Find a captioned equivalent. On YouTube, search your topic and apply the Subtitles/CC filter under Filters. Faster than everything below it.
  3. Use the vendor’s own documentation. helpx.adobe.com is professionally captioned and it will not be deleted out from under your course in two years, which a random YouTube tutorial absolutely will be. For a software demonstration this is usually the correct answer.
  4. Ask the owner. A comment or an email asking them to add captions costs five minutes and sometimes works.
  5. Re-record it yourself. Not the burden it sounds like. A three-minute screen capture in Studio, in your own voice and your own course vocabulary, is usually better pedagogy than a stranger’s video and it resolves the problem permanently.
  6. Amara (amara.org) can caption third-party video without re-uploading it. Note its free public workspace closed in April 2026; it now runs roughly $5 per video.
What does NOT satisfy this standard
  1. A transcript is not a caption. WCAG 1.2.2 requires captions specifically. A deaf student reading a transcript in a separate window cannot watch your cursor and read the words at the same time, which is the entire point of a software demonstration.
  2. Auto-captions left uncorrected are not captions. They are a first draft. 70 to 90 percent accurate, and they fail hardest on the exact words the video exists to teach.
  3. “It has CC available” is not the same as “it is captioned.” Look at the track label.

Institutional Learning Outcomes

Institutional Learning Outcomes · purpose and scoring standard

How this section works

Institutional Learning Outcomes are how your college proves its students can do the things a degree claims they can do. Every student’s attainment is tracked across their whole time in college, first semester to last.

They are not course points. An ILO score rides alongside the grade and is never used to evaluate an individual student or a faculty member. A student can earn an A and score Emerging, and nothing is wrong.

And here is the rule that is almost always applied backwards. The ILO reads “by the end of their college experience…”, so you score against a graduating student, not the one in front of you.

Work that is proficient for your course may be emerging for the ILO. A 100-level student should mostly score Emerging (2). This is the expected distribution, not a deficiency. It is how the institution sees growth over time. If every intro course reports Proficient, the data is worthless and no one can prove students learned anything.

Score honestly. Score low. That is the assignment.

One outcome, one criterion, one assignment

How this section works

Canvas calculates ILO mastery as Highest Score. So attaching an outcome to nine rubrics does not make it more rigorous, it gives more chances at the same number, and it burdens the rubric with criteria that will not be scored.

Attach each ILO once, to the single criterion where the evidence is strongest, late in the course, where the student is at their best.

Three to six ILOs is a well-instrumented course. More than that is theatre.

Each highlighted outcome is work you are already grading. The rubric simply never said what it was for. Attaching one changes nothing about how you teach, and it puts your learners into the institution’s record of what they can do.
The six, and where each one attaches
ILO-VC5 · Analysis: Critique & Interpretation
Find Outcome → Communication Visual
“Analyzes, critiques, and interprets work from a visual, historical, or conceptual framework.”
Attach to: The critique criterion on your last graded project of the term.
The highest-value attachment in most studio courses. Critique frequently constitutes 20 percent of a rubric and maps to no course competency. VC5 names it exactly. Use the last critique of the term, not the first, you want their most mature analysis.
ILO-TE1 · Acquiring: Evaluates Creative Process
Find Outcome → Creative Thinking
“Evaluates creative process and product using domain-appropriate criteria.”
Attach to: The criterion where a learner articulates what they changed after critique, and why.
If your rubric asks a student to name the principle behind a revision, you are already assessing this and not claiming it.
ILO-TE2 · Risks: Seeks Untested Approaches
Find Outcome → Creative Thinking
“Actively seeks out and follows through on untested and potentially risky directions… may include personal risk or risk of failure.”
Attach to: Any project criterion. Consider adding one.
Read this one twice. Most studio rubrics punish creative risk: a learner who attempts something ambitious and lands it at 80 percent scores below one who plays it safe and lands at 95. TE2 is institutional permission to reward the ambitious attempt that does not fully land, and it costs nothing to attach.
ILO-W4 · Job Search: Marketing Self to Employers
Find Outcome → Career Goals & Workplace Skills
“Ability to market themselves and clearly communicate how classes… translate into qualifications for possible careers.”
Attach to: The published portfolio, and specifically its written description.
That criterion describes a portfolio without ever using the word. If your course ends in a published body of work, this attachment is free.
ILO-VC4 · Delivery: Visual Production
Find Outcome → Communication Visual
“Makes compelling choices regarding the delivery of a final visual production; choices reflect a physical or digital environment.”
Attach to: The criterion where a file is prepared for output, resolution, color mode, bleed, delivery size.
Print versus screen is “physical or digital environment.” If you grade whether the file would actually print, you are assessing this.
ILO-VC3 · Execution: Technical Skills
Find Outcome → Communication Visual
“Technically executes an idea… selects appropriate equipment, software, peripherals, and workflow.”
Attach to: The build step with the fullest toolchain, or the criterion assessing how physical work was captured.
“Equipment and peripherals” covers the camera or scanner a learner uses to bring paper work into a digital workflow. Most courses do this and grade only the artifact, never the capture.
Why this matters, and how to score it

An institutional outcome is the college’s answer to the question a degree implies: can our graduates communicate visually, think creatively, use information responsibly, find the support they need? Attainment is tracked across a student’s whole time in college, and a course contributes to that record only where an outcome is bound to a rubric criterion on graded work.

An outcome assessed only inside the major produces data describing majors alone. An introductory course full of non-majors is the most consequential place to attach one, because for many of those students it is not their first data point. It is their only one.

Score against a graduating student, not the one in front of you. Work that is proficient for your course may be emerging against a terminal standard, and emerging is the correct rating at the introductory level. That is the signal, not a deficiency: it is how growth across a degree becomes visible at all. Uniform proficiency in introductory courses renders the longitudinal data uninformative, and the institution loses its ability to demonstrate that its students grew.

Canvas calculates mastery as Highest Score, so one attachment per outcome is sufficient. ILO scores are not grades. They do not affect a student’s standing and are never used to evaluate a student or an instructor.

What to do
How to attach an ILO to a rubric. About two minutes, once.
  1. Open the assignment you want to attach it to.
  2. Scroll to its Rubric and click the pencil to edit. If it has no rubric, create one first. An ILO attaches to a rubric criterion, not to an assignment.
  3. Click Find Outcome.
  4. In the left column, open the domain folder named above.
  5. Select the outcome, read its description to be sure, and click Import.
  6. It is now a criterion on your rubric, scored 0 to 4. Save.
Do not see Outcomes in your course navigation?
  1. Settings → Navigation tab.
  2. Drag Outcomes into the upper list. Save.
Consult your institutional assessment office
  1. Where to ask: ILO Faculty Guide, see “Importing ILO rubric items into Canvas.”

What to do

Deadline stacking is the most common cause of a late-work spike. Students triage, and the item they drop is the one worth fewest points, which is often the one that scaffolds the next unit.

What to do
  1. Move the lowest-stakes item on each stacked date. Canvas: Syllabus shows every date in one table; edit from there rather than opening each assignment.
  2. Aim for no more than two deadlines on a day, and keep the pattern predictable across weeks so students can plan.
