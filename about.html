About

On my first job out of design school, a colleague handed me a printout and said, “Here’s HTML. Learn it.” He had a server under his desk and a website already started. I learned it in a week, and we shipped the company’s first website. I had to learn a tool that was not taught when I was in school, and in a creative field that is not a one-time event. It is the normal condition of the work.

That is the whole idea behind how I teach. The fundamentals stay: grid, hierarchy, typography, composition, the ability to defend a decision out loud. The tools always change. My job is to send students out able to learn the next tool in a week, and to leave with something they keep using after the grade posts, a portfolio, a routing map, a habit.

Under that is real learning experience design. I align activities and assessment to measurable outcomes, design for competency over seat time, and favor authentic work over tests. Accessibility is in the first draft, not a retrofit. I build in the LMS rather than around it, so a course ships as a clean Canvas package, and I design from open and licensed materials, which keeps the cost off students and the copyright clean. The learners I design for are mostly working adults balancing jobs, family, and school, and I was one of them. I earned my master’s in Educational Technology online while working. Connectivism and personal learning environments, learning organized around the individual and their own network of tools, people, and goals, are still the floor under everything I build.

AI is the biggest shift learning has seen, and I am all in. I build with frontier tools every day, because it is not a side interest, it is how I think now. What most AI experimentation skips is the learning science. I design tools that act for students, keep a human in the loop, and get tested for efficacy before anything scales, judged on course-level learning analytics, persistence, and retention rather than features shipped, and held to a hard line on student data: no personally identifiable information, ever.

The question I keep returning to is what still counts as evidence of learning now that a model can produce the artifact. A portfolio piece, a reflective essay, a client rationale: an AI model will write any of them, and they are all authentic tasks. What resists substitution is the record of making. The iteration. The response to critique. The decision a student can defend. So I design assessment around process evidence rather than the finished thing, and I test whether it holds before I ask anyone else to adopt it.

The craft underneath it stays unglamorous. I storyboard, I write the scripts, I record the video, and I write the instructional copy a student actually reads at eleven at night. I run the pilot, and I run the professional development that follows, because a design nobody is trained to teach is a design that quietly fails.

That thinking scales past my classroom. I co-chair a district AI study across ten colleges, mapping where students hit barriers. The method does not change with the size of the audience. Start with the people and the problem, never the technology. Write the requirements down before anything gets built. Prototype, put it in front of real users, watch where it fails, and revise. Measure against learning and retention rather than features shipped, and do not scale anything until the evidence says it works.
