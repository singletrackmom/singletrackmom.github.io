About

AI is the biggest shift in how we work, and I am all in. I build AI tools and processes that solve real problems, for a student, an instructor, or an organization, keep a human in the loop, and test for efficacy before anything scales, judged on whether the problem actually got solved rather than features shipped. I build with frontier tools every day; it is how I think now. What most AI work skips is the learning science, and grounding what I build in how people actually think and learn is my edge.

My method does not change with the size of the audience. Start with the people and the problem, never the technology. Write the requirements down before anything gets built. Prototype, put it in front of real users, watch where it fails, and revise. Measure against whether the problem actually got solved for the people who have it, not features shipped, and do not scale until the evidence says it works. That approach scales past my own campus: I co-chair a district AI study across ten colleges, mapping where students hit barriers reaching the support that already exists.

The question I keep returning to is what still counts as evidence of learning now that an AI model can produce the artifact. A portfolio piece, a reflective essay, a client rationale: an AI model will write any of them, and they are all authentic tasks. What resists substitution is the record of making, the iteration, the response to critique, the decision a student can defend. So I design assessment around process evidence rather than the finished thing, and I test whether it holds before asking anyone else to adopt it. Closing that loop matters more to me than any single result: when a measure is not doing its job, I change the design and measure again. In one course the data showed a vocabulary quiz was not discriminating, so I replaced it with an applied task tied to each student’s own work and re-checked the next term.

Learning-experience design is where I go deepest. I align activities and assessment to measurable outcomes, favor authentic work over tests, and put accessibility in the first draft rather than a retrofit. I build in the LMS rather than around it, so a course ships as a clean Canvas package, and I design from open and licensed materials to keep costs off students and the copyright clean. I earned my master’s in Educational Technology online while working, and connectivism and personal learning environments, learning organized around the individual and their own network of tools, people, and goals, are still the floor under everything I build.

I have been learning the next tool on the job since my first design job out of school, when a colleague handed me a printout and said, learn HTML, and we shipped the company’s first website that month. In a creative field, learning the next tool is never a one-time event. It is the normal condition of the work, and it is why the current shift feels less like disruption and more like the job I have always done.

Bio

Michelle Blomberg is tenured faculty in Digital Media at Glendale Community College, one of the ten Maricopa Community Colleges. She co-chairs the Student Support and Success domain of the district AI Resource Center, where she is leading a student-journey usability study across all ten colleges to find where students hit barriers and where AI can help close the gaps. She has spent her career designing learning experiences in higher education. She began as the Online Learning QA Coordinator for the University of Michigan College of Engineering’s online professional programs, testing every course before its release. She was later a higher-education product manager at the EdTech startup XanEdu, a ProQuest division. At Glendale she led the Digital Media programs and served as Director of Instructional Technology for the Innovation Center. Her Educational Technology MEd research in connectivism and personal learning environments is the foundation under both her teaching philosophy and her recent work building AI tools. She is a League for Innovation AI Fellow, holds an EDUCAUSE microcredential in AI for instructional design, and has been recognized for her work advancing open educational resources (OER) at Glendale.
