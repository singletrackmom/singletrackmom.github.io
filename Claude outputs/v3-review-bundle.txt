## About
 [image:/cultivate/mblomberg.jpg] 
I’m a learning experience designer, AI strategist, and innovator, grounded in learning science, focused on closing the gap between what people are taught and what the work actually demands. I prototype with frontier AI every day, and what most AI work skips is the human science: grounding what I design in how people actually think, learn, and adopt is my edge.
My method doesn’t change with the size of the audience. Write the requirements down before anything gets built. Run structured pilots, then make an honest call: scale, modify, or stop.
The part that takes longest is the people. A tool nobody is trained to use quietly fails, so I plan the training and the support alongside it. I’ve done that for twenty years, starting with bringing a campus onto its first learning management system. It’s the same work now with AI.
Colleges have remarkable resources; what breaks down is the connection between them and the students who need them most, most of them working adults. When students feel connected and supported, they persist, and that’s the problem I focus on now. I co-chair the Student Support and Success domain of the Maricopa district AI Resource Center and sit on its steering committee, working across all ten colleges on how AI can reduce friction in the non-classroom services that decide whether students stay.
My background spans design, education, and educational technology: from web and graphic design, to UX, to product management at an EdTech startup, to seven years directing instructional technology in a campus Innovation Center inside IT, to faculty in Digital Media, where I teach design and was Program Director for over a decade. I hold a master’s in Educational Technology with an adult online-learning emphasis, and connectivism and personal learning environments are still the floor under everything I design. I convened a campus AI community of practice as a League for Innovation AI Fellow.
I start from measurable outcomes: what students need to be able to do when they graduate, including the AI skills their industries already expect. Because the goal is demonstrated skill, students show what they can do through authentic, performance-based work: portfolios, presentations, real job searches, networking. Experiential learning is central to how I teach. I built a design studio where students take on real client work with live briefs and hard deadlines, which grew past the course into a grant-funded paid studio I now advise, and I oversee the program’s internship, placing and mentoring students in real work with local businesses and industry partners. Giving young people genuine ownership, and watching them rise to it, is some of the most important work I do.
The question I keep returning to is what still counts as evidence of learning now that an AI model can produce the artifact. So I design assessment around process evidence rather than the finished thing, and I test whether it holds before asking anyone else to adopt it.

## Dial Your Course
Course Design Tools · Case study
A Canvas course goes in. Nineteen checks run. What to fix comes back.
Dial Your Course reads a real Canvas course package, runs nineteen checks against it, from seat hours to outcome alignment to accessibility, and writes the approved fixes back into the package. Every check is rule-based and contains no AI. A quality standard is a published list, so checking against it is a lookup: the same course returns the same answer every time, and every finding traces back to the sentence in the standard that produced it. A faculty member being told their course falls short deserves that traceability. It began as the checks I performed by hand as a peer and lead reviewer for Quality Matters and OSCQR.

## Build Your Course
Course Design Tools · Case study
Dial Your Course tells you what is wrong. This builds the replacement.
Checking a course and building one are different problems with different trust requirements, and collapsing them into one tool would damage both. The checks are deterministic. Building an assessment, or judging whether an open license permits what you are about to do, is not. So these two tools use AI, and the human stays in every loop. Nothing is applied automatically: every suggestion arrives as a proposed replacement with four choices, apply, edit first, dismiss with a reason, or defer.
 [link:/v3/build/assessment/overview.html] Screenshot to come
Authentic Assessment
Pilot build. Takes an assignment a student could hand to a model and proposes a replacement that asks for process evidence instead.
 [link:/v3/build/oer/overview.html] Screenshot to come
OER Finder
Specified, not built. Finds openly licensed material for a module and records the license that permits the use.
## Status
Authentic Assessment is built and working as a pilot build and has produced results. OER Finder is specified and not built. Both methods were first run by hand on a full course: a complete fifteen-week graduate data science course built from open educational resources in a field I do not teach, with four original simulations for its assessments and a recorded oral defense carrying the grade.

## Render
Student Success Tools · Case study
 [link:/v3/render/overview.html] Overview [link:/v3/render/prd.html] PRD
Training wheels for building agents. Students assemble a career-launch environment by hand across the capstone, and leave owning it.
## Goal
Design students graduate with a portfolio and no method for finding work. The goal is that a student leaves the semester with working career infrastructure they own, rather than a folder of assignments they will never open again.
## Audience
Final-semester students in a capstone course, and the career services staff who would otherwise see them for the first time after graduation.
## Process
Students set goals and pick one real reach job on day one, then build the environment across the AVC 248 capstone. At the end, Render runs a gap analysis between what the student actually built and what that job asks for, and exports the package: their agents and skills as prompt files, a job tracker, their tailored documents, and a personal learning plan. Sign-in is a first name only, kept in the student’s own browser. Usability tested with students in March 2026 and revised from what that surfaced.
## Status
Prototype, in pilot this fall in one section of the AVC 248 capstone. Not in production. The interface is being redesigned against updated requirements. The scheduled agents in the second row run outside Render: they are the same pattern with the training wheels off.

## Job Search Agent
Student Success Tools · Case study
 [link:/v3/render/job-search/overview.html] Overview [link:/v3/render/job-search/prd.html] PRD
A search agent built from the student’s own goals that confirms every job is still open before showing it.
## 1. Summary
A search agent built from the student’s own goals that confirms every job is still open before showing it. A search result is not proof a job exists. Students lose days applying to postings that closed months ago. The goal is a feed of openings that fit the student and are verified live.
## 2. Goal
A search result is not proof a job exists. Students lose days applying to postings that closed months ago. The goal is a feed of openings that fit the student and are verified live.
## 3. Users and context
Capstone students starting a first professional search, who run the agent in their own AI account.
## 4. How it works
The student sets the titles, the location, a pay floor, and what to skip. Before a job reaches the feed, the agent opens the employer’s own careers page and confirms the posting is there. Fit is judged against the posting’s stated requirements, compared with the student’s resume and portfolio.
## 5. Data, privacy, and governance
Each tool collects only what it needs to work and tells the user what that is. Privacy, security and accessibility are reviewed before a pilot starts, not after.
## Status
Prototype, in the fall capstone pilot.

## Mid-Career Job Search Agent
Student Success Tools · Case study
 [link:/v3/render/mid-career-search/overview.html] Overview [link:/v3/render/mid-career-search/prd.html] PRD
A twice-weekly search ranked against a written rubric, with every link opened and checked.
## Goal
A mid-career search drowns in aggregator noise, dead links and roles that do not fit. The goal is a short ranked page of roles the person can realistically land and actually wants.
## Audience
One mid-career job seeker.
## Process
Runs twice a week. It scans named employer boards directly and runs a fixed keyword set against each one. Every role is rated on a five-star rubric against the posting’s stated minimum qualifications, and anything under four stars is left off. Each apply link is opened on the employer’s own page the day of the run and checked for a closed notice, and a tracker is cross-checked so nothing already applied to comes back.
## Status
Built and running twice a week. The rules file records each failure and the rule written to prevent it.

## CopaMigo
Student Success Tools · Case study
Student-facing routing for campus services, so a student asking a question in their own words reaches the right office.
Every campus already offers more support than its students can find. The services exist; students just do not know which office handles their problem, or what it is called. A student describes the situation in plain language, in their own language, and CopaMigo routes them to the right service with a handoff card: the contact, the hours, and what to ask for. Its answers are written rather than retrieved, drawn from the questions students actually bring and shaped with the offices that handle them. Anonymous, no login.

The lesson is the one the student journey study also surfaced: adoption has to be planned before the purchase, not after.

## CopaMigo
Student Success Tools · Case study
 [link:/v3/copamigo/overview.html] Overview [link:/v3/copamigo/prd.html] PRD
Student-facing routing for campus services, so a student asking a question in their own words reaches the right office.
## Goal
Campus service information is organized the way the institution is organized, not the way a student asks. The goal is that a student describing a problem in their own words, in their own language, reaches the right human being with enough context to make the handoff work.
## Audience
Students who do not know the name of the office they need, and the advising and support staff who currently absorb the routing work by hand.
## Process
Fourteen service modules built on more than a hundred hand-verified college URLs, with a campus picker covering all ten district colleges. A student types or speaks the situation and gets an answer in the same language, plus a handoff card carrying contact details, opening hours and what to ask for. Routine questions are answered inline; everything else reaches a person faster. It is anonymous, with no login.
## Status
A working prototype, embedded in the Digital Media Arts program’s Discord, where students are testing it. Not in production. The college purchased an enterprise platform for this need. Implementation has stalled on change management, not on the technology, so CopaMigo continues as the working pilot. The lesson is the one the student journey study also surfaced: adoption has to be planned before the purchase, not after.

## Student Journey Barriers Study
Usability Studies · Case study
 [link:/v3/studies/journey/] Overview [link:/v3/studies/journey/method.html] Method [link:/v3/studies/journey/agents.html] Agents and ethics [link:/v3/studies/journey/progress.html] Progress [link:/v3/studies/journey/next.html] What happens next
A ten-college study of the district student journey, from finding a college through to work, mapping where students hit barriers reaching support. I designed the study and I lead it as co-chair of the Student Support and Success domain, working with advisors, financial aid specialists, and student-facing staff across the district.

## Goal
Set a baseline of how many students use each service now, then find and rank where students hit barriers reaching support by how many students each gap touches, and hand staff located, severity-rated evidence they can act on. A successful AI tool answers routine questions without a booking or an office visit, so help is counted through the tool even as office traffic falls.
## Audience
The Student Support and Success domain of the district AI Resource Center, made up of advisors, financial-aid, and other support staff across all ten colleges, plus the college leaders and faculty who help run the study and recruit the student build teams.
## Process
Build fifty demographic-grounded personas as specialist agents, add one orchestrator agent that sequences the journey and routes the runs, and walk the path from finding the college through to work. Each service is walked at each of the ten colleges until added personas surface no previously unseen barrier, the saturation stopping rule. Log what breaks and how badly with more than one rater, rank the highest-reach gaps, decide where AI fits first, recommend and pilot the most promising fixes to prove they help before anything scales, then have student teams build them as human-in-the-loop tools. Humans decide what to fix. No one is replaced.
The walk runs in three parts by access.
 The three access phases of the study
PhaseWhat it coversWhere it stands
Phase 1Public tasks that need no login.Run twice. A third round is planned.
Phase 2Signed-in tasks, on one sanctioned test account.Not started. Waits on district approval and the data-governance review.
Phase 3Tasks that depend on the district’s incoming Salesforce platform.Not started. Waits on that platform.
## Scope
The study follows the student-services journey, finding and reaching advising, financial aid, basic needs, career services, and the rest, from how a prospective student first finds the system through to a job. It does not go inside the learning management system or the coursework itself; the in-course experience belongs to the Teaching and Learning domain (Domain 2).
## In this domain’s lane
The student-services journey across all ten colleges: finding a college, applying, enrolling, paying, getting advising and support, belonging, and graduating. Mapping where students hit barriers reaching those services, and where AI can lift routine work off staff.
## Owned by another domain
Classroom, curriculum, and faculty tools go to Teaching and Learning (Domain 2). Data governance and security go to Strategy, Security and Governance (Domain 1). Retention and institutional-research data is coordinated with Operational Efficiency (Domain 3).
The domain documents every barrier it finds, but acts only on the ones in its lane. It favors the lightest thing that works: a plug-in or feature configured inside a system already in use, a small purpose-built assistant such as a Google Gem, or a compact agent a student build team can produce with staff. No college is ever asked to rename its own service.
## Technology
 The pieces of the study’s technology and how each works
PieceHow it works
The agentsA multi-agent system: fifty synthetic students, what UX researchers call synthetic users, plus one orchestrator and one research aggregator. Each synthetic student walks a college’s live site the way that student would, narrates what it is looking for, and returns a finding.
How the AI is kept honestA growing “golden set” of known barriers every agent version is re-run against, several runs of each scenario to measure consistency, and versioned persona and agent templates so every finding traces to the exact build that produced it.
Structured outputEvery run returns its finding in a fixed JSON schema: the barrier, its location, the path taken, the local service name, a severity flag, and the persona and campus that produced it.
The log formHuman and AI runs are logged on the same Google Form, so the two are directly comparable. The form collects no email and no student data.
How the gaps get decidedAffinity mapping so themes emerge from the data, then severity by reach: each theme placed on a matrix of how severe it is against how many of the roughly 140,000 students it touches.
Agents carry breadth; people carry judgment. Severity is the one call the agents never make, because the research is clear that it is where AI is least reliable. Findings are candidates until human raters confirm them. [link:/airc-sss/agents.html] How the agents work, and the research behind the method.
## Outcomes
The study produces a prioritized, severity-rated map of where students hit barriers, ranked by how many students each gap touches, alongside a service crosswalk across all ten colleges and a human-in-the-loop AI roadmap routed to the right owners.
## Status
Rollout paused for a change management reset. The research is built and has run: all fifty-two agents, twice across all ten colleges, with every severity tester-assigned and pending human rating. Findings began circulating before there was a plan for how people would receive and act on them, and adoption stalled. The next step is to regroup around the Prosci ADKAR model (awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement), with Prosci training planned to support it, before a third round or any pilot. Findings stay unpublished until the domain confirms them.

The register holds thirty-four candidate barriers. Every severity is tester-assigned and pending human rating. Nothing here is a confirmed district finding, and the barriers themselves stay unpublished until the domain has reviewed them.
## Round log
Round 1a · 27 July 2026 · A district-wide findability sweep, eleven persona runs, and three end-to-end walks of the full enrollment funnel. The same barrier types recurred across colleges within the first handful of runs, which said the instrument was working.
Round 1b · 25 August 2026 · Forty-four scenarios drawn from the bank, narrowed to specific service paths. Four first-pass claims collapsed when the pages were actually opened, which produced a stricter evidence rule for testers.
Round 1c · proposed · A channel-discovery round. Where do students actually go first when they hit a problem?
## What the first two rounds established
A single mechanism accounts for most of what both rounds found, and it is more useful than any individual barrier: retrieval matches vocabulary, not need. A page ranks on a student’s query when it contains the student’s word, and does not when it contains only the service’s name. The services exist. The language of need exists. They sit in different documents.
## The open question shaping the next round
Both rounds so far tested one channel: the public college website. The study assumes a student with a problem goes to the website. That is likely true for some students and it is certainly not true for all of them. A student who needs help could reasonably start at any of these, and the corpus covers exactly one:
The public college website. Rounds 1a and 1b, the only channel tested.
The signed-in student portal. Phase 2, not started.
Canvas, where an enrolled student already spends their time.
The chat widget on the college home page.
A student mobile app, if one exists at that college.
The phone.
A person. An instructor, a friend, a family member who went here.
It does not invalidate the findings. It bounds them: they are findings about the public web, on the public web.

We are not naming specific fixes yet. Those come only after the study is scored and its findings are confirmed. What follows is how a ranked gap becomes a piloted AI fix, who builds it, and how it is judged.
## How a gap becomes a pilot
From a ranked gap to a pilot
Group. When the study is scored, the domain will group barriers into themes.
Rank on two axes. Reach is how many of the roughly 140,000 students would plausibly hit it. Severity is the Nielsen 0 to 4 rating, the mean of at least three independent raters.
Plot, not multiply. The two are plotted on a matrix, not multiplied into one score, so a small friction that every student meets sits alongside a rare but dramatic failure.
Pilot the top. The highest-reach, highest-severity gaps are where the first pilots go.
Prove it before it scales. Each fix runs in staged pilots against the success measures, is revised, and only then rolled out.
Deciding what to fix stays staff-led; no one is replaced.
## The three shapes an AI fix takes
Build a tool. A small, purpose-built assistant, built with staff and, where it fits, by student teams. Human in the loop.
Buy or plug in a tool. An existing, vetted tool configured into a system the colleges already use, when that is the lightest thing that helps.
Create a workflow. An AI-assisted workflow or agent built with the department that owns the service, to take routine, repetitive work off staff.

The tools that come out of this study are built by three multidisciplinary student teams, drawn from across the ten colleges and led by ARC members. They work the way a design studio works: a brief, a client, a deadline, and a deliverable someone is waiting for.
The line is firm. Students build tools. When a fix turns out to be a change in how a department runs its own process, that redesign is led by the domain, working directly with the staff who own the service.
The intent is to pay the build teams. This is skilled, sustained work with a real deliverable, and it should be compensated as an internship, work-study placement, or part-time position rather than absorbed as volunteer time.
## How a team will run: the sprint model
 Roles and ceremonies in the student build-team sprint model
ElementHow it works here
The backlogNot invented by the team. It is the ranked barrier list the study produces, translated into build items. Reach and severity set the order, so the team never argues about what matters most.
The clientThe office that owns the service, represented by a named staff member from the domain. They accept or reject the increment. A student team building a financial aid tool answers to financial aid.
Product directionHeld by the domain, not the team. The domain decides what gets built and in what order; the team decides how to build it.
Sprint lengthTwo weeks, sized to an academic term. A semester yields roughly six sprints, which is enough for a discovery sprint, three build sprints, and two for accessibility, testing, and handoff.
Sprint planningThe team pulls from the top of the backlog only. Anything not in the sprint is not being worked on, which is how twelve students across three teams avoid quietly duplicating each other.
Daily check-inAsynchronous and written, not a standing meeting. Students carry class schedules and jobs, so the cadence has to survive people never being free at the same hour.
Sprint reviewA working demonstration to the staff client, not a status report. The rule is the same one the study applies to itself: show the thing, do not describe it.
RetrospectiveFifteen minutes, three questions, one change carried into the next sprint. Kept short because a long retrospective is the first ceremony students stop attending.
Definition of doneAccessible to WCAG 2.1 AA, tested with at least one student who is not on the team, and documented well enough that the owning office can maintain it. A tool that fails any of these is not done, regardless of how it looks.

## How success is judged
Success is the student’s need met, not an office kept busy. Every fix is measured four ways: whether the need was met, how easy it felt (the Single Ease Question), time to resolution, and persistence over time. These read against a baseline taken before any tool ships. A busy office is not a working journey, and a quiet one is not a failure.
## This is proven elsewhere
Connection-focused, AI-assisted support has moved persistence at other colleges. These are the models the design learns from, not findings of this study.
## Georgia State University
An AI chatbot answering enrollment, financial-aid, and deadline questions cut summer melt by 22 percent and lifted persistence, used most by first-generation and Pell students.
## CUNY ASAP
Wraparound support, advising, money, transit, and tutoring, more than doubled three-year graduation, 53 percent versus 24.6 percent, and has been replicated at more than 60 colleges.
## Valencia College
Connected advising raised the match between students’ plans and the courses they actually took from 43 percent to 60 percent, an Aspen Prize model.
## Caring Campus
Connection as a staff practice, now at more than 120 colleges, the human infrastructure that makes AI-assisted outreach meaningful.

## What Gemini Blocks for Students Under 18
Usability Studies · Case study

Can a student under 18 complete a course assignment that uses Google Gemini? A study by the Student Support and Success domain of the Maricopa AI Resource Center.
In committee review. The district approved Gemini in other areas. Access for students under 18 was the open question, and this study was commissioned to answer it. The report is with the Student Support and Success committee for feedback before it goes to the Faculty Executive Council. Testing ran from 3 to 4 October 2026. Both accounts were run on all 27 example assignments: 14 AI courses, 11 high-risk content courses, and 2 courses added for Deep Research and music generation. The assignments are examples built for demonstration. The results come from one run per step.

## Goal
The study asks one question: can a student under 18 complete an assignment that uses Google Gemini, as written? Where the student is stopped, the study records what was blocked and what the student does instead. The instructor teaches to the course competency either way, so each alternate step is written to keep it.
A meaningful share of Maricopa students are under 18, and Google gives those students a restricted version of Gemini. The study shows what that restriction changes in real coursework, so instructors know where to add an alternate step.

## Audience
Maricopa faculty and faculty leadership, district administration, the AI Resource Center, and Google. Faculty use the results to see whether their own assignments need an “Under 18: do this instead” clause.
## Process
How the study runs
Start from the competency. Each assignment is written from one official Maricopa course competency, as a simulation, case, portfolio or project.
Run it in both accounts. The same prompts, typed the same way, in the under-18 account and the 18 and older account.
Decide: could the assignment be completed? Completed as written, completed with an alternate step, or not completable in Gemini.
Where it was not met, write the fix. The step that stopped the student, and an “Under 18: do this instead” clause for the instructor.

## Content: what Gemini refused in the under-18 account
Google’s documentation does not list these. They were found by running the assignments. The 18 and older account completed each one with the same prompt. The refusals were not consistent. The same account answered other prompts on the same subject: nursing and emergency medicine content, the nude in art, Holocaust denial claims, and both sides of the physician-assisted suicide debate were all answered in full.

Recommended practice for every student. AI deep research should never be the only way a student does research. In any assignment with a research step, students also search library databases and other vetted scholarly sources, and check what the AI cites against them. For a student under 18, the library databases replace Deep Research. For a student 18 and older, they verify it. In this study Deep Research was started twice in the 18 and older account and did not finish a report either time, which is one more reason not to depend on it.
## Proposed solution
The blocks found so far follow patterns, and each one has a workable alternate. So the fix does not have to be found one assignment at a time. The proposal is an under-18 check that an instructor runs on any assignment that uses Gemini. It is proposed, not built, and not yet tested.
Proposed under-18 check for an assignment
Compare the assignment to the patterns this study found. Does a step use Deep Research, music, image editing or video? Does it ask for a role-play with violence or thoughts of death, an analysis of one person’s drug use, a clinical report on sexual health, or a security scan evaluation?
Draft the alternate. A prompt, or a check inside the Dial Your Course tool, writes an “Under 18: do this instead” clause for each step that matches a pattern, keeping the same competency.
Verify in an under-18 test account. Run the flagged steps once. This is the only reliable test.
Add the clause to the assignment. Every student gets the same assignment, and the clause tells a student under 18 what to do at that step.
Why the third step is needed: Gemini cannot reliably predict its own under-18 filter. In this study the same account answered one prompt and refused the next on the same subject. A prompt can draft a good alternate. It cannot promise the original will be blocked or that the alternate will pass.

## AI Opportunity Pipeline
Case study · AI Tools & Strategy
 [link:/pipeline/overview.html] Overview [link:/pipeline/] Prototype [link:/pipeline/prd.html] PRD
An inventory of the AI tools ten colleges already have, and the front door for what they want next. Early stage, in development. Not a finished prototype yet.
 [image:/pipeline/pipeline_cover.jpg] 
## Goal
Give the Student Support and Success domain of the Maricopa AI Resource Center one front door for AI ideas, and one consistent, written way to decide what happens to everything that comes through it.
Without a front door, the same product gets licensed separately by several colleges, a request is answered by whoever happened to be asked, and nobody writes down a decline. The pipeline exists so that every idea leaves with an answer and a reason, and so the answer is sometimes “this is not an AI problem.”
## Audience
Student services staff and leadership across the ten Maricopa colleges, most of them advisors and support staff, and the domain members who assess what comes in. It exists to take routine work off people, not to replace anyone, and nothing moves forward in a department without that department.
## Process
The pipeline does not start with requests. It starts with an inventory, because the cheapest solution to most problems is something another college is already running.
## What is built, and what is designed
Built in the prototype. The inventory of what colleges already have, its dashboard, the submission form, and a decision log for inventory entries.
Designed, not yet built. The assessment of requests and their dispositions. A request submitted through the form is saved and listed as new. Nothing scores it yet.
## Two tracks, one register
What exists. Each college reports the AI tools and automated workflows it already has, how each is licensed, and whether it is helping. Every entry ends at one of five answers: share it across colleges, consolidate to a district license, replace it with something built, retire it, or leave it as is.
What is wanted. Requests for a build, for training, or for a workflow audit. Every request ends at one of four answers: pilot, discovery, not an AI problem, or declined.
## How an entry is assessed
The design is that six dimensions structure the conversation: student impact, staff time returned, readiness, risk, feasibility, and scale. They are a conversation structure, not a formula. The submission form asks each department to walk through its current process, which is how an AI problem is told apart from a process problem.
## What gets recorded
A decision log holds every assessed entry, its answer, and the written reason. Declines and not-an-AI-problem calls are shown as prominently as pilots, because a function that only reports its approvals is not doing prioritization.
## What the review changed
An AI director in district information technology reviewed the model in September 2026. The review found that privacy, security, and accessibility should be pass-or-fail gates checked before any scoring, that scale should multiply value instead of sitting beside it, that risk should score its worst axis and not its average, and that four dispositions are too few. Those revisions are the next increment, and they are listed in the PRD.
## Technology
A single self-contained HTML file: vanilla JavaScript, no framework, no backend.
Bar and stacked charts drawn in code, with no chart library.
localStorage for entries submitted in the prototype, so nothing leaves the browser.
Light and dark themes.
No AI model in the tool. People do the assessing.
Designed and built with Claude as the coding assistant.
Hosted on GitHub Pages.
## Status
Early-stage prototype. The domain committee is reviewing it and sending revisions. Phase one, the inventory, is partly built and is not a finished prototype yet. The request side takes a submission but does not assess it yet. It runs on sample data, with three records verified from real reporting and marked as such, and it has not taken a live request. Nothing shown in it is a district finding.
The prototype demonstrates the operating model, not the tooling. In a district with a service management platform, this function belongs in that platform, and settling that question is part of the testing. Next: revise the model from the September review, calibrate it against real past requests, then open the inventory round with the colleges.

An intake, assessment, and decision register for AI ideas across a ten-college district. Early stage, in development. Not a finished prototype yet. It has taken no live requests.

## 1. Summary
The AI Opportunity Pipeline is a front door for AI ideas and a consistent way to decide what happens to each one. It is designed to collect what colleges already have, take requests for what they want, assess each entry on the same dimensions, and record the decision with a written reason. The prototype builds the first part: the inventory, its dashboard, the submission form, and a decision log for inventory entries. Assessing requests is designed and not yet built. The prototype runs on sample data. It has not launched and has not taken a live request.
## 2. Goal
Every AI idea that reaches the Student Support and Success domain leaves with an answer and a reason, reached the same way each time.
Three rules follow from that goal.
Inventory comes before requests. The cheapest fix is usually something another college already runs.
“Not an AI problem” is a real answer. A process, policy, or staffing matter is documented and routed to whoever owns it.
Declines are recorded as visibly as approvals.
## 3. Users and context
Two groups use it. Student services staff and leaders at the ten Maricopa colleges submit what they have and what they need. Most are advisors and support staff with no time for a long form, so the form takes a few minutes and accepts “I do not know” as a useful answer. Members of the domain assess what comes in and record the decision.
The context is a district where the same service carries a different name at every college, where several colleges may license the same product without knowing it, and where there is no shared place to ask for help with AI.
## 4. How it works
## The submission form
One form serves both tracks. It asks who is submitting, how many students the office serves, what tools it already has and how they are licensed, whether those tools are helping, which questions staff answer over and over, where students get stuck before they reach the office, and what would help. Its last question asks the department to walk through the current process. That answer is how an AI problem is told apart from a process problem.
## Track one, what exists
Each reported tool or automated workflow is logged with its college, category, license, and how it is reported to be working. Each ends at one of five answers: share across colleges, consolidate to a district license, replace with something built, retire, or leave as is.
## Track two, what is wanted
Requests for a build, for training, or for a workflow audit. Each ends at one of four answers: pilot, discovery, not an AI problem, or declined. This track opens after the inventory is out. In the prototype, a submitted request is saved and listed as new. The dispositions are designed and not yet built.
## Assessment
Six dimensions structure the conversation: student impact, staff time returned, readiness, risk, feasibility, and scale. They are a conversation structure, not a formula, and the prototype describes them on its Method screen. It does not yet record an assessment against them. This prototype is the source of truth for the dimensions until the revisions in section 10 are made.
## Dashboard and decision log
The dashboard shows college coverage, products duplicated across colleges, how tools are licensed, what kinds of tools exist, whether they are working, and where the repetitive work is. The decision log lists every assessed entry with its answer and the reason.
## 5. Data, privacy, and governance
The prototype holds sample data. Three records are verified from real reporting and are marked verified. Everything else is marked sample.
It collects no student data. The form asks about a department’s tools and processes, and for counts of students served, not for any student record.
A submission in the prototype is saved only in the submitter’s own browser. Nothing is sent to a server.
A launched version would hold staff names, departments, and decisions, so it needs sign-in, access control, and an audit trail. The prototype has none of these, which is one reason it is not launched.
Proposed tools would be checked for privacy, security, and accessibility as pass-or-fail gates before any scoring. See section 10.
## 6. Build and portability
One self-contained HTML file. Vanilla JavaScript, no framework, no backend, no account.
No AI model and no API key. People do the assessing.
Charts are drawn in code with no chart library. Light and dark themes.
Hosted on GitHub Pages.
The operating model is the portable part. In an enterprise information technology environment this function lives in the service management platform, as a catalog item with a workflow, an assessment record, and a reporting layer. ServiceNow, TeamDynamix, and Jira Service Management all do this. The prototype demonstrates the model, not the tooling.
## 7. Pilot and testing
Testing happens before launch, in this order.
Settle one set of assessment criteria. Earlier drafts described the dimensions three different ways. The prototype’s six are the starting point.
Revise the model from the September 2026 review by an AI director in district information technology. The specific changes are in section 10.
Calibrate on eight to ten real past requests. Score each one after the fact and compare the result to the decision that was made. Each disagreement is either a flaw in the model or a decision worth revisiting.
Build the assessment record and the request dispositions into the prototype, or configure them in a district platform.
Run the inventory round with the ten colleges.
Open the request track to a small number of real requests, and take at least one through to a decline.
## 8. Definition of success
Every entry leaves with a disposition and a written reason.
On the calibration set, the model’s result and the real decision agree, or the disagreement is explained.
A department can complete the form in a few minutes without help.
A request cannot become a pilot unless the requester names one measure and its current baseline.
A decline is accepted because the criteria were published before the request arrived.
The platform question is answered: build on this prototype, or configure the model in a system the district already owns.
## 9. Rollout
Phase one. Inventory of what already exists at each college.
Phase two. The request track, opened after the inventory is published.
Out of scope. Building the solutions, making purchasing decisions, and anything that turns out not to be about AI. Those are routed to their owners.
## 10. Open questions and risks
Gates before scores. Privacy, security, and accessibility should be pass or fail, checked before anything is scored. A tool that fails one does not get a middling score. It does not proceed.
Scale is a multiplier. Scored beside the other dimensions, a district-wide low-value item outranks a high-value single-college one. Value should be impact multiplied by reach.
Risk should score its worst axis. A tool that is fine on six kinds of risk and severe on one should not average out to medium.
No measure of effort. The model can rank but cannot sequence. Adding a simple size estimate would let priority account for effort.
Missing dimensions. Strategic alignment and urgency are not assessed.
Four dispositions are too few. Missing: route to something the district already has, refer to another owner, defer with a named blocker and a revisit date, and what happens after a pilot (scale, sustain, or sunset).
Build or buy. If the district already licenses a service management platform, a homegrown intake tool is the kind of request this pipeline would redirect.
One maintainer. A tool kept up by one faculty member on top of a full teaching load is a risk in itself.
## 11. Roadmap
September 2026. Prototype built. Model reviewed by district information technology.
Fall 2026. One criteria set. Model revised. Calibration on past requests.
After calibration. Inventory round with the colleges, then the first real requests.
Before any launch. Decide the platform, and add sign-in, access control, and an audit trail.

## Dial Your Course
Case study · AI Tools & Strategy · Learning Design
 [link:/course-dialer/overview.html] Overview [link:/course-dialer/] Live tool [link:/course-dialer/report-sample.html] Sample report [link:/course-dialer/workload.html] Student workload [link:/course-dialer/prd.html] PRD
Drop a Canvas course in. Nineteen checks run against it: seat hours, outcome alignment, accessibility, AI resistance. Approve the fixes, get the course back.
 [image:/course-dialer/cover.jpg?v=2] 
## Goal
This started with a simple question: how does an online course show it delivers the student hours a credit promises? A credit carries a set number of student hours, and the syllabus commits to them. Online, that evidence lives in the course package itself, so a clean, consistent way to check it helps.
Course review is usually done by reading, one course at a time. Dial Your Course checks the countable things in a single, consistent pass and hands the course back with fixes ready to approve.
It answers three questions. Does the work fit the hours the credit allows? Are the objectives written in verbs a student can be observed performing? Is every outcome the course claims actually evidenced by something it grades? Assessment validity follows from there: which assignments could a model complete on its own, and what process evidence does the rubric already collect?
This did not begin with a rubric. It began with the checks performed by hand, cycle after cycle, as a peer and lead reviewer for Quality Matters and OSCQR. The tool automates the countable part of that review and declines to automate the part that takes judgment.
## Audience
Faculty and instructional designers preparing an online course for launch or review. No technical background assumed.
Formal review comes once a year, and for most faculty it is the first time anyone reads their course closely. That is a hard moment to discover a missing alt attribute, an outcome that was never attached to a rubric, or a module that drifted past the hours the credit allows. This is a rehearsal for that review, not a replacement for it. It reports to you and nobody else. Nothing is submitted, scored, or sent to an administrator.
## Process
Export the course from Canvas, drop the file in, choose the checks. Nineteen of them run. Every finding names the standard it comes from and where in the course to fix it. Approve the changes you want, and the tool writes them back and hands you a package ready to reimport, plus a short list of the few things only Canvas itself can do.
Your course content never leaves your browser. No account, no server, no vendor, and no AI reads your course. A Canvas export contains no student data by design, so none enters the tool, and nothing you drop in is uploaded, stored, or used to train any model.
It reports only what it can prove. A check that cannot be computed is never reported as a pass, and nothing changes without your approval.
## Technology
A browser-based tool: vanilla JavaScript, no backend, no account, no database.
Reads a Canvas Common Cartridge (.imscc) export and writes a corrected package back, entirely on your own device.
The nineteen checks are deterministic rules, not AI: seat hours, outcome alignment, accessibility, workload, AI-resistance. Your course is never sent to any AI to be read, stored, or used for training.
Applies the SUNY OSCQR rubric alongside its own alignment and workload criteria.
Optional AI is a future feature only, for the authentic-assessment module, and is designed to run solely through the institution’s sanctioned AI (for example a district Google agreement, under which institutional content is not retained and is not used to train the model). ## Syllabus Checker
Case study · AI Tools & Strategy
 [link:/syllabus-checker/overview.html] Overview [link:/syllabus-checker/] Live tool [link:/syllabus-checker/prd.html] PRD
Submit a syllabus. It checks the required district language and the seat-hour statements, names exactly what is missing, and shows the department who has complied.
 [image:/syllabus-checker/cover.jpg] 
## Goal
Every term, each syllabus is reviewed by hand against the MCCCD required elements, program-specific additions, and approved institutional language that must appear verbatim rather than paraphrased. The review is slow, its consistency depends on who performs it, and the feedback loop is long enough that an instructor may learn in week six that a required statement was absent in week one. Meanwhile the department has no consolidated view of who has complied.
## Audience
Two distinct users with distinct needs. The instructor must act, and therefore requires specific, actionable feedback. The department must monitor, and therefore requires an aggregate view. Conflating these is the design error most compliance systems make.
## Process
Faculty submit through the department’s existing Google Form, preserving established practice. Submission triggers a review against the MCCCD Regulation 3.6 required elements, program additions, and the approved seat-hour and Regular and Substantive Interaction language. The instructor receives a response within seconds: confirmation of completeness, or an itemized account of what is absent, with the correct approved wording supplied inline so that no further search is required.
The result is simultaneously written back into the form’s existing response spreadsheet as status columns, giving the department a single sortable view of compliance across all sections without generating an inbox of individual reports.
The system is read-only. It reports, it never revises. No instructor should discover that an automated process has silently altered a document they are accountable for.
## Technology
Built on the department’s existing Google Form and its response spreadsheet with Google Apps Script, so there is nothing new to learn and nothing leaves the district’s Google environment.

## Design Studio
Case study · Teaching/Program Design
Real clients, real briefs, real deadlines. For a decade this course sent students into live work from campus partners and had them ship it, from a 90-foot mural to motion, print, publications, and an app. It outgrew the course, won a grant, and now runs as a paid studio, four students at $18 an hour.

## Goal
Put students in front of actual clients and actual deadlines, so they graduate with professional work rather than class assignments. Every project on this page was made inside the course.
The design of it is the point. In an ordinary assignment the instructor is the audience, so the student is really performing for a grade. Here a client is waiting, the work goes out into the world, and the student has to defend the choices to someone who did not assign them. The assessment is the professional task. There is no way to fake it, no way to paste it, and nothing a model can hand back that a client will accept.
## Audience
Students first, and not only design students. The studio is a multidisciplinary group: film, photography, animation, graphic design, and sound design students, who we bring in often on the videos, because a real client brief rarely stays inside one discipline. Campus partners second: the offices, programs, and committees who need this work and have no creative staff of their own. Both are real, and neither is a simulation of the other.
## Process
Students work in teams, and one student on each team serves as the project manager. That student writes the design brief and the timeline, interfaces directly with the client, and keeps the team on track. Not the instructor. The brief names the client, the goal, the approach, and what the client actually needs; the timeline carries the proof cycles, the client approval gates, and the delivery date. Teams run in Basecamp, Slack, Miro, and Discord.
While it ran as a course, the instructor’s job was to decide which projects the studio took on and in what order, and to hold the standard. The students’ job was to run it. A student who has written a brief, held a timeline, and told a client that a date is slipping has done something no assignment can simulate.
The boards below are the working evidence, not a reconstruction. Both were built by student teams while the work was live.
 [image:/studio/miro1.jpg] 
 The OER promo board. Team roles, stakeholders, a dated timeline from kickoff to final delivery, storyboards, character sheets, script drafts, color and type, sound planning, and a dot vote to decide.
 [image:/studio/miro2.jpg] 
 The Traveler board. A concept statement, a decade of design history per chapter, an alumni questionnaire, an icon library, and a timeline that includes presenting the concept to the college president.
So they leave with two things, not one. They leave with the work, and they leave having practiced real project management methodology: scoping against a client’s actual need, sequencing a build, running a proof and approval cycle, tracking a team, and delivering on a date they committed to in writing. Designers are rarely taught any of it, and it is the thing that decides who gets to lead.
Which is why it belongs on a student’s resume as an internship, because that is what it is. They had a client, a brief, a deadline, and a deliverable that shipped. The only difference between this and an internship is that it happened to be worth credit.
The model outgrew the course. We wrote a grant, it came through, and the class no longer runs, because it did not need to. The studio now employs four paid students each semester, drawn from film, photography, animation, and design, doing client work for a wage instead of for credit. Students who want the experience without the job take an internship placed with a client instead.
A film faculty member runs it now. It is advised by three faculty, one from animation, one from photography, and one from design. Handing it off was the point. The work students made inside the course is what made the case for funding it, and it outlasted any one instructor.
## Technology
Student teams running on Basecamp, Slack, Miro, and Discord, with one student per team acting as project manager over the brief, the timeline, and direct client contact.
Adobe Creative Cloud across the work: motion, print, publications, illustration, and interface design.
A brief-and-timeline workflow with proof cycles, client approval gates, and a committed delivery date, the same project-management methodology a professional studio runs on.

## Nobody knew the free-textbook filter existed
Client · Open Educational Resources Committee
 [link:https://mcli.maricopa.edu/resources/open-maricopa] Open Maricopa, formerly Maricopa Millions, is a district program created by district colleagues. It funds faculty to build their own course materials instead of assigning a textbook, and it has saved students more than $36 million. Students see it as a filter in course search, and that is the problem: a filter nobody notices saves nobody anything, which makes this an adoption problem rather than an engineering one. The college’s OER committee brought it to the studio as a brief with one hard constraint. The piece had to play on Gaucho TV and the screens in the student union, and those screens have no sound. ## Render, AI Student Career Launch Framework
Glendale Community College · AVC 248 Integration · singletrackmom.github.io/render · 2025 to Present
A connectivist personal learning environment for students developed in conjunction with Career Services. Gives final-semester students one place to use compartmentalized AI prompting to create their own projects and learning goals, track job search or freelance work, build their portfolio pipeline, and maintain career infrastructure after graduation. Seven modules map to a formal career-launch competency taxonomy. Owned end to end from discovery and user research through learning-experience and journey design, requirements, build, and usability testing. Students choose one real job on day one and build the environment against it across the term; at the capstone Render runs a skills-gap analysis between what a student actually built and what that job requires, then exports a portable career agent and a personal learning plan the student keeps and runs in any AI tool after graduation. It is a single self-contained HTML file with vanilla JavaScript, fifteen AI functions on the Claude API, Google Sheets and Apps Script for an anonymous Career Services data flow, and no student PII, first name only, stored on the student’s own device. Usability tested with students in March 2026 and refined from what it surfaced. Prototyping in HTML/CSS/Vanilla JS using Claude AI; student pilot underway Fall 2026. ## District Student Support & Success AI Strategy
