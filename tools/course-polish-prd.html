Dial Your Course

Product Requirements Document · OSCQR course review and remediation

A faculty-facing tool that audits an online course and fixes what it finds

Version: 0.2 · Status: Prototype, validated against a real course

Michelle B. · Digital Media Arts Faculty · Glendale Community College

1. Executive Summary

The Problem

Getting an online course ready to teach means checking dozens of things by hand, every term, across every module: alt text, heading structure, color contrast, whether due dates land on the days you promised, whether every activity maps to an official competency, whether the required seat-hours language is present and worded exactly as approved. Nobody has time to do this thoroughly, so it gets done partially, or not at all, and problems surface during a quality review or an accreditation visit.

Meanwhile a second, quieter problem: Canvas silently destroys formatting. Its editor sanitizes HTML on save and discards style blocks, font links, and CSS classes. Faculty paste in a carefully designed page, it arrives plain, and nobody can explain why. Most people conclude Canvas just looks bad and give up.

The Solution

Dial Your Course takes a Canvas course export, reviews the whole thing in one pass, and fixes what it can rather than only reporting. It restyles every page to one accessible palette and typeface, converts stripped formatting into inline styles that Canvas cannot discard, drafts the required compliance language, and hands back a package ready to import.

It is called Polish rather than Auditor on purpose. An audit looks and reports. This looks, reports, and repairs and calling it an audit undersells what it does while also making faculty defensive before they open it.

1.1 Audience

Faculty and instructional designers preparing an online course for launch or a quality review. Primary user is a faculty member who is not a developer, does not want to learn HTML, and has a week to get three courses ready.

Secondary user is a department or division that needs consistency across many sections taught by many people.

2. Product Vision

What if the tedious half of course prep, the part that is mechanical and checkable, was actually done mechanically, so the human hours went to the part that needs judgment?

Most course-quality tools stop at a checklist. A checklist is a to-do list you now have to do. Dial Your Course does the work that can be done deterministically and leaves you a shorter, sharper list of the things only a person can decide.

3. Data and Privacy

3.1 There is no student data to collect

This is the foundational design fact and it is worth stating precisely, because it is stronger than a privacy policy.

A Canvas course export (.imscc IMS Common Cartridge) intentionally excludes student enrollments, grades, assignment submissions, quiz attempts, and student-authored discussion posts. Instructure strips them from the package for FERPA compliance. The export contains course content: pages, assignments, quizzes, question banks, discussions (the prompts, not the replies), modules, files, and settings.

So Dial Your Course does not need a privacy policy that promises not to misuse student data. It never receives any. There is nothing in the package to collect, leak, or retain.

3.2 Nothing is transmitted anywhere

The .imscc is a zip of HTML. Opening it, rewriting the HTML, and rezipping it are all operations a browser can perform on its own. There is no server, no account, no upload, no vendor in the middle, and nothing retained after the tab closes.

This is a deliberate position, not a convenience. The dominant tools in this category work by shipping a course to a vendor cloud. Dial Your Course does not need to, and a district evaluating it can verify that claim by watching the network tab.

3.3 The one real residual risk: Files

The export does include everything uploaded to course Files. If an instructor ever put a roster spreadsheet, a gradebook CSV, or a graded example with a student name into Files, that ships in the package. This is not a Canvas flaw or a tool flaw. It is a thing people do.

Requirement: Dial Your Course scans the package for likely PII and flags it as a finding. Candidate signals: .csv and .xlsx files, filenames containing "roster," "grades," "gradebook," or "list," and documents containing patterns that look like student ID numbers. It flags, it does not delete, because deleting someone's file without asking is worse than the problem.

3.4 On sandbox courses

Building in a sandbox shell rather than a live course is good hygiene and worth continuing. But it is not what protects student data here, and the tool should not claim it is. The export format is. Requiring a sandbox would imply the tool is unsafe without one, which is untrue and a weaker story than the truth.

4. Features

4.1 What this tool does NOT do, and why

The single most important scoping decision. GCC already runs YuJa Panorama inside Canvas. Panorama does AI-driven PDF remediation (text recognition, tagging, table structure, reading order), suggests alt text, checks contrast and heading structure, scores every file 0 to 100, and generates alternative formats (audio, ePub, Braille, HTML). It is paid for and it is good at this.

Dial Your Course must not rebuild any of it. A half-rebuilt Panorama is worse than no Panorama, and rebuilding a tool the college already owns is the fastest way to have this rejected.

ThingCan Dial Your Course do it?Who does
Remediate a PDF (tagging, reading order, tables)No. The PDF in the package is a binary. Real remediation is genuinely hard work.YuJa Panorama, Remediation Max
Write alt text for an imageNo. It can detect a missing alt attribute, but it cannot know what the image shows without a vision model.YuJa Panorama
Check whether a video has captionsNo, and structurally cannot. The export contains links to Studio and YouTube videos, not the video files. There is nothing in the package to inspect.Canvas Studio / YouTube

Requirement: the accessibility section is a handoff report, not a checker. It counts what exists and routes it to the right tool:

  • "This course embeds 14 videos. Verify captions in Studio. Auto-captions are not sufficient."
  • "6 PDFs are attached. Check their Panorama scores and remediate there."
  • "3 images have no alt attribute. Panorama can suggest text."
  • The content-strategy recommendation, which no other tool gives you: "4 of these PDFs are lecture notes or handouts. Consider rebuilding them as Canvas pages. A Canvas page is accessible by default, is readable on a phone, and never needs remediating again. Remediating a PDF every term is a treadmill; replacing it is a fix."

4.3 Style guide, and the Canvas problem it solves

Canvas sanitizes HTML on save. It strips <style> blocks, <link> tags, and CSS classes with no stylesheet behind them. Only inline style="" attributes survive. This is why faculty formatting disappears, and almost nobody knows it.

Requirements:

  • Everything the tool writes is fully inline styled. No style blocks, no classes, no external CSS. This is the whole reason the output holds.
  • Five palettes solid colors only, never gradients. Every text-on-background pair in every palette must clear WCAG AA (4.5:1). The tool shows the live contrast ratio for each pair, so a failing combination cannot ship. Currently the lowest ratio across all five palettes is 4.86:1.
  • Five typefaces only. Georgia, Palatino, Helvetica/Arial, Trebuchet, Verdana. This constraint is not aesthetic conservatism: Canvas strips the <link> that loads a web font, so a Google Font fails silently. The editor preview looks correct while students see a fallback. These five are installed nearly everywhere and actually render.
  • Components: header in a solid color box, colored subheadings, callout box, rubric table with a colored header row.
  • Diagnostic findings, not just fixes. The tool reports which pages contain stripped formatting (and have therefore been rendering plain without the instructor knowing) and which web fonts are failing to load.

Known constraints on the inline approach, stated honestly:

  • Canvas allow-lists which CSS properties survive inline. Common layout and color properties are kept. Exotic ones (position transform animation) are stripped. The output is deliberately plain, solid colors, boxes, tables, partly for this reason. There is nothing fancy to lose.
  • Editing a styled page in the Rich Content Editor can reflow inline styles. Once a page is styled, it should be edited in HTML view. This needs to be said in the interface, not buried, because it is the most likely way a user breaks their own result.
  • Not yet verified against a live Canvas instance. The approach follows from how the Canvas sanitizer works, but it has not been tested end to end on a real course. Validation step: paste one styled block into the Canvas HTML editor, save, reload, inspect. This is the first thing to do before building the parser.

4.4 Seat hours

A syllabus promises a student a number of hours. Nobody checks whether the course actually contains them. Dial Your Course does the arithmetic.

The rule: total student hours = credits × 40. Always. Load hours do not change the total; they change the split between direct instruction, in-class studio time, and outside work. A 3-credit course is 120 hours whether it loads at 3, 4, 5, or 6.

This is the thing that is easy to get backwards. A 3-credit studio loading at 6 is not the same student workload profile as a 3-credit lecture, and scoring them the same is how a course drifts out of alignment with what its syllabus claims.

Requirements:

  • Course profile selector: credits, load hours, activity type, modality. Profiles come from the department reference sheet as a lookup table, not a derived formula because the approved language is fixed text.
  • Estimate each module and write the estimate into the module introduction so students know what they are signing up for.
  • Flag when the course exceeds its budget when a single week is overloaded, and when the same deliverable is assigned twice (which quietly bills students for the same hours in two places).
  • Verify the approved seat-hours statement appears verbatim. This is a string comparison, deliberately not an AI judgment.
  • Every estimate is a draft for the instructor to confirm, never published silently. A wrong number in a module intro is worse than no number, because students plan around it. The tool proposes, the human approves.

4.5 Report

  • Findings grouped by category, each with severity (needs change / review / good) and location.
  • Each finding has a free-text box ("how do you want to change this?") and a mark resolved checkbox.
  • Running counts: flagged, need changes, to review, resolved.
  • Exportable report, and an exported .imscc with the fixes applied.

5. The AI Question

5.1 The core needs no model

Worth stating plainly, because it is the thing that makes this buildable today with no budget and no procurement. Every check unique to Dial Your Course is deterministic. Seat hours is arithmetic. Verbatim language is a string comparison. Due dates are date math. The style guide is a transformation. Duplicate detection is comparison. The accessibility handoff is counting.

The tool ships, and is genuinely useful, with the model turned off.

5.2 Where a model would help, and what it costs

Three checks need judgment: are these objectives measurable, are these directions complete and in order, is this a typo. These are real value, but they are the smaller half, and Canvas and Panorama already cover some of it.

The path: Gemini via Google Workspace. Maricopa runs Google Workspace, and the Gemini API is callable directly from Apps Script (the GeminiApp library exists for exactly this). Gemini is included in paid Workspace for Education editions. No separate vendor, no new contract, no student data leaving the district tenant.

The catch, and it is a real one: the free Gemini API tier is rate-limited to roughly 2 requests per minute and 50 per day. A single 47-page course would exhaust that in one run. So a personal free key does not work. This needs district-provisioned quota.

5.3 Why that is an argument, not a blocker

A working prototype that delivers real value without AI, and that documents precisely where a model would help and what quota it would need, is a stronger artifact to bring to a district AI committee than a tool that cannot run at all without a budget decision.

Requirement: AI-dependent checks are clearly labeled as such in the interface, and are disabled by default. The tool must never silently degrade. If the model is unavailable, it says so and runs everything else.

6. OSCQR

Dial Your Course is built on OSCQR the SUNY Online Course Quality Review Rubric: 50 standards across six sections. It is the spine of the tool, not a feature of it.

6.1 Why OSCQR and not Quality Matters

OSCQR is licensed CC BY 4.0. It can legally be built into a tool, quoted, and redistributed with attribution. Quality Matters is proprietary; building a QM checker and publishing it would not be lawful. This is not a small detail, the license is what makes this tool possible at all.

OSCQR is also explicitly formative and non-evaluative designed to guide faculty rather than judge them. That matches what this tool is for.

6.2 The six sections

SectionStandardsMachine-checkable?
Overview & Information1-10Partly. Std 9 (measurable objectives) is fully checkable with a verb bank.
Technology & Tools11-15Partly. Std 14 (privacy policy links) is checkable by scanning for tool names and matching links.
Design & Layout16-28Mostly yes. Contrast, headings, fonts, tables, blinking text are all measurable.
Content & Activities29-37Partly. Alt text and link text yes. Captions no the export contains links, not video files.
Interaction38-43 · RSIMostly no. RSI is a behavior, not a file. See 6.4.
Assessment & Feedback44-50Partly. Rubric presence and point mismatches yes. Assessment quality no.

6.3 The rule the tool runs on

It will never report that a course passed something it did not actually check.

A course-quality tool that reports a fake pass is worse than no tool because it buys false confidence right before a review. Everything the tool cannot verify appears in a dedicated section of the report, with instructions for verifying it by hand.

6.4 What the tool cannot check, and says so

Roughly half of OSCQR is about what you do during the term not what the course file contains.

  • Std 38 · RSI. Regular and Substantive Interaction is a behavior. No export contains it. Verification: pull Canvas Course Statistics and Gradebook history. Did you post most weeks? Is median feedback turnaround under 72 hours? Did you initiate contact, or only respond? If you cannot evidence it, it did not happen.
  • Std 19 · well-written instructions. A machine finds missing steps; it cannot tell whether a beginner can follow them. Verification: hand your directions to one person who has never used the software and watch. Do not help. Every hesitation is a defect.
  • Std 35 · captions. The export contains links to videos, not video files. Structurally uncheckable.
  • Std 29 · engaging resources. "Engaging" is a judgment. Counting media types is not the same thing.
  • Std 40 · instructor presence. A "Meet Your Instructor" page can exist and be lifeless.
  • Std 45 · authentic assessment of mastery. Whether an assignment measures its objective is pedagogical judgment. But see section 7, one part of this IS checkable.
  • Std 49 · gradebook. Lives in Canvas settings, not the content export.
  • Std 13 · unused tools removed. Navigation settings are not fully represented in an export.
  • Std 3, 4, 5, 44 · syllabus, policies, grading. Usually in Syllabus+ or a linked doc, outside the package.

7. Authentic Assessment

7.1 The question the tool asks

Can an AI complete this assignment, correctly, in under sixty seconds?

If yes, you are no longer assessing the student.

This is deliberately not framed as a pedagogy argument. "Your assessment isn't authentic" invites a philosophical debate a busy faculty member will decline. "Your assessment no longer measures anything" is a defect report.

A multiple-choice quiz and a take-home essay are not old-fashioned instruments. They are broken ones. They have measured nothing since roughly 2023, and the profession has been politely not saying so.

7.2 Where OSCQR stops

OSCQR 31 and 45 ask whether authentic assessment is present. Neither asks whether the assessments sitting next to it still work. OSCQR predates the thing that makes this urgent. This section is the extension.

7.3 The trap: authentic ≠ AI-resistant

The artifact never protects you. A student can have AI write their portfolio case study, their rationale, their reflection. A polished final artifact proves nothing about who made it.

What resists AI is process evidence.

Resistant becauseEvidence
AI produces a finished thing, not a trailThumbnails, drafts, version history, working files
AI cannot respond to feedback it never received"What did your classmates say, and what did you change?"
AI cannot defend a decision live and unrehearsedWalkthrough video, oral defense, critique
AI has no access to a situated contextA real local client, a campus problem, a place the student went
AI cannot make a physical objectPaper sketches, a photo they took, a printed piece
Assess the process, not just the product. The product can be faked. The process cannot, at least not cheaply.

7.4 The replacement ladder

Requirement: the tool never says only "this is bad." Every flag comes with a replacement at the same cognitive level.

Broken instrumentReplace with
Multiple-choice knowledge quizApplied identification. Not "what is hierarchy," but "find hierarchy in a classmate's actual poster and name the element carrying it."
Definition matchingVocabulary in use. Require the terms inside a critique post about real work.
Take-home essayA decision memo with evidence. "Three options, I chose this, here is what changed after critique."
Generic reflection promptReflection anchored to artifacts. "Point to the change between v2 and v3 and the critique that caused it."
Summative final examPortfolio + live walkthrough.
Canned case studyA real, local, current problem. No published answer exists.

7.5 The six-point score

Three or more yeses and the assessment is probably doing its job. Zero or one, and it is decorative.

  1. Requires a personal or local context a model could not know?
  2. Requires process evidence not just a final artifact?
  3. Requires responding to feedback a specific human gave this student?
  4. Requires a live, unrehearsed defense of a decision?
  5. Produces something with an audience beyond the instructor?
  6. Is it usable after the course ends?

7.6 Validated against AVC100

Run on a real course, this framework produced a finding worth the whole section:

The only AI-vulnerable assessments in AVC100 are the two vocabulary quizzes. They are also the two that are corrupted, and nobody noticed for a full term.

The assessment nobody learned anything from is the one whose failure nobody detected.

Everything else in that course already survives: thumbnails on paper, peer critique given and received, revision from that critique, a process board, the student's own destination, a published portfolio. The course was AI-resistant and was never framed that way.

7.7 To build: the examples library

Requirement: a public, CC BY licensed repository of authentic assessment patterns by discipline. For each: the broken version, the replacement, why it resists AI, and a rubric.

Nothing like it exists as an open resource. Publishing it CC BY, the way OSCQR is licensed, turns it from a portfolio piece into a contribution.

8. Design Principles

  1. Deterministic where it matters. Compliance checks are string comparisons and arithmetic, not model output. On anything an accreditor reads, a confident wrong answer is the worst possible outcome, and an LLM might accept a paraphrase as a verbatim match. AI is reserved for genuinely fuzzy judgments, and the tool is useful without them.
  2. Fix, do not just complain. A checklist is a to-do list handed back to the person who was already overwhelmed.
  3. Propose, do not silently publish. Every generated estimate and every restyled page is reviewable. The instructor is accountable for the course, so the instructor decides.
  4. Solid colors only, never gradients. Every palette passes WCAG AA before it ships.
  5. No data leaves the machine. Because it does not need to.

9. Known Limits

  • This is not DesignPLUS and cannot be. DesignPLUS is an LTI that injects CSS into Canvas globally, which is why it can use real stylesheets and classes. A paste-based approach can never do that. Inline styling gets most of the visual result but produces verbose HTML, and there is no way to restyle a whole course later by changing one variable. That is the price of not needing a district-purchased tool.
  • No hover states, no media queries, no responsive breakpoints. Inline styles cannot express them. Output looks right on a phone because the layout is simple, not because it adapts.
  • Seat-hour profiles are department-specific. Currently the GCC Art & Humanities table. Other departments need their own profiles added. The credits × 40 rule is federal and does not change.
