## 1. Brief description of my roles and responsibilities as a faculty member

I am Residential Faculty in Digital Media Arts at Glendale Community College, where I teach graphic design, UX, and capstone career readiness. I served as Program Director for Digital Media Arts and Animation from 2011 to 2022, and since 2022 I have been faculty only, teaching 15 or more credit hours each semester. My courses are online and hybrid, and I refine them continuously using student performance data, Canvas analytics, and student feedback.

My core teaching is AVC 248 Design Self Promotion, the capstone career-launch course for Digital Media Arts, Animation, and Photography, along with AVC 100 Introduction to Digital Media and AVC 183 Digital Graphic Design 1. Beyond teaching I advise students on academic and career planning, serve as the department's eCourses lead and Canvas representative, and review online courses as a Quality Matters and OSCQR lead reviewer.

Digital Media Arts is a workforce program. Our purpose is to graduate students who are employable in a creative industry that is changing quickly. Over this evaluation cycle, the change that mattered most was the arrival of artificial intelligence in creative and professional work. Employers now expect AI skills. That reality is what drove the focus of this FEP.

## 2. Focus of the FEP and a brief statement of rationale and purpose

The focus of this FEP is learning to use AI well enough to teach it, and then building that learning into my courses, my tools, and my service to the college and district.

Here is the honest origin of this. In early 2026 I made a deliberate decision to start using AI seriously, specifically Claude, because I did not want my students left behind. Digital Media Arts is a workforce program, the workforce is asking for AI skills, and I did not feel I could teach those skills credibly without first building real fluency myself. So I committed to learning by doing: not just reading about AI, but using it daily and building real things with it.

The rationale connects to something I have believed for a long time. The creative tools my students use have always changed. What survives every shift is the underlying thinking, design principles, visual communication, how people process information. AI is the most significant of those shifts so far. My job is to prepare students for tools that keep changing, and to model that kind of learning myself rather than teach it from a distance.

This focus produced three connected bodies of work over the cycle: professional development to build genuine AI fluency, the development of courses and tools that put that fluency to use for students, and service that helps the college and district think through AI in a thoughtful, faculty-grounded way.

## 3. Summary of accomplishments and outcomes

_(Canvas guidance: write this so a faculty member outside Digital Media Arts can understand it. Explain what each tool is in plain terms. Demonstrative, not a list.)_

**Building real AI fluency through professional development.** When I started, the problem was not a lack of resources, it was too many resources scattered across too many places. I committed to a structured course of learning. Over this cycle I completed the League for Innovation AI Fellows cohort, an eight-week program for community college leaders advancing campus AI work; Google Generative AI Leader training; the University of Michigan Generative AI as a Learning Design Partner specialization on Coursera; the Anthropic Academy Teaching AI Fluency certificate; and Purdue University's Prompt Engineering microcredential. Alongside the formal coursework I have been reading widely in the field, watching technical talks such as Andrej Karpathy's introduction to large language models, and following research from EDUCAUSE, the Online Learning Consortium, Stanford HAI, and the Achieving the Dream report on the AI-enabled community college. A fuller log is in the appendix. Some of this work is still in progress; the point of this cycle was to build a durable learning practice, not to collect certificates.

**Cultivate, a tool for tending my own professional learning.** As I tried to learn AI while teaching full time, I built Cultivate, a personal learning environment for faculty professional development. It holds a multi-year PD plan, conference proposals, career strategy notes, and a curated news feed drawing from 62 sources across AI in education, EdTech, student success, UX, and product management, many of them organizations Maricopa already subscribes to that faculty rarely use. It is grounded in my 2017 graduate research on connectivism and personal learning environments, the idea that a learner should construct and tend their own learning environment rather than have one delivered to them. Cultivate is the first time I applied that research to my own practice. It is built in HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, works offline for travel, and stores no data on any server.

**Render, an AI career-launch tool for capstone students.** Render grew directly out of a problem in my AVC 248 capstone. Students spend a semester building career-readiness work, resumes, job research, networking plans, interview practice, and then lose all of it when Canvas access ends at graduation. Render is a tool students build during the capstone and keep afterward. It is organized around seven areas that map to course competencies: profile and goals, job and client log, resume vault, skills and professional development, networking, interview practice, and a launch plan. AI assistance is built into each area as structured coaching tied to the student's own goals, rather than a blank prompt with no guidance. It collects no personally identifiable information and exports as a standalone file the student owns. A classroom pilot is planned for Fall 2026. I am presenting Render at the Mesa Community College AI Summit in May 2026.

**CopaMigo, a bilingual student-support routing prototype.** CopaMigo addresses a different gap: students often stop asking for help not because resources do not exist, but because they do not know what to call what they need. A student describes their situation in plain English or Spanish, and the tool routes them to the right campus service with a handoff card showing contact information, hours, and what to ask for. It covers 14 service areas and more than 100 hand-verified GCC service links, and is designed so it could be adapted for other Maricopa colleges.

**AVC 100 course redesign.** I completed a redesign of AVC 100 Introduction to Digital Media, the department's highest-enrollment course and one with a documented low success rate. The redesign is built on retention research, and every module connects students directly to a campus support service, Advising, GCC Cares, Tutoring, Disability Resources, so support is part of the course rather than separate from it. It was built for Canvas import and launches in May 2026.

**AVC 248 capstone development.** The capstone is where this cycle's work reaches students most directly. Developing Render gave the course a structured, AI-supported tool in place of what I think of as the blank-prompt problem: handing students an open AI chat box with no scaffolding and hoping for the best.

**Service: a faculty proposal that became district and college work.** Early in this cycle I authored a proposal for a campus-wide Faculty AI Advisory Committee at GCC, with supporting research, and submitted it through college governance to the VP of Academic Affairs. The proposal was not adopted as a standalone committee. The thinking behind it, that faculty need a structured, grounded way to navigate AI, has since found two other homes. I am now co-chair of the Student Support and Success domain on the Maricopa Community Colleges District AI Resource Committee, helping lead a district AI strategy across all 10 colleges. And I am launching a Faculty AI Community of Practice at GCC in Fall 2026, developed in partnership with the Center for Teaching, Learning and Engagement, with a kickoff during Accountability Week. Both are described below.

I also plan to keep developing CopaMigo toward something other Maricopa colleges could use.
