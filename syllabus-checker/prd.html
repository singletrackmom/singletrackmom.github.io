1. Summary

Every term, each submitted syllabus is read by hand against the MCCCD Regulation 3.6 required elements, the program’s own additions, and the approved seat-hour and Regular and Substantive Interaction (RSI) language, which has to appear verbatim rather than paraphrased. That review is slow, its consistency depends on who is doing it, and the feedback loop is long enough that an instructor can learn in week six that a required statement was missing in week one. The department, meanwhile, has no single view of who has complied.

The Syllabus Checker attaches to the department’s existing Google Form. On submit, it reads the approved-language document live, extracts the syllabus text, compares the two, and emails the instructor an itemized report with the correct approved wording included so there is nothing to go find. The same result is written back into the form’s response spreadsheet as status columns, so compliance becomes one sortable log rather than an inbox.

The tool is read-only. It reports, it never revises. No instructor should discover that an automated process quietly rewrote a document they are accountable for.

2. Goal

Move syllabus compliance from a manual, end-of-review bottleneck to an instant, consistent check that runs at the moment of submission, so that the required language is correct before the term starts rather than after a problem surfaces. Two measurable outcomes: the instructor learns what is missing in seconds instead of weeks, and the department sees the status of every section in one place without opening a single attachment.

3. Users and context

UserNeedWhat they receive
Faculty memberTo know what is missing and to fix it without hunting for the approved wording.An email within seconds naming each gap, with the correct approved language supplied inline, ready to paste.
Department chair or administratorTo monitor compliance across every section of the term.Status columns written back into the response spreadsheet they already own. One sortable log, no forty-report inbox.

The split between those two outputs is the central design decision. Faculty need to act, so they get a message they can act on. The administrator needs to see the whole term, so she gets a log. Sending the administrator a copy of every individual report is not monitoring, it is drowning, and it is the mistake most compliance tools make.

Context: the department already collects syllabi through a Google Form on a college Workspace account. Faculty habit does not change. Seat-hour profiles are currently written for GCC Art and Humanities.

4. How it works

  1. Faculty submit through the existing Google Form.
  2. An onFormSubmit trigger fires inside the department’s Workspace.
  3. The script reads the department’s approved-language document live and extracts the submitted syllabus text.
  4. It normalizes both texts, compares them, and builds the findings.
  5. It emails the instructor the report and writes status columns back to the response sheet.

The approved language is read live, never copied

The script reads the reference document on every run and stores no copy. A stored copy going stale is how compliance checklists fail: the department revises the wording, nobody updates the checker, and the tool starts approving syllabi against language that is no longer current. Reading live means the check is correct the same minute the source document changes, and there is nothing to maintain.

What is checked

CheckMethodUses AI

MCCCD Reg 3.6 elements (Title IX, accommodations, response time, and similar)Keyword match for the literal ones, a model for the fuzzy ones.Partly

Deterministic where it matters

Whether an approved statement appears verbatim is a string comparison, not a judgment call. A language model would be worse at it, because it might decide a paraphrase counts as a match, and on text an accreditor reads a confident wrong answer is the worst possible outcome. AI is reserved for the ambiguous Reg 3.6 elements and is clearly labeled as a suggestion. The tool delivers most of its value with the model turned off.

Normalization

A verbatim check that fails on a curly apostrophe is useless. Before comparing, both texts are normalized: curly quotes to straight, dashes to hyphens, non-breaking spaces to spaces, whitespace collapsed, case lowered. Formatting noise must never produce a false failure.

Reading the syllabus, and the honest limit

FormatReliableHow
Google DocYes, nativeRead directly through DocumentApp.
Word (.docx)YesConverted through Drive, read, temporary copy discarded.
PDFNoConverted through Drive, but text extraction is imperfect.
Syllabus+ PDF URLNot yet supportedRequires a fetch-and-parse step. See section 11.

A verbatim check against imperfectly extracted PDF text produces false failures, and a checker that fails people incorrectly will be ignored, correctly. So when the submission is a PDF, the report says plainly that extraction may be unreliable and tells the instructor to resubmit as a document if the report flags language they know is present. The tool never fails anyone silently. The durable fix is policy rather than code: ask the department to accept Google Docs and Word.

The reports

  • To the instructor: an email, complete or incomplete, with each finding named and the approved wording supplied where relevant.
  • To the department: four columns written into the existing response sheet, check status, missing items, file type, and date checked, with the status cell colored by state (solid colors only).
  • Optional weekly digest of what is still outstanding.

5. Data, privacy, and governance

A syllabus is instructor content, not student content. It is a course document written by the faculty member and distributed to a class. It contains no grades, no enrollment records, no submissions, and no student identifiers, so no FERPA-protected record is created, transmitted, or stored at any point in this workflow.

  • The system never touches a student record and has no path to one.
  • There is no database, no logging, and no analytics. The only things written anywhere are a status value in a spreadsheet the department already owns and an email to the person who submitted.
  • Everything runs inside the district’s own Google tenant. Nothing is sent to an external service in the default configuration, which uses no AI.
  • If the optional fuzzy Reg 3.6 checks are enabled, the only text sent to a model is syllabus prose written by the instructor. Instructor name and email stay in the Workspace layer and are never included in a model call. If the district prefers that no content leave the tenant at all, the fuzzy checks can be turned off and the deterministic checks still run.
  • The script is read-only, with no write access to any submitted syllabus.
  • The script is owned by the department account rather than a personal one, so it survives staff turnover.

Why Apps Script

The department already runs the form in Google Workspace on a college account. Apps Script runs inside that Workspace as an authorized user, with access to Docs, Drive, Sheets, and Gmail already granted. There is no OAuth consent screen to publish, no client secret to protect, no server to host, no CORS proxy, and no vendor. The department owns the script rather than any one person.

Model swappability

The AI call is a single function behind one interface: text in, findings out. Nothing else in the system depends on which model answers. It can point at Claude, at Gemini (which is already inside the Workspace tenant and is the lowest-friction option for a district that wants no traffic leaving Google), or at a district-hosted endpoint. Swapping providers means changing one function, not rearchitecting the tool. And because the deterministic checks carry most of the value, the system runs correctly with no model at all.

Running on district infrastructure

It already does. Apps Script executes on Google infrastructure under the district’s own Workspace tenant, using accounts, permissions, and audit logging that OIT already administers. There is nothing to procure, nothing to host, and no new vendor relationship. Installation is an administrative authorization on the department account, not a deployment.

What it takes to serve more than one college

  • Seat-hour profiles are department-specific and currently written for GCC Art and Humanities, so each department or college needs its own. That is configuration rather than code: move the profiles into the approved-language document and a department maintains its own.
  • Form field titles are matched by exact string, so a reworded question makes the script read nothing. Matching by field ID instead is a small change, and it also removes the current fragility at GCC.
  • Each college has its own form, sheet, and reference document, so the script is copied and pointed at that college’s three document IDs, with the district-wide Reg 3.6 checks shared.
  • The MCCCD Reg 3.6 elements are district-wide and identical everywhere. They are the portable core and need no work at all.

The honest read: the district-required checks port with no work, and the local checks are configuration. There is no multi-tenant version and none is planned before the pilot, because ten copies of a small script that each department owns is a better fit for how these forms are actually administered than one central system nobody owns.

7. Pilot and testing

The purpose of the pilot is to take the repetitive part of compliance review off the people doing it. Chairs and faculty currently spend hours confirming that a paragraph of approved language is present, word for word, in dozens of documents. That is machine work. Removing it does not remove anyone: it returns that time to the judgment calls, the course-content conversations, and the teaching that only a person can do. No role is reduced, and the human still decides on every finding.

ElementPlan
Who tests itFaculty in GCC Art and Humanities who already submit through the form, plus the department chair and the administrator who currently performs the manual review.
ScopeOne department, one term, every syllabus submitted through the existing form.
MethodShadow mode for the full term. The checker runs and logs results but sends no faculty email. The manual review continues in parallel and remains the system of record.
What is measuredAgreement between the checker and the human reviewer on every syllabus. False failures (the checker flags language that is present) and misses (the checker passes a syllabus that is missing something) are counted separately, since a false failure destroys trust fastest. Also: reviewer hours before and after, file-type distribution, and the PDF extraction failure rate.
Faculty feedbackAfter shadow mode, a short round with a small group of faculty on whether the report is clear and immediately actionable.

8. Definition of success

These thresholds decide whether the prototype becomes version 1.0, meaning it has passed the pilot and is cleared for rollout.

MeasureThreshold to clear
False failures on the deterministic checksZero across the shadow term on Google Doc and Word submissions. The verbatim checks are string comparisons, so anything above zero is a bug, not a tolerance.
MissesZero. A syllabus the checker passed that the human reviewer failed is a defect that must be fixed before rollout.
Agreement with the human reviewerFull agreement on every deterministic check. Disagreement on the AI-assisted fuzzy elements is expected, which is why they are labeled as suggestions and never as failures.
Reviewer timeA clear reduction in the hours the administrator spends on manual verbatim checking, reported by the person doing it.
Faculty responseFaculty can act on the report without asking anyone what it means, and no one reports being flagged for language they know is present.
Unreadable filesEvery one is reported as unreadable. The tool never guesses.

Version 1.0 means the pilot passed and the tool is cleared for rollout. Nothing is in production today.

9. Rollout

  1. Turn on faculty email in the pilot department for the following term, with the manual review kept as a backstop for one more term.
  2. Retire the parallel manual verbatim check once the department chair is satisfied. Human judgment stays on the fuzzy elements and on every decision.
  3. Fix the fragility before it travels: match form fields by ID, add the modality question, and move the seat-hour profiles into the approved-language document so a department maintains its own without touching code.
  4. Offer it to a second department at GCC, which needs its own Google Form, its own approved-language document, and a department account to own the script. Setup is pointing the script at three document IDs.
  5. Offer it to another college on the same three requirements. The district-wide Reg 3.6 checks come along unchanged, and the college supplies its local language. This is the honest test of portability, and it should be taken deliberately, one college at a time, rather than announced district-wide.
  6. Before any district-wide offer, ask OIT and the ARC to review the AI configuration (on, off, or pointed at a district-hosted model) and to put the data boundary described in section 5 in writing.
