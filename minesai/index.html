The intake pipeline, what actually existsShe has read “I designed and built the intake” in your cover letter. Know this cold.

AI Opportunity Pipeline, the prototype

Built 10 September 2026 · one web page, runs in the browser · open it before the call

  • It is real and it works. Six screens: Dashboard, What exists, Requests, Decision log, Submit, Method.
  • The Submit form asks a department what it already has, how it is licensed, whether it is helping, where the repetitive work is, what would help, and to walk through the current process. That last question is how it tells an AI problem from a process problem.
  • Two tracks. Track one is an inventory of what already exists, because the cheapest fix is usually something another college already runs. Track two is requests.
  • Six assessment dimensions: student impact, staff time returned, readiness, risk, feasibility, scale.
  • Four dispositions for a request: pilot, discovery, not an AI problem, declined. Declines show as prominently as pilots.
  • A decision log: every entry leaves with an answer and a written reason.

What it is not

  • It has taken zero real requests. It runs on sample data. Three records are from real reporting. The page says so itself.
  • It is a prototype, not a system. Submissions save in the browser only. There is no database and no one else is using it.
  • It has not been tested with users. An AI director in district IT reviewed the model on 18 September and found real flaws. That review is the content of the opening move tab.

What to say

“I built a working prototype: an intake form, an inventory of what colleges already have, six assessment dimensions, four dispositions and a decision log. It runs on sample data and has not taken a real request. I had a district IT AI director review it, and the review changed my thinking. Privacy, security and accessibility should be pass or fail gates, not scores. And four dispositions is too few. I would add route to something we already own, and a real defer with a date.”

That answer is true, it matches your cover letter, and it shows you can take criticism of your own design. It also sets up the best question you have: “What does Mines use today to take in AI requests?”

One more thing she may raise

  • The prototype’s own Method screen says this belongs in a service management platform like ServiceNow or TeamDynamix, and that the prototype shows the operating model, not the tooling. Mines already takes project requests through its IT Help Center. So the right answer to “would you build this here?” is: “No. I would configure it in what Mines already runs. The model is the part I bring.”

If the synthetic student study comes upChristy knows instructional design and will know the critique

What to say, about 25 seconds

“The research on this cuts both ways, so I designed around the weak spot. Agents are decent at finding where a task breaks and unreliable at judging severity, so they never decide severity. Human raters do. And about ten real testers, one per campus, run the same tasks to check the agents before I scale anything.”

What Nielsen Norman Group says

  • “Synthetic users are fake users generated by AI. While there may be a few use cases for them, user research needs real users.” Rosala and Moran, June 2024.
  • Their three conclusions: real user research is essential, supplement and do not substitute, use synthetic research for specific purposes.
  • What they found wrong: AI participants were too agreeable, idealized their own behavior (“Yes, I completed all the courses I mentioned”), cared about everything equally, and “cannot produce behavioral data.”
  • What they say it is good for: desk research, preparing for a real study, and generating hypotheses. “Treat them as hypotheses that need testing.”

Why your study is a different thing

  • Their critique is about asking an AI persona for its opinions in an interview or survey. Your agents do not give opinions. They attempt real tasks on each college’s own site and log where the path breaks. A broken link is broken no matter who clicks it.
  • Do not oversell it. The agents cannot say how a real student feels, what they would give up on, or which barrier matters most. That is why humans rate severity and real testers validate.
  • You already hold their rule: every finding is a hypothesis, you verify by opening the actual page, and you retracted four early findings that did not hold up.
  • The study is in progress. Say early findings, not results.

If she asks why not just test with real students

“I will, for the barriers that matter most. The agents let me cover ten colleges’ systems first, so I spend real students’ time on the problems worth their time.”

Engineering a world of possibilities

Join Mines in a new AI Solutions Manager position!

The AI Solutions Manager is a member of the AI Acceleration team within the Information Technology department at Mines and serves as the primary customer-facing intake, engagement, adoption, and value-realization lead for agentic AI opportunities. This role manages the AI solution lifecycle from idea intake through use-case development, prioritization, implementation readiness, adoption planning, change management, feedback collection, and outcome measurement.

Working closely with IT teams, and campus partners, the AI Solutions Manager helps departments identify meaningful opportunities to use agentic AI to improve academic, administrative, research, and operational outcomes. This role translates campus needs into clear AI use cases, assesses value and readiness, coordinates stakeholder alignment, and helps ensure that AI work is prioritized, adopted, and measured in a responsible and sustainable way.

This is a high-visibility individual contributor role that requires strong relationship-building, analytical judgment, change management experience, business acumen, and the ability to communicate clearly with technical and non-technical audiences. The AI Solutions Manager does not manage people; the role manages the AI opportunity pipeline, campus engagement process, adoption lifecycle, and value-realization activities for AI-enabled solutions.

This position currently operates in a hybrid work model with regular campus presence required. Work arrangements are subject to change based on institutional needs. The successful candidate must be able to commute to campus in Golden, Colorado.

Visa sponsorship is not available for this position now nor in the future.

Primary Responsibilities

AI opportunity intake, pipeline management, and prioritization support

  • Serve as the primary front door for campus AI opportunities, ideas, use cases, and requests from academic, administrative, research, and operational units.
  • Build and maintain an organized AI opportunity pipeline that captures request details, business needs, expected outcomes, stakeholders, readiness factors, dependencies, risks, and status.
  • Meet with campus partners to understand pain points, opportunities, goals, current processes, service impacts, customer needs, and potential value of AI-enabled solutions.
  • Shape early ideas into clear, actionable use cases that can be evaluated by the AI Acceleration team and institutional decision-makers.
  • Assess incoming opportunities for institutional alignment, business value, feasibility, urgency, complexity, risk, readiness, data needs, governance considerations, accessibility, privacy, security, and sustainability.
  • Develop prioritization recommendations for review by the Director of AI Acceleration and appropriate governance or leadership groups.
  • Identify requests that should move forward as AI projects, requests that need additional discovery, requests that are better addressed through training or process improvement, and requests that should be deferred or declined.
  • Partner with the Senior Project Manager to transition approved AI opportunities into structured project planning and delivery processes.
  • Track the AI opportunity pipeline and prepare summaries, dashboards, presentations, or decision-support materials for leadership, governance groups, and stakeholders.
  • Evaluate potential solution approaches, including AI-enabled solutions, enterprise applications, workflow automation, process improvements, vendor products, and existing technology capabilities, to help identify practical and sustainable paths forward.

Campus engagement, partnership, and AI consulting

  • Establish and maintain trusted relationships with campus departments, faculty, staff, administrative leaders, researchers, IT teams, and other stakeholders interested in AI-enabled solutions.
  • Serve as a consultative partner to departments by helping them understand how AI may support business process improvement, service delivery, operational efficiency, productivity, decision support, and customer experience.
  • Facilitate discovery conversations, stakeholder workshops, interviews, listening sessions, demonstrations, and other engagement activities to understand needs and align expectations.
  • Translate campus needs and user perspectives into clear narratives, business cases, use-case summaries, stakeholder impacts, adoption considerations, and success measures.
  • Help departments understand responsible AI expectations, approved tools, data handling considerations, governance requirements, and appropriate use boundaries.
  • Partner with AI Solutions Architects to ensure campus needs are clearly understood before technical design, prototyping, or implementation begins.
  • Support communication between technical teams and campus partners so that AI concepts, solution options, tradeoffs, constraints, and decisions are understandable to non-technical audiences.
  • Identify opportunities to expand AI fluency and responsible AI adoption across campus through targeted outreach, documentation, office hours, presentations, and engagement activities.
  • Connect related requests, common pain points, and recurring needs across departments to identify scalable AI solutions and reduce one-off delivery patterns.
  • Advise stakeholders on available technology options and approaches by evaluating tradeoffs, capabilities, limitations, implementation considerations, support requirements, and organizational impacts.

Adoption, organizational change management, and readiness

  • Lead adoption planning for AI-enabled solutions, including stakeholder readiness, communication needs, training needs, workflow impacts, support model considerations, and resistance management.
  • Develop change management approaches that help faculty, staff, and departments understand, accept, and effectively use AI solutions in their work.
  • Partner with project teams, communications staff, training resources, service owners, and campus departments to coordinate implementation readiness and adoption support.
  • Create or coordinate communication plans, stakeholder impact summaries, adoption plans, training materials, knowledge articles, FAQs, user guidance, and other enablement resources.
  • Assess department readiness for AI-enabled change, including process maturity, stakeholder support, data availability, operational capacity, governance needs, and likely adoption barriers.
  • Monitor adoption after implementation and identify whether additional communication, training, documentation, process adjustment, or technical refinement is needed.
  • Gather user feedback and work with AI Solutions Architects and project teams to support solution iteration, improvement, and sustained use.
  • Promote practical, ethical, secure, accessible, and responsible AI adoption consistent with Mines policy, data governance expectations, and institutional priorities.
  • Help build a culture of AI fluency by reinforcing effective use, responsible experimentation, practical examples, and shared learning across campus.

Value realization, metrics, and continuous improvement

  • Define expected outcomes and success measures for AI opportunities in partnership with campus stakeholders, the Director of AI Acceleration, AI Solutions Architects, and project teams.
  • Develop and track value-realization measures such as adoption, user satisfaction, effort reduction, process improvement, service quality, cycle-time reduction, accuracy, risk reduction, cost avoidance, or other relevant outcomes.
  • Collect feedback from users and stakeholders after implementation to evaluate whether AI solutions achieved the intended business or operational value.
  • Prepare summaries, reports, dashboards, presentations, and recommendations that communicate AI outcomes, lessons learned, realized value, adoption challenges, and future opportunities.
  • Identify patterns across AI requests and delivered solutions to recommend improvements to intake processes, prioritization criteria, governance workflows, adoption practices, and service delivery.
  • Partner with the Senior Project Manager to support post-implementation reviews, lessons learned, project closure, and transition to operational support.
  • Maintain documentation of use cases, decisions, outcomes, lessons learned, success stories, and reusable practices to support future AI work.
  • Recommend improvements to the AI opportunity intake model, prioritization methods, change management practices, campus engagement approach, and value tracking framework.

AI governance, responsible use, and cross-functional alignment

  • Support responsible AI implementation by helping ensure proposed use cases consider privacy, security, accessibility, data governance, compliance, model transparency, user impact, and institutional risk.
  • Identify AI use cases that may require review by governance, compliance, security, legal, accessibility, data governance, or other institutional stakeholders.
  • Partner with the Director of AI Acceleration, AI Solutions Architects, Information Security, Compliance, Procurement, Academic Affairs, and other relevant teams to support responsible and aligned AI decision-making.
  • Help translate governance expectations into practical guidance for departments and project teams.
  • Ensure AI intake, prioritization, adoption, and value-realization activities align with institutional goals, IT standards, and Mines’ responsible AI expectations.

The move to build the interview around

Mines published thirteen assessment criteria in their own posting. Her intake scores six. Do not expand to thirteen. Thirteen scored dimensions is an unusable instrument and saying so is the point.

Bring the mapping: here are your thirteen, here is how they compress into six scored dimensions and four pass/fail gates, and here is why privacy, security and accessibility are gates rather than scores at a public institution.

Their thirteen

Institutional alignment · business value · feasibility · urgency · complexity · risk · readiness · data needs · governance · accessibility · privacy · security · sustainability

Why it compresses

  • Privacy, security and accessibility are gates, not scores. Pass or fail, checked before anything gets scored. A tool that fails privacy does not get a medium score, it does not proceed. At a public institution with the ADA Title II WCAG 2.1 AA deadline in April 2027, accessibility cannot be a number that averages away.
  • Scale is a multiplier, not a peer dimension. Scored as a peer, a district-wide low-value item outranks a high-value single-college one. Value should be impact times reach, one number.
  • Risk should score its worst axis, not its average. NIST AI RMF names seven trustworthiness characteristics. A tool that is fine on six and catastrophic on one currently scores medium, which is exactly backwards.
  • Complexity and feasibility are the same question asked from two directions, and both belong to the architect rather than to intake.
  • Urgency and institutional alignment are the two her own model is missing and theirs names. Worth conceding on the spot.
  • There is no effort denominator, which means a model can rank but cannot sequence. T-shirt sizing, S=1 M=2 L=4, and priority becomes weighted score divided by effort.

The dispositions

The JD names four: move forward, needs discovery, is really a training or process problem, defer or decline. Hers names the same four. Four is too few, and saying which ones are missing is a strong move:

  • Route to existing capability. The cheapest disposition there is, and the one most often skipped.
  • Refer to another owner. A request that belongs to another office should exit with a named owner, not a decline.
  • Defer is not decline. Deferred needs a named blocker and a revisit date or it disappears.
  • Pilot is not a terminus. Add scale, sustain and sunset, with a gate at the end of every pilot.

Two things to propose regardless

  • Require a value measure and a baseline at intake, not after. If the requester cannot name one measure and a current baseline, it is not a pilot, it goes to discovery. This is the answer to the value-realization gap and it costs nothing to implement.
  • Calibrate on eight to ten real past requests. Score them retrospectively and compare to the decision actually made. Every disagreement is either a model bug or a bad decision, and both are worth finding. This is also a credible first-ninety-days answer.

Say the limit out loud. The intake has not taken its first request. Leading with the mapping and then saying so is much stronger than being caught by it. The design is the evidence; the volume is not claimed.
