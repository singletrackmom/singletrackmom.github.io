1. Summary

CopaMigo is a student-facing AI triage tool. A student types or speaks a problem in their own words, in the language they think in. CopaMigo reads it for urgency and need, answers directly when the responsible department has already supplied the answer, and hands off to a person with a card carrying the office name, contact, hours, and the specific next step. It requires no login and collects no student data.

Unlike a website chat widget that scans pages and returns links, the answers are written by the advisors, financial aid staff, librarians, disability resource staff, veterans coordinator, social workers, and tutoring staff who already field these questions. The bot is the delivery layer.

One engine, two front doors. The standalone page is a full-screen destination a college can link from a homepage, a Canvas tab, or a QR code. The embedded widget is the same tool dropped into an existing page. Same prompt, same cards, same anonymous architecture, one deployment to maintain.

2. Goal

Get a stuck student to the right person quickly, without requiring them to name the office or know the vocabulary.

The second goal shapes every architectural decision in this document: prove that a college can deploy a useful student-facing AI tool without collecting student data.

CopaMigo takes routine, repetitive questions off advisors and financial aid staff: hours, locations, deadlines, process steps, what to bring, the “we will get back to you in a few days” scheduling email. Nobody is replaced. Staff write the answers, own them, and receive every student the tool routes. Every path CopaMigo opens ends at a human.

4. How it works

Every incoming message sorts into one of three routing tiers. The tier is decided by the nature of the question, not the topic.

TierBehaviorExamples
1, bot answers directlyThe department has already supplied a current, correct answer. The bot delivers it in plain language.Tutoring hours. Food pantry location. Last day to withdraw. How to request a transcript.
2, bot answers, then opens a pathA useful general answer exists, but the student’s specifics need a person. The bot answers, then hands over phone, scheduling link, and walk-in location.What happens to aid if I drop a class. How do I request accommodations. Where do I start on transferring.
3, direct to humanNo answer attempted. Empathy and the fastest possible warm handoff.Self-harm or safety risk (immediate escalation to counseling and 988). Title IX. Imminent eviction or hunger.

Routing behavior

  • Empathy first, label second. The first response acknowledges what the student said in human language. The office is named only after the student has been received.
  • One situation can route to several offices at once. “I am failing and my aid is on hold” reaches advising and financial aid, each with context.
  • Each routed service surfaces as a warm handoff card: office, what it does in plain language, contact, hours, map link, next step. A chat fallback to that office is the planned safety net for students who cannot act on the card alone.
  • Voice in, voice out, using the browser-native Web Speech API. No translation service, no per-message cost.
  • The bot names no courses. Training data on course codes is unreliable across semesters, and a hallucinated course name sends a student to register for the wrong class. The tool links the verified published program checksheet (a JavaScript lookup table of program to URL, no AI-constructed links) and routes to an advisor. First-semester students always go to an advisor before registering.
  • Crisis and Title IX routing is hard-coded and runs ahead of everything else, including when the topic is raised indirectly.

The prototype covers 23 service categories, from advising and financial aid to disability resources, counseling, tutoring, library, basic needs, career services, campus police, Title IX, crisis, IT help, veterans, records, testing, international students, and AI policy. Each card carries verified contact information.

5. Data, privacy, and governance

CopaMigo collects no student data and no personally identifiable information. No login, no MEID, no name, no email, no student record.

The only data leaving the browser is the student’s question and the response. No identity is attached because none is collected, so there is nothing to mask or redact. The district declined a vendor AI add-on to its existing CRM because it would have put student data in reach of a third-party model. That decision sets the bar every scenario in section 6 has to clear: no student conversation data may be used to train an underlying language model, under any hosting arrangement.

ItemPosition
Student identityNot collected. No login is added for the pilot. Anonymity is a launch requirement, not a preference.
Conversation dataSent to the model for a response, not retained for training under any hosting scenario in section 6.
LogsAnonymous category and routing counts only, in institutional infrastructure, no identifying fields.
Handoff to a personThe one place a student may choose to share contact information, with the receiving staff member only.
FERPAReviewed with district legal counsel before pilot. The review rests on the anonymous-first design.
AccessibilityProduction release targets WCAG 2.1 Level AA under the ADA Title II web rule, audited before launch.
Data processing agreementSigned institutionally with the chosen provider, directly or through the cloud provider, before any student traffic.

6. Build and portability

6.1 Current stack

  • Single HTML file, vanilla JavaScript, no framework, no backend, hosted on GitHub Pages, mobile-first.
  • Voice input and speech output via the browser-native Web Speech API. Transcription happens in the browser; no audio is sent anywhere.
  • Routing logic, tier behavior, service cards, and every department-written answer live in one system prompt.
  • Program checksheet lookup is a plain JavaScript table. Staff intake forms are ordinary web forms with no AI in them.
  • The AI layer is the Claude API, called from the browser with a tester-supplied key.

The browser-held credential is the one thing that must change before a student uses the tool: a credential in the browser is a credential anyone can read. A placeholder chat modal also stands in for the not-yet-wired live chat and comes out before launch. The exclusion list behind it stays, so crisis, Title IX, instructor, and campus police cards never offer a chat link.

6.2 Model portability

CopaMigo is built on a system prompt, a set of service cards, and department-written content, all provider-agnostic text. Swapping models changes the endpoint and the request and response shape, roughly a dozen lines of code, then requires re-running the test set to re-tune wording. That is tuning, not a rebuild. Interface, routing, multilingual behavior, verified links, checksheet lookup, and intake forms are untouched.

PathShapeAssessment
Claude via AWS BedrockServer-side app in a district AWS account, credentials on the application role, no API key, anonymous logs in the district’s account.Recommended pilot path. Uses an existing cloud contract, and the prompt is already tuned to this model family.
Claude directSame app, calling the provider API under an institutional agreement.Cleanest data chain, no intermediary. Requires a new vendor relationship.
Gemini via Vertex AISame app, same content, calling a district-provisioned Google model.Viable if the district runs Google Cloud. Deciding question: the Gemini assistant inside Workspace is a chat product, not Vertex AI API access, which is what a custom tool calls. Which one the district holds decides quick swap versus new procurement.
District-hosted or other providerAny model the district provisions and can call from its own infrastructure.Supported. Content and routing carry over. Re-tuning and re-testing the response set is the real cost.

Billing on every path is one institutional account, usage-billed, serving every student through one tool. Not a per-student subscription.

6.3 Running it on district infrastructure

StageWhat it isReady for
Controlled prototypeCurrent state. Browser-held key.Demonstrations and department review only.
Lightweight hosted serviceA thin server between student and model holds the credential. One serverless function on a free or low-cost tier.The smallest step that can pilot with students. Design, content, and experience do not change.
District-hosted productionSame application in a district cloud account, secrets manager, monitoring, accessibility audit, governance sign-offs.Rollout beyond the pilot.

Production shape in a district cloud account: a static front end served from object storage behind a content delivery network, plus one stateless function behind an API gateway that adds the system prompt, calls the model, and returns the response. The browser never holds a credential, never calls the model directly, and never sees the system prompt. Session continuity stays in browser local storage, so the application needs no database. Traffic is bursty, which suits serverless.

  • Credentials live in a secrets manager, never in code, a deployment artifact, or a baked-in environment variable. On the Bedrock path there is no key to store at all.
  • Model versions are pinned explicitly. Model access is enabled per region.
  • Rate limiting runs at two levels: a per-address ceiling with a slow-down message, and a global daily token budget that alerts an owner and degrades to a static message carrying the most-used office contacts.
  • Prompt injection is handled by explicit instructions in the system prompt. The application executes nothing that comes from user input.
  • Logged: timestamp, cards shown, token count, latency, detected language, success or error, and a rotating hash of the address used only for rate limiting. Not logged: question text, response text, raw address, conversation history, or any free-text field.
  • The system prompt is treated as software. It lives in source control, changes arrive as reviewed commits, and every change runs against a fixed test set (stigma-aware routing, crisis escalation, multilingual response, instructor-first routing) in staging. If latency or error rates move after a deploy, the previous version is restored.
  • Department content arrives through the staff intake form, verified by opening every link and calling every number. Departments keep editorial control of their own answers.

Remaining work to run on district infrastructure.

  1. Move the AI call server-side and hold credentials in a secrets manager. The only architectural change of consequence.
  2. Point the application at whichever model the district provisions, then re-run the response test set and tune wording.
  3. Stand up rate limiting and anonymous logging in the district’s cloud account, with dashboards for traffic, errors, routing quality, and cost, and an owner on call.
  4. Split service data into shared district services (Title IX coordinator, district help desk, crisis lines) and college-specific services keyed by college code, so additional colleges onboard by supplying data rather than forking the tool.
  5. Complete the WCAG 2.1 Level AA audit and resolve blocking issues.
  6. Sign the data processing agreement and complete the FERPA and CIO reviews.
  7. Select the chat fallback platform, embed it per office, and train receiving staff. The library already runs a chat product, so that is the lowest-friction starting point.

6.4 Embedding it in an existing page

A college does not have to adopt a new platform or send students to a new address. The widget drops into an existing student services page, a portal, or (Phase 2) a Canvas LTI tab, and behaves identically to the standalone page. Both modes call the same server and the same prompt.

7. Pilot and testing

7.1 Usability testing

Usability testing runs before the pilot. It tests whether a student understands the tool within thirty seconds, whether the empathy-first response reads as human, whether routing lands on the right office, whether the card carries enough to act on, whether it works on a phone, whether voice handles the way students actually speak, and whether it feels safe for a sensitive topic. If a participant gets stuck, the tool failed, and the moderator watches rather than rescues.

Eight to ten GCC students, recruited for deliberate variety.

  • Year: at least two first-semester, two continuing, and one returning student.
  • Language: at least two Spanish-dominant or bilingual students. Other languages common across Maricopa are a bonus.
  • Program: a mix of academic and career and technical programs.
  • Life circumstance: at least one veteran or military-connected student, one student parent, and one student who has used counseling or basic needs.
  • Assistive technology, required: at least one student who uses a screen reader, voice control, or switch input day to day, or who is blind or has low vision. Disability resources can help recruit.
  • Neurodivergent, required: at least one autistic student, who will surface idioms landing wrong and interaction surprises such as an unannounced modal. ADHD students are valuable for the same reason.
  • Technology comfort: at least one student who is not confident with technology, and one who is fluent and already skeptical of AI.

Testing with disabled students does not replace the WCAG audit, and the audit does not replace testing with disabled students. Automated tools find structural compliance issues; real users find the distance between technically accessible and actually usable. Both run before the pilot.

7.2 Session shape

About an hour: a scripted introduction (you cannot break this, if something does not work that is the finding, think out loud), then scenarios, then a debrief. Scenarios are handed over as a situation with no office named. The exact words a student types are data and get captured verbatim.

Six core scenarios run in every session: first-semester course questions (no courses named, advisor required), struggling in a class (instructor first, then tutoring), mental health described without clinical language, a question asked in Spanish (answered fully in Spanish), suspected ADHD with no diagnosis (disability resources and basic needs, without leading with the word disability), and basic needs pressure on a student parent. Each session adds one rotating spotlight set: financial aid, technology and printing and file storage, AI policy, or disability and veterans services.

Recorded per scenario: what the student typed, whether they understood what to do, which cards appeared and whether routing was right, whether the empathy message landed, whether the contact information told them who to call and what to say. Technical checks every session: links open the right page, phone numbers tap to call, map links open the right building, hours are current, voice transcribes and speaks back in the language of the input, and the page renders on a phone with nothing cut off.

7.3 Accessibility and sensitive-topic checks

  • Every interactive element announces itself with a meaningful label, not “button” or “link.”
  • Focus order follows visual order, the focus indicator is visible, and every card, chat link, and modal is keyboard reachable. A modal traps focus and closes on Escape.
  • Voice input still works with a screen reader running, since some screen readers conflict with the Web Speech API.
  • Color is never the only signal. Text resizes to 200 percent without breaking layout.
  • On sensitive scenarios the moderator watches for distress, keeps the counseling number and the 988 line at hand, does not probe when a student volunteers something personal, and closes the session lightly. Wellbeing comes before the data.

9. Rollout

Rollout begins only after the pilot clears section 8.

  1. Evaluation gate. A formal decision: continue, expand, revise, or sunset. Governance moves here from faculty-led ownership to a designated institutional unit with a budget line and a service level agreement.
  2. Campus selector at GCC. Main and North differ in hours, locations, and on-site services. The selector surfaces only when a question is location-sensitive, so it never gates a student in distress.
  3. Multi-college picker. A one-time prompt on first launch asks which Maricopa college the student calls home, saved to local storage with a switcher in the header. All ten colleges appear, with those not yet onboarded marked as coming soon.
  4. Embedded rollout. Colleges that do not want a new destination drop the widget into an existing page.
  5. Canvas LTI tab in every course, labelled “Find Help.” Students spend their time in Canvas, so this is where discoverability is won. LTI passes identity; CopaMigo ignores it.
  6. Staffed per-office chat fallback. Each card’s chat link connects to the person who already answers that office’s phone. Does not ship until the staffing model is real.
  7. Department onboarding at scale. Roughly 20 to 30 hours of distributed staff time per college over three to four weeks. Advising and basic needs first, then financial aid, then disability and accessibility, then the rest.

The boundary between counseling and basic needs is drawn differently at different colleges. At GCC they are deliberately separate. Intake has to surface each college’s actual organizational structure rather than exporting GCC’s org chart to nine other campuses.

10. Open questions and risks

Question or riskPriorityPosition today
Is the vendor CRM AI the better answer than building this?HighDifferent problems. A CRM-grounded agent serves students already in the system asking record-dependent questions. CopaMigo serves students not in the system yet. A hybrid is plausible: CopaMigo as the anonymous front door, the CRM handling students who escalate. The district’s data-governance position decides it.
Which model does the district provision?HighUnresolved. The deciding question for OIT is whether the district holds Gemini API access through Vertex AI or only the Workspace assistant, and whether an institutional Claude or Bedrock path exists. Content work continues either way.
Course advising accuracyHighThe known failure mode. Current mitigation is total: the bot links the verified checksheet and names no courses. Lifting that requires advisors to own and approve per-program data.
Chat fallback staffingHighUnproven. The plan routes each office’s chat to the person who already answers its phone, with work-study students as the next layer. Sustainability is a pilot question.
Student skepticism of chat widgetsMediumStudents have learned to ignore the widget on their college homepage. CopaMigo has one exchange to prove it is different.
Content maintenance over timeMediumStale hours and dead links are how tools like this die. Named department owners and the 48-hour currency threshold are the mitigation. A department that cannot commit should not be in the tool.
Multilingual quality beyond SpanishMediumSpanish auto-detect is implemented and needs native-speaker testing for tone. Arabic, Vietnamese, and others follow once Spanish is validated.
Remaining content verificationMediumPending confirmation with the responsible offices: LGBTQ+ resource details, behavioral intervention team contact, financial aid live-chat hours.
Who owns it after the pilot?MediumUndecided. Faculty-led ownership does not scale. Scaling requires an institutional unit, a budget line, and a service level agreement. A gate decision, not an assumption.
