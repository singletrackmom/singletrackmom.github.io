Advanced Data Science:
a graduate course built from open educational resources

Case study · Learning design

A complete graduate course design, and a demonstration of a specific modern workflow: assembling a rigorous course from vetted open educational resources with AI assistance when a dedicated subject-matter expert has not yet been assigned, then building it to be reviewed and finished with the faculty member who will teach it, never shipped without one. It is outcomes first, from vetted open and interactive materials, assessed through professional simulations judged like real client work, designed to a recognized online-course-quality standard (OSCQR) and to Regular and Substantive Interaction from week one, checked for alignment and AI-resistance in the Dial Your Course tool, and validated by a subject-matter expert before delivery. Modeled on the published outcomes of a real graduate course at a leading STEM university.

Graduate, MS level · 3 credits · 15-week semester at about 9 hours per week · 135 student hours (the credit-hour standard HLC applies) · Python · assessed through simulations and a project, not exams · designed to a recognized quality standard (OSCQR) · built to WCAG 2.1 AA.

Status of this draft. This demonstration course was produced by the synthetic-SME method and has not yet been reviewed by a human subject-matter expert, which is the required final step of the method before any course reaches a student. Its technical content should be read as a rigorous, standards-aligned starting point rather than verified fact: specific claims, datasets, or code may contain errors, and surfacing them is precisely what the human-SME review exists to do. What the demonstration establishes is the method and the standards it enforces on every build, not the delivery-readiness of this particular draft.
Purpose and status. This is a demonstration of a course-building method, not a course in production, and not primarily a test of any single tool. The method is a reusable skill that runs the standards-driven build, outcomes, OSCQR, Regular and Substantive Interaction, accessibility, seat time, assessment, and style, and calls on AI agents cast as subject-matter experts (synthetic SMEs) to draft each part, with a human designer directing and the human subject-matter expert who will teach the course reviewing and finishing it before it reaches a student. It is made for the setting where courses are built with faculty, a teaching-and-learning center or an academic program. The outcomes are adapted from the publicly published description and outcomes of a real graduate data-science course at a leading STEM university (verified against its catalog: Python, real datasets, a semester project on the student’s own domain dataset, acquisition through visualization, plus ethics); every learning resource was verified live and its license checked in July 2026; the interaction, assessment, and rubric are original and reviewed to OSCQR. A human subject-matter expert confirms technical accuracy prior to delivery. No student data is involved. The source institution is not named here and this is not affiliated with or endorsed by it.

Regular and Substantive Interaction, designed in from week one

The first design decision, not the last. Under U.S. Department of Education rules (34 CFR 600.2), an online course earns Title IV eligibility only when it delivers Regular and Substantive Interaction (RSI): instructor-initiated, on a predictable schedule, with proactive outreach when a student disengages. A course that is only posted content and readings is, in the Department’s terms, a correspondence course. This design meets RSI every week through at least three of the five recognized substantive activities:

  • Direct instruction — a recorded instructor introduction and concept walkthrough opens every module (placeholders below), plus a live weekly working session.
  • Feedback on coursework — individualized rubric feedback on each deliverable, returned within a posted window, in writing and by screencast.
  • Facilitating discussion — an instructor-seeded, instructor-moderated discussion tied to each learner’s own project, with a required, specific peer response.
  • Answering content questions — a monitored Q&A space and weekly announcements.

Regular is satisfied by a fixed weekly rhythm: a Monday announcement and intro video, a midweek live session and discussion, an end-of-week deliverable, and proactive early-alert outreach to any learner who has not engaged by midweek. Every module names the RSI activities it carries; the schedule below maps them week by week.

Authentic assessment through simulation

The through-line of the course, and the answer to the question a data-science program actually asks: can this person do the work, not can they pass a test a model could sit for them. Every graded task is a professional simulation with a private ground truth and a client-facing defense. The design is grounded in the authentic-assessment literature and built to be structurally resistant to generative-AI substitution.

The grounding. Authentic assessment, after Wiggins, measures performance on the real criterion task under real constraints, with criteria known in advance and room to iterate. Gulikers, Bastiaens and Kirschner’s five-dimensional framework (task, physical context, social context, result/form, and criteria) treats authenticity as a continuum defined by the student’s perceived resemblance to professional practice. Ashford-Rowe, Herrington and Brown add eight criteria, including transfer to a new context, metacognition, real fidelity, and a defended product. This course satisfies all three at once.

The AI-resistance mechanism. The graded object is live reasoning about a private artifact, three things a generative model cannot supply on a student’s behalf:

  • Hidden test data, where the field actually uses it. In the competition module the labels are held out on a private leaderboard, the pattern Stanford’s STATS 202 and DrivenData use, so the score rewards generalization rather than a producible answer. In the two skills drills, a per-student dataset is generated from a process only the instructor holds, so a pasted generic solution scores poorly. These are named as drills, not as client work.
  • Injected incidents. Leakage, bias, missingness, and post-deployment data drift are planted so the student must detect, diagnose, and adapt, the actual work of a data scientist, not a producible text.
  • Judged like a real engagement. The consulting tasks and the capstone are graded the way real sponsors and faculty grade them, on the quality of the decision, the validity of the evaluation design, the business impact, and a live defense, never against a secret answer key, because no real client has one.
  • A live defense. Each major deliverable is defended in a simulated stakeholder meeting where a role-played executive, compliance officer, and engineer ask unscripted, decision-relevant questions.

Formative practice is low-stakes and interactive; summative assessment is a simulation. The two are named in every module below.

Goal

Give a working professional the applied data-science pipeline end to end: acquire and document real data, clean and reason about messy and dynamic data, analyze and model it appropriately, evaluate rigorously, communicate to a decision-maker, and do it ethically, and prove they can do it through a real project they build and defend.

Audience

Graduate and professional learners in an online master’s program, most of them employed adults with a STEM or quantitative background who need the skill to apply at work. The design assumes programming fundamentals (per the source course’s prerequisite) and scaffolds the statistics and machine learning.

Process, how it was built

The method is explicit and outcomes-first: backward design from the published outcomes (Wiggins & McTighe), constructive alignment (Biggs) so every reading, activity, and assessment traces to a stated outcome, Universal Design for Learning for access, and the OSCQR and RSI standards for online quality and interaction. For each outcome I sourced the strongest material across three kinds of open resource, not just textbooks: interactive courseware and explorable models a learner manipulates, runnable lab notebooks, and targeted readings pinned to specific chapters. Every resource was fetched and verified live and separated by license into what can be adapted and re-hosted and what may only be linked and assigned. The connective tissue, the module structure, the simulation-based assessments, and the rubric, is original, authored with AI assistance, then checked for alignment and AI-resistance and reviewed against OSCQR, with technical accuracy validated by a faculty reviewer before delivery. This is a course engineered backward from what a graduate must be able to do, the opposite of pouring content into a template.

Built by orchestrated agents and skills, with a human in the loop. The build is itself part of the case study, and it reflects an emerging, documented practice: using agentic AI as a partner in instructional design, now in the peer-reviewed literature and on conference programs (for example, Rapid AI-Assisted Instructional Design: Using Agentic LLM Tools, a sequential agent-based approach to instructional co-design, and EDUCAUSE’s “AI and Course Design: Machines Can Help, but Only Humans Can Teach”). A lead designer directed a panel of AI agents cast as subject-matter experts, instructional designers, and a synthetic student modeled on the incoming learner, each packaged as a reusable skill (in Google’s Gemini environment the equivalent is a Gem) and held to fixed parameters, the outcomes, OSCQR, Regular and Substantive Interaction, accessibility, the seat-time budget, the student video-and-software scaffolding, and the visual style, so that every part started compliant rather than being audited into compliance afterward. The designer set the parameters, assigned the work, and judged the output; the agents did the search, the drafting, and the adversarial review. The operational detail is held back here on purpose. What is not held back is the principle, and it is the one that EDUCAUSE title names: machines can help, but only humans teach. This produces a rigorous starting point, not a finished course to drop on students. By design it waits until the subject-matter expert who will teach it is engaged, and then designer and expert walk the whole course together, grounding, customizing, and refining until the instructor owns it, because a build without a human expert can be confidently wrong. The agents compress the first-draft labor that once ran for weeks; the human supplies the domain truth and stays accountable. That is the difference between a course built without a subject-matter expert and one built to be ready for one.

Learning outcomes

Adapted from the source course’s published outcomes and written to be observable and assessable. On successful completion, the learner will be able to:

  1. Acquire data from varied structured and unstructured sources (files, databases, APIs, and the web), documenting provenance and access method.
  2. Apply preprocessing strategies to complex, messy, and dynamic datasets: cleaning, missing-data handling, outlier treatment, and feature engineering.
  3. Differentiate among machine-learning approaches, regression, classification, and clustering, and select a method appropriate to a problem and its data.
  4. Evaluate the predictive power and validity of statistical and machine-learning methods using resampling, appropriate metrics, and the bias-variance trade-off.
  5. Communicate findings to a non-technical decision-maker through sound visualization and a defensible data narrative.
  6. Assess the ethical dimensions of a data problem using a recognized governance framework.
  7. Construct a comprehensive data-science project from inception to presentation, and defend its decisions.

Seat time and the 15-week schedule

Sized to the credit-hour definition the Higher Learning Commission applies, the source institution’s regional accreditor. Under the federal standard (34 CFR 600.2), one semester credit is roughly 45 hours of student work; at three credits the course carries 135 hours, delivered across a 15-week semester at about nine hours per week. Big outcomes span two weeks; the capstone spans three. Every module and task opens with an estimated-time note, carried by words and a bold label, not styling alone (WCAG 1.3.1).

The Getting Started module

Before content begins, a Getting Started module orients the learner to the online environment, as every Maricopa online course is expected to and the field’s quality rubrics require. It carries all six elements, and it is where instructor presence starts.

▶ Instructor welcome video (record). A two to three minute welcome: who the instructor is, why the work matters, and the tone for the term. Captioned, with a slide alternative.
▶ Course orientation tour (record). A short screencast walking the course structure in the LMS, where the schedule, deliverables, feedback, and discussion live.

And the remaining four: course expectations (participation, the nine-hours-a-week workload, technology and Python environment, academic-integrity and AI policy, netiquette), student resources (library, tutoring, disability and accessibility services, technical help, plus environment setup), a student introduction activity tied to the course (introduce yourself through the domain and dataset you want to work in, respond to two peers), and a low-stakes student-success assignment (a syllabus quiz and a first environment check so any technology issue surfaces in week one, not week nine).

Instructor presence and communication plan

Presence is designed, not improvised, so the course cannot become “post it and leave”:

  • Before the course: a welcome email about a week out, with the syllabus open to view.
  • Weekly: a Monday announcement and intro video; a live working session; a monitored Q&A discussion.
  • Weekly teaching reflection (not a canned announcement). The Friday note reflects on what the cohort actually posted that week, the discussion, the deliverables, the competition board: a pattern done well and worth copying, a common misstep and how to correct it, and what to shore up before the next module. It responds to the live class’s real work, which is substantive interaction, not a template.
  • Response standard: email and questions answered within 24 to 48 hours, with availability windows stated up front.
  • Early alert: proactive outreach to any learner not engaged by midweek, using the LMS “message students who” and analytics, the RSI “regular” obligation made operational.
  • Feedback: what the learner did well, what needs work, and the next step, in writing and by screencast for the heavier deliverables.
  • Netiquette: a stated policy in the Getting Started module, the syllabus, and any charged discussion.

The course, module by module

One real dataset, the learner’s own choice from a vetted list of authentic sources, runs the length of the course. Each module opens with a recorded instructor introduction and an interactive model the learner manipulates before reading about it, then a low-stakes formative exercise, then a summative simulation on their own data. Resources are tagged: adapt openly licensed and re-hostable, public domain, link only free to use and assign but not to copy.

Assessment architecture and academic integrity

The course is graded on simulations and a project, not exams, which places assessment at the apply level and higher and makes it resistant to generative substitution by design. AI tools are permitted and disclosed: students mark where a model helped and reflect on the judgment they exercised, and the secured elements of the grade, the hidden-ground-truth recovery, the private-test prediction, the planted-defect audit, the drift response, and the live oral defense, are what establish individual achievement. The design assesses the student who uses AI well rather than trying to detect or forbid it, the only durable position now that a model can produce a competent artifact.

Professional-grade artifacts. To reach the bar a PhD reviewer expects, the course adopts recognized instruments: Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al.) and Data Statements for provenance; Model Cards (Mitchell et al.) bridging modeling to the ethics dossier; explicit leakage controls (preprocessing fit inside cross-validation folds only, a frozen pipeline before the private test, a written leakage audit); statistical rigor on metrics (bootstrap confidence intervals and a significance test when comparing models, reporting fold variance rather than a single number); and a reproducibility standard (pinned environment, fixed seeds, one-command reproduce). An interim proposal and mid-project milestone gate the capstone.

Capstone analytic rubric

Eight criteria, each scored Mastery, Proficient, Developing, or Emerging. Mastery requires every criterion at Proficient or above (a competency threshold, not an average). Dimensions adapt the AAC&U Quantitative Literacy and Inquiry & Analysis VALUE rubrics and the EDISON data-science competence framework.

CriterionWhat Mastery looks like
1. Problem framing & acquisitionA precise, answerable question; data acquired from a documented source with provenance and terms of use stated.
2. Data quality & preprocessingCleaning, missing-data, and outlier decisions are principled, reversible, and defended; the pipeline reproduces.
3. Method selection & modelingThe method fits the problem and data; alternatives considered; assumptions stated.
4. Evaluation & validityAppropriate metrics and resampling; generalization shown on unseen data with uncertainty; drift detected and handled; limitations named.
5. Communication & visualizationFigures follow sound encoding; a non-technical decision-maker could act on the narrative.
6. Ethics & responsible practiceBias, privacy, and fairness assessed in governance terms with concrete, proportionate mitigations.
7. Reproducibility & code qualityAnother analyst can rerun the work from the repository; environment pinned; code readable and versioned.
8. DefenseThe learner explains and defends decisions live and answers an unrehearsed follow-up.

AI as a tutor and a discussion partner

Consistent with a course that teaches students to point AI at hard work rather than cheat with it, the design uses AI in two supporting roles, each with a boundary stated plainly.

A course-grounded AI tutor. A custom assistant (a Google Gemini for Education Gem or a ChatGPT Edu GPT) is loaded with the course’s own materials, the syllabus, the reading list, the rubrics, the assignment briefs, and a curated FAQ, so it answers questions grounded in this course rather than the open internet. It is available at any hour, it is patient, and it supports Universal Design for Learning by meeting learners where they are. Its boundary: the tutor is student support, not instructor presence, and it does not count toward Regular and Substantive Interaction, which by federal rule must be instructor-initiated and human. It supplements the instructor’s weekly presence; it does not replace it.

Discussion and role-play agents. The course’s simulations already cast AI as the simulated client, the skeptical reviewer, and the governance board. Custom discussion bots make that concrete and scalable for a solo online learner: rehearse the executive briefing against an AI “vice president,” practice the governance review against an AI “compliance officer,” or argue an ethics position against a bot that pushes back, the kind of role-play a distance student otherwise cannot get, now available on demand. Its boundary: the bots power practice and the scenario, but every graded defense remains a live meeting with faculty and peers, because that human defense is what secures individual achievement and keeps the assessment resistant to substitution.

This is contested territory, and the course’s stance is deliberate. The cautionary cases are real: an AI course tool that repackaged faculty materials without their consent, out of context, and with accuracy errors is exactly what erodes trust in this work. The answer is not to avoid AI; it is to use it the way this course does, with faculty consent and in the loop, on vetted and human-validated material, with a human accountable for teaching and for the grade. AI does the tireless supporting work; it does not quietly take a professor’s content or ship an unreviewed course.

Authentic data, and the dynamic-data design

Each learner picks one real dataset and runs it the length of the course, from a vetted list of authentic, well-documented sources across domains: FRED (economics, updating), the Census/ACS API (demographics, annual vintages), OpenAQ (air quality, near real-time), NOAA (climate), NYC and Chicago open data (civic, daily refresh), World Bank (CC BY 4.0), and UCI / OpenML as documented static fallbacks. The updating sources make the capstone’s mid-project drift event real: the same query, pulled at two points in the term, legitimately differs, so a learner on ACS can watch a small geography drop below the reporting threshold and its rows disappear. For static datasets, a scripted per-student drift injector supplies the same shock. Either way the learner is assessed on diagnosing and repairing a pipeline under a shift that did not exist when the assignment began.

Aligned to the profession

A graduate course should connect to the field, which also keeps it current in a way a fixed textbook cannot. A curated industry news feed runs in the course (by RSS, so it stays current with no maintenance): KDnuggets, Towards Data Science, the arXiv stat.ML feed, and association news; learners react to one item a week that connects to the module. Two compliance flags a reviewer will respect. Interactive models such as MLU-Explain, R2D3, and Setosa are free to use but not openly licensed, so they are linked and assigned, never re-hosted. And the synthetic-data generator used to create hidden-ground-truth datasets (the Synthetic Data Vault) carries a Business Source License, free for academic use but not a permissive open license, so a real deployment either uses it under those terms or pairs the MIT-licensed Faker library with hand-written generative processes. Naming these constraints up front is part of doing the work correctly.

Software and setup

The simulations ask students to record themselves (oral defenses, screencasts, stakeholder briefings) and to run a Python data-science toolchain, and many working professionals have never screen-recorded before. So the course ships a student-facing setup guide, and everything required is free and cross-platform.

Recording. One recorder, the learner’s choice: OBS Studio (free, cross-platform, highest quality, with a five-minute scene-setup walkthrough) or a lower-friction option, Zoom local recording, Loom, QuickTime on Mac, Xbox Game Bar on Windows, or Panopto if the LMS provides it. Every recording is captioned: the guide gives the built-in caption paths (Loom, Panopto, Zoom) and the YouTube-unlisted auto-caption fallback for OBS and QuickTime, and requires an .srt or .vtt file, which is also the WCAG obligation. Work submits as .mp4, named consistently, under a stated length and size, with a large-file path (a Drive or OneDrive link) so nothing fails at the deadline.

The toolchain. A Python environment (Miniconda or venv, Jupyter, Git and GitHub), with Google Colab as a zero-install browser fallback so no learner is blocked by a laptop. Course-specific tools are introduced where they are used: the competition platform (Kaggle Community Competitions or DrivenData), Streamlit for the client app, and Evidently for drift detection. A pre-week-one checklist has every learner test-record a sixty-second clip and confirm the environment runs before content begins, so technology problems surface in week one, not week nine.

Continuous improvement: seat time, learning, and metrics

The design closes its own loop, which is good practice and, in this role’s terms, using learning analytics and performance data to improve the course. Four instruments run every term, mapped to Quality Matters, OSCQR, and Kirkpatrick’s four levels.

  • End-of-course improvement survey (Kirkpatrick level 1). Anonymous and separate from the institutional evaluation, mapped item by item to OSCQR categories: clarity, the value of each material and assessment (which modules helped most and least), workload and pacing, interaction quality, and open feedback. Any item below a set threshold flags a specific design element to revise, not a vague complaint.
  • Seat-time validation. Every module ends with a three-item time log, actual hours and whether they ran over or under the published estimate and why, triangulated against LMS activity, video watch-time, and notebook-session logs. A reconciliation rule adjusts any module outside 0.85 to 1.15 of its estimate and holds the course total inside 135 hours plus or minus ten percent, which is the credit-hour compliance artifact for HLC.
  • Learning measurement (Kirkpatrick level 2). A pre- and post-course competency self-rating on the seven outcomes, corrected for response-shift bias with a retrospective then-and-now item, alongside the direct measure that governs the claim: rubric-scored outcome attainment on the capstone, with a stated success criterion (for example, at least eighty percent of learners at Proficient or above on each outcome).
  • A term-over-term metrics dashboard (Kirkpatrick levels 3 and 4). Completion, pass, and DFW rates, per-outcome attainment, engagement and RSI interaction logs, feedback timeliness, time-on-task, satisfaction, and a three-to-six-month follow-up on whether graduates apply the skills at work, each tied to the decision it informs. A change log records the finding, the mapped standard, the change made, and the re-measured result, the continuous-improvement evidence a quality review expects.

Accessibility and universal design

Built to WCAG 2.1 AA from the first draft: semantic structure, sufficient contrast, meaning never carried by color alone, and no figure whose message depends on sight without an alternative. Every instructor video is captioned with a slide or transcript alternative; every image carries alternative text; and every interactive tool is paired with a non-interactive path to the same concept for any learner who cannot use it. Under Universal Design for Learning, the competency stays fixed while the means of expression flexes, most deliverables accept a written or a recorded form, and each module publishes its time cost so a working adult can plan.

Designed to a recognized quality standard (OSCQR)

Reviewed against the OSCQR rubric, an open, formative online-course-quality standard from the State University of New York, stewarded by the Online Learning Consortium and used widely across the field. The design maps to all six OSCQR categories, and the two that most often fail a course are where it is strongest:

  • Course overview and information: the Getting Started module, stated expectations, and per-week schedule.
  • Design and layout, and content and activities: a consistent module pattern, chunked content, module intro with objectives, and interactive materials rather than static text.
  • Interaction: the RSI plan, weekly instructor presence, and structured peer work.
  • Assessment and feedback: aligned, rubric-scored simulations with individualized, timely feedback.
  • Technology and accessibility: WCAG 2.1 AA and an accessible alternative for every interactive.

Benchmarked against real programs

The project architecture was checked against the actual capstone structures of Berkeley Data 100, Harvard CS109A, Stanford CS229, and the University of Chicago Data Science Clinic, and against the source course’s catalog description. Every deliverable here maps onto what those programs assign, and the design exceeds their baseline on provenance (datasheets), a named ethics framework (NIST AI RMF and model cards), dual technical-and-stakeholder reporting, and a reproducible-repository standard. Following the expert-panel review, hidden test data is confined to where the field genuinely uses it, the private-leaderboard competition and two clearly-named skills drills, while the consulting tasks and the capstone are judged the way real sponsors and faculty judge them, on decision quality, evaluation validity, and defense. The seven outcomes map almost one to one onto the source course’s published outcomes, so this reads as a faithful build of that course, not a generic one.

How this maps to what a designer actually does

This is a responsible open-educational-resource build in full: outcomes first, the best vetted open and interactive material found and correctly licensed, an interaction and presence plan built to RSI and OSCQR from week one, authentic simulation-based assessment grounded in the learning-science literature and engineered to resist generative substitution, accessibility built in, the course connected to the living profession and to real, updating data, and, before it ever reaches a student, technical accuracy reviewed and the course finished with the faculty member who will teach it. It is discipline-agnostic craft applied to a graduate STEM subject, which is the job.
