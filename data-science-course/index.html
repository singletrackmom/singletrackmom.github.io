Advanced Data Science: a graduate course built from open educational resources

Case study · Learning design

A complete, standards-aligned graduate course design, built the way a modern learning-experience designer builds one without a subject-matter expert: from vetted open educational resources, assembled with AI, checked for alignment and AI-resistance in the Dial Your Course tool, and validated by a faculty reviewer before delivery. Modeled on a real master's course, Colorado School of Mines DSCI503, Advanced Data Science.

Graduate, MS level · 3 credits, 135 student hours (the credit-hour standard HLC applies) · 8 modules · assessed by projects, not exams · built to WCAG 2.1 AA.

Purpose and status. This course was designed to test how the Dial Your Course tool handles a graduate STEM build. It is a complete course design, not a course in production. The learning outcomes are adapted from the publicly published Colorado School of Mines DSCI503 outcomes; the readings are current open educational resources with their licenses verified in July 2026; the assessment architecture and rubric are original, grounded in published models. A faculty subject-matter reviewer confirms technical accuracy prior to delivery. No student data is involved.

Goal

Give a working professional the applied data-science pipeline end to end, acquire and clean real data, choose and evaluate models rigorously, communicate findings to a decision-maker, and do it ethically, and prove they can do it through a real project they build and defend, not a test a model could sit for them.

Audience

Graduate and professional learners in an online master’s program, most of them employed adults with a STEM or quantitative background who need the skill to apply at work, not a certificate to frame. The design assumes programming fundamentals (per the DSCI503 prerequisite) and scaffolds the statistics.

Process, how it was built

Designed backward from the published course outcomes. For each outcome I sourced the strongest openly-licensed or freely-available material, separating what can be adapted and re-hosted (Creative Commons BY, public domain, BSD) from what may only be linked and assigned (all-rights-reserved and no-derivatives texts). The connective tissue, module structure, activities, assessments, and rubric, is original, authored with AI assistance and then run through an alignment and AI-resistance check. Because the build proceeds without a subject-matter expert, technical accuracy is validated by a faculty reviewer as the accuracy backstop before delivery. This is the exact workflow a modern learning-experience designer uses for an OER build: find, vet, license, align, author with AI, verify.

Learning outcomes

Adapted from the published DSCI503 student learning outcomes and written to be observable and assessable. On successful completion, the learner will be able to:

  1. Acquire data from varied structured and unstructured sources (files, databases, APIs, and the web), documenting provenance and access method.
  2. Apply preprocessing strategies to complex, messy, and dynamic datasets: cleaning, missing-data handling, outlier treatment, and feature engineering.
  3. Differentiate among machine-learning approaches, classification, regression, and clustering, and select a method appropriate to a problem and its data.
  4. Evaluate the predictive power and validity of statistical and machine-learning methods using resampling, appropriate metrics, and the bias-variance trade-off.
  5. Communicate findings to a non-technical decision-maker through sound visualization and a defensible data narrative.
  6. Assess the ethical dimensions of a data problem, collection, consent, bias and fairness, privacy, and responsible use, using a recognized governance framework.
  7. Construct a comprehensive data-science project from inception to presentation, and defend its decisions.

Seat time, to the accreditor’s standard

The workload is sized to the credit-hour definition the Higher Learning Commission applies, the accreditor for Colorado School of Mines. Under the federal credit-hour standard (34 CFR 600.2), one semester credit represents one hour of instruction and two hours of out-of-class work each week for approximately fifteen weeks, or an equivalent amount of student work for an online course, which is approximately 45 hours per credit. At three credits, the course carries 135 hours of total student engagement, distributed across the eight modules.

Every assignment states its expected time. Each module and each graded task opens with an estimated-time note, for example “Estimated time: approximately two hours to complete this assignment,” so a working professional can plan the week. In keeping with WCAG 1.3.1, the meaning is carried by the words and a bold label rather than by styling alone.

The course, module by module

One real dataset runs the length of the course. In each module the learner completes a piece of the pipeline on their own project and submits a graded deliverable, so the capstone is assembled continuously rather than written at the end. Every reading below is tagged for how it may be used: adapt openly licensed and re-hostable, public domain maximal freedom, link only free to read and assign but not to copy or modify.

Module 1 · about 15 hours · ~5 h reading and video, ~5 h practice, ~5 h on the deliverable

Foundations and the reproducible workflow

Serves: Outcomes 1, 2, 7 (tooling and habits).
Focus: the data-science lifecycle, the Python and pandas environment, notebooks, and version control as the substrate of reproducible work.
  • Software Carpentry, Plotting and Programming in Python and Version Control with GitCC BY 4.0 · adapt
  • Berkeley Data 100, course notes (intro chapters)BSD-3 · adapt
  • MIT OCW 6.0002 (computational-thinking framing)CC BY-NC-SA
Deliverable: a version-controlled project repository with a reproducible notebook that loads a candidate dataset and states the question. UDL: submit the setup as a written walkthrough or a short screen recording. Interaction: instructor-authored welcome and a first-week check-in that reaches anyone who has not committed.
Module 2 · about 16 hours · ~4 h reading and video, ~6 h practice, ~6 h on the deliverable

Data acquisition

Serves: Outcome 1.
Focus: reading structured and unstructured data, file formats, relational data and SQL, APIs, and responsible web scraping, with provenance documentation.
  • Data Carpentry lessons (data organization, SQL)CC BY 4.0 · adapt
  • R for Data Science, 2e, import and databases chaptersCC BY-NC-ND · link
  • Python Data Science Handbook (pandas I/O; example code is MIT-licensed and adaptable)text link, code adapt
Deliverable: acquire the project dataset from a real source (an API or a documented scrape) with a provenance note on source, method, and terms of use. UDL: tabular or narrative provenance log accepted.
Module 3 · about 17 hours · ~4 h reading and video, ~6 h practice, ~7 h on the deliverable

Preprocessing and wrangling

Serves: Outcome 2.
Focus: cleaning, missing-data strategies, outlier detection and treatment, and feature engineering, with the reasoning behind each choice.
  • Python Data Science Handbook, pandas chapters (indexing, missing data, groupby, joins)text link, code adapt
  • Berkeley Data 100, data-cleaning and feature-engineering notesBSD-3 · adapt
  • NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods (EDA, outlier detection)public domain
Deliverable: a documented cleaning pipeline plus a one-page data-quality memo that defends each decision (why this imputation, why this outlier stays or goes). Interaction: instructor feedback on the memo against the rubric.
Module 4 · about 16 hours · ~4 h reading and video, ~5 h practice, ~7 h on the deliverable

Exploratory analysis, visualization, and storytelling

Serves: Outcome 5.
Focus: exploratory data analysis, principled visualization (encoding, color, avoiding misleading figures), and building a narrative for a non-technical decision-maker.
  • Fundamentals of Data Visualization, Claus WilkeCC BY-NC-ND · link
  • Computational and Inferential Thinking (Data 8), visualization and EDACC BY-NC-ND · link
  • Berkeley Data 100, EDA and visualization notesBSD-3 · adapt
Deliverable: an EDA plus a stakeholder-facing visualization narrative: three to five figures and a short brief that a manager could act on. UDL: the narrative may be a written brief or a recorded three-minute walkthrough.
Module 5 · about 18 hours · ~5 h reading and video, ~6 h practice, ~7 h on the deliverable

Supervised learning: regression and classification

Serves: Outcomes 3 and 4.
Focus: linear and logistic regression, trees and ensembles, and matching a method to a supervised problem, with interpretation.
  • An Introduction to Statistical Learning (ISLP, Python edition), the applied spine, chapters 3, 4, 8free to read · link
  • MIT OCW 6.036, Introduction to Machine LearningCC BY-NC-SA
  • MIT OCW 18.650, Statistics for Applications (inference foundation)CC BY-NC-SA
Deliverable: fit, interpret, and compare at least one regression and one classification model on the project data, with a written rationale for the choice. Interaction: a facilitated discussion where each learner critiques a peer’s method choice.
Module 6 · about 18 hours · ~5 h reading and video, ~6 h practice, ~7 h on the deliverable

Unsupervised learning and rigorous model evaluation

Serves: Outcomes 3 and 4.
Focus: clustering and dimensionality reduction; cross-validation, the bias-variance trade-off, appropriate metrics (ROC and AUC, precision and recall, F1), and the difference between fitting and generalizing.
  • ISLP, resampling (ch. 5) and unsupervised learning (ch. 12)free to read · link
  • The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 7, model assessment and selection (graduate-rigor bias-variance)free to read · link
  • Berkeley Data 100, cross-validation and bias-variance notesBSD-3 · adapt
Deliverable: a model-evaluation study using cross-validation and justified metrics, plus a held-out prediction scored on data the learner never saw (a private-test-set component, in the spirit of a Kaggle private leaderboard, which rewards generalization over leaderboard-gaming and is inherently AI-resistant).
Module 7 · about 15 hours · ~4 h reading and video, ~5 h practice, ~6 h on the deliverable

Data and AI ethics

Serves: Outcome 6.
Focus: the ethics of data collection and consent, bias and fairness in the pipeline, privacy, and responsible-AI governance, applied to the learner’s own project data.
  • Fairness and Machine Learning, Barocas, Hardt, NarayananCC BY-NC-ND · link
  • Data Feminism, D’Ignazio and Klein (open access)open access · link
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage)public domain
  • MIT OCW, Social and Ethical Responsibilities of Computing (cases)CC BY-NC-SA
Deliverable: an ethics audit of the learner’s own project, sources of bias, privacy exposure, and fairness considerations, expressed in NIST AI RMF terms with concrete mitigations. This grounds ethics in the student’s real work rather than a canned case a model could analyze.
Module 8 · about 20 hours · ~2 h reading and video, ~3 h practice, ~15 h on the deliverable

Capstone integration and defense

Serves: Outcome 7 (and all others in integration).
Focus: assembling the eight weeks into one coherent project, a reproducible analysis and a decision-maker-facing presentation, and defending the decisions live.
  • Model deliverable structures adapted from the University of Chicago Data Science Clinic rubrics (planning, progress, one-pager, presentation, code cleanup)public models
Deliverable: the final capstone, a reproducible repository, a written technical report, a stakeholder brief, and a recorded or live oral defense and code walkthrough in which the learner explains one modeling decision and answers a follow-up. The defense plus the commit history plus a reflective memo form a chain of process evidence that a generative model cannot supply on the student’s behalf.

Assessment architecture

The course is graded on the project, not on exams, which places assessment at the apply level and higher and makes it resistant to generative substitution by design. A single real-dataset project is decomposed into module deliverables (the University of Chicago Data Science Clinic model), so the grade rests on the record of making, not a single final artifact. Predictive tasks are scored on a held-out private test set (the Kaggle private-leaderboard pattern) so that generalization, not memorization or prompt-engineering, is what earns the score. Individual achievement is secured by a mandatory oral defense and code walkthrough, the mechanism a model cannot sit in for.

Capstone analytic rubric

Eight criteria, each scored Mastery, Proficient, Developing, or Emerging. Mastery of the course requires every criterion at Proficient or above (a competency-based threshold, not an average). The dimensions are adapted from the AAC&U Quantitative Literacy and Inquiry & Analysis VALUE rubrics and anchored to the EDISON data-science competence framework.

CriterionWhat Mastery looks like
1. Problem framing & acquisitionA precise, answerable question; data acquired from a documented source with provenance and terms of use stated.
2. Data quality & preprocessingCleaning, missing-data, and outlier decisions are principled and defended; the pipeline is reproducible.
3. Method selection & modelingThe method fits the problem and data; alternatives are considered; assumptions are stated.
4. Evaluation & validityAppropriate metrics and resampling; generalization demonstrated on unseen data; limitations named.
5. Communication & visualizationFigures follow sound encoding principles; a non-technical decision-maker could act on the narrative.
6. Ethics & responsible practiceBias, privacy, and fairness assessed in governance terms with concrete, proportionate mitigations.
7. Reproducibility & code qualityAnother analyst can rerun the work from the repository; code is readable and version-controlled.
8. DefenseThe learner explains and defends decisions live and answers an unrehearsed follow-up.

Academic integrity and the use of AI

AI tools are permitted and their use is disclosed, consistent with a process-over-product stance. Students are asked to mark where a model helped and to reflect on the judgment they exercised, and the secured elements of the grade, the private-test prediction, the oral defense, the commit history, and the reflective memo, are what establish individual achievement. The design assesses the student who uses AI well rather than trying to detect or forbid it, which is the only durable position now that a model can produce a competent artifact.

Accessibility and universal design

Built to WCAG 2.1 AA from the first draft: semantic structure, sufficient contrast, meaning never carried by color alone, and no figure whose message depends on sight without an alternative. Under Universal Design for Learning, the competency stays fixed while the means of expression flexes, most deliverables accept a written or a recorded form, and each module publishes its time cost so a working adult can plan. Every course video would be captioned; every image would carry alternative text.

How this maps to what a designer actually does

This is the OER-without-a-subject-matter-expert build in full: outcomes first, the best vetted open material found and correctly licensed, original design and assessment authored with AI assistance, alignment and AI-resistance checked, accessibility built in, and technical accuracy validated by a faculty reviewer before delivery. It is discipline-agnostic craft applied to a graduate STEM subject, which is the job.
