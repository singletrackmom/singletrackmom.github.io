1. Experience with AI, automation, or related technologies

Over the past year I’ve designed, built and run AI systems for student-facing and administrative work at the Maricopa Community Colleges. I build on the Claude API and Claude’s agent tools, Google Apps Script and Sheets, Discord webhooks, and GitHub Pages, and I work in Canvas daily.

My role has been project lead and builder, and increasingly evaluator. The clearest example is a scheduled agent that replaced a task I used to do by hand. It searches for entry-level digital media jobs, checks each posting live on the employer’s own site, filters by pay, experience and level, and posts to our students’ job board. Since August it has published more than 60 verified postings. When two early posts were too senior for our students, I rewrote the rules, not just the posts.

I also built a course quality suite whose core checks are rule-based on purpose, so the same course always gets the same answer. Run on a real course, it found that 14 of 27 graded items weren’t tied to any outcome, a gap a manual review had missed. At the district level, I co-chair the AI Resource Center’s Student Support and Success domain. There I designed a 52-agent study that tests where students get stuck across ten colleges’ systems, and I’m using those findings to evaluate vendor, enterprise and build options.

2. A specific project

At Glendale Community College, students and employees had to navigate separate offices and phone numbers for help: the student helpdesk, the employee helpdesk, campus safety and classroom AV support. Nobody owned the whole experience, so people bounced between offices.

I led the consolidation into a single campus-wide point of contact, one number with a phone tree behind it. That meant working with each office to [how she worked with each office], agreeing on routing rules, and [how she handled offices that wanted to keep their own number]. Adoption depended on the offices trusting the routing, so [what built that trust].

The result was one front door for campus support, [any measure], and the college’s Kudos Award. I’m doing the same work now with AI across ten colleges. Our service crosswalk found that the same service carries a different name at every college, so a problem stalling students at one campus often already has a working fix at another. I want to find that before anyone buys or builds something new.

3. Staying informed and applying it

I learn by building and through structured programs. I completed the League for Innovation AI Fellows program and hold the Google Generative AI Leader credential. I’m at EDUCAUSE in Denver this week meeting student-success platform vendors. I also convened a campus AI community of practice of early adopters from faculty, staff and administration, which keeps me honest about what works outside my own experiments.

Applying it mostly means knowing when not to use a model. In the course quality suite, I kept generative AI out of the core checks because compliance checks need the same answer every time. In the journey study, four early findings fell apart once we opened the actual pages instead of trusting search results. So I wrote a two-tier evidence rule and applied it back across the whole study. Knowing what AI can’t yet be trusted to do is as useful to an institution as knowing what it can.
