1. Describe your experience with AI, automation, or related technologies in a professional setting.

What tools, platforms, or technologies have you worked with?

I build on the Claude API and Claude’s agent tools, with Google Apps Script and Sheets for workflow automation, GitHub Pages for publishing, and simple integrations such as Discord webhooks. I also work with ChatGPT, Google Gemini and Microsoft Copilot. None of this building was assigned to me. I took it on because I wanted to know firsthand what AI could do for the work around me. Four tools I designed and built:

  • Course quality suite (Dial Your Course). Audits a course against quality and compliance standards. The core checks are rule-based, with no model in them, because a compliance check has to give the same answer every time. In pilot. Overview · Requirements
  • Student journey study. A usability study run by 52 AI agents across ten colleges’ systems, in progress now. Described in question 2. Study
  • Job search agents. Six scheduled agents that find openings, verify each posting on the employer’s own site, filter by level and pay, and publish a matched list. Overview
  • Render. A career tool for the capstone course I teach, where students will build and keep their own career agent. First pilot this fall. Overview · Requirements

What was your role (e.g., project lead, business analyst, change leader, technical contributor, strategist, evaluator)?

Project lead and builder on each tool, and I write the product requirements document for each one. I am also a change leader and an evaluator. I spent seven years as Director of Instructional Technology in the Innovation Center, a unit of the college’s Office of Information Technology. I work in one of the largest community college districts in the country, the Maricopa Community Colleges, with ten colleges and more than 140,000 students. I sit on the steering committee of the district AI Resource Center, which decides how AI tools and practices roll out across the ten colleges, and I co-chair its Student Support and Success domain. On my own campus I convened an AI community of practice of early adopters from faculty, staff and administration. Earlier, I led more than 45 faculty to fully online teaching in a matter of weeks during the pandemic.

How were these technologies used to support business operations or decision-making?

Decision-making. Early findings from the journey study are shaping how we evaluate vendor, enterprise and build options for student support, and I bring that work to the district AI Resource Center steering committee. I have also designed an intake and scoring process for AI requests from across the district, which sorts each request into a pilot, more discovery, a training need, or a decline. The design is complete and it has not launched yet.

Operations, at pilot scale. These tools are pilots, not production systems. CopaMigo, a multilingual tool that routes students to the right campus service, is in a program-wide pilot in my department. The course quality suite, also in pilot, turns a manual course review into a consistent, repeatable check. The job search agents are the one piece that runs on its own every day. They automated searching and verifying I used to do by hand, and the one for our students has posted more than 60 verified jobs since August.

2. This role requires partnering with business stakeholders to identify opportunities for AI-enabled improvements. Share a specific example of a project or initiative involving AI, automation, or digital transformation.

What business challenge or opportunity were you addressing?

Across our ten colleges, students get stuck trying to reach basic services such as advising, financial aid and registration, and each college delivers those services differently. Before the district buys or builds AI for student support, we need to know where the real barriers are and which ones AI can responsibly address.

How did you work with stakeholders to evaluate a process, gather requirements, or drive adoption of a new technology or way of working?

As co-chair of the Student Support and Success domain, I built a cross-college service map and sent it to representatives at each campus to verify. That review is how I gather requirements and document the current state: each campus corrects how its process actually runs, not how the website says it runs.

What approach did you take, and what was the result? (Outcome or impact on the organization, team, or process)

I designed a usability study with 52 AI agents, fifty of them playing student personas, that work through real tasks on each college’s own systems and record where a student would get stuck. I rate each barrier by severity to separate where automation could help from where a student needs a person. The study is in progress, and early findings are already shaping how we evaluate vendor, enterprise and build options. I took them into vendor meetings at EDUCAUSE this week to research tools that could tie the district’s AI student success initiative together. Study

For a completed example of the same kind of work: I was Director of Instructional Technology in the Innovation Center, a unit of the college’s Office of Information Technology (OIT) where faculty came for help with teaching and learning ideas and projects. In that role I led our campus onto its first learning management system. We started with no platform, no standards and no faculty using one. I implemented it with district OIT and sat on the district LMS steering committee, made up of representatives from every campus and OIT leadership. On campus I created the course templates, worked with the training department to develop the faculty training, and taught a class for faculty on online learning. After launch I maintained the system, helped faculty build their courses, and coached them on quality standards.

3. How do you stay informed about emerging AI technologies and trends, and how have you applied that knowledge in your work?

How I stay informed

I learn by building. The first AI tool I built for myself was a professional development dashboard I use on my iPad to keep up with the field, and I maintain a custom RSS feed that follows what leading universities are doing with AI. I completed the League for Innovation AI Fellows program and the Google Generative AI Leader training, and I have taken AI courses through Coursera and Anthropic Academy. This week I attended EDUCAUSE in Denver, where I went to sessions on change management for universities rolling out AI initiatives and met with vendors about tools for our student success work. I am attending two more AI conferences in the next few weeks.

How I have applied it

Mostly by knowing when not to use a model. In the course quality suite I kept generative AI out of the core checks, because a compliance check has to give the same answer every time. In the journey study, four early findings fell apart once I opened the actual pages instead of trusting search results, so I wrote a two-tier evidence rule and applied it back across everything collected so far. Knowing what AI cannot yet be trusted to do is as useful to an institution as knowing what it can.

1. CopaMigo Lead with this one.

Use it when they ask: Tell us about something you built. · How would you roll out an assistant across campus? · How do you work with departments? · How do you keep AI answers accurate?

The point, in your own words: I built a student services assistant, and the hard part was never the AI. It was getting each office to own its answers.

  • Why: students did not know which office handled their problem, or what it was called. The website assistant could only hand back links.
  • Where it came from: I built it after starting the student journey study, which showed where students get stuck.
  • First step: I checked what already existed. The big platforms do case management and alerts. None had an answer bank written the way students ask, owned by the office.
  • What it does: a student types or speaks in their own words, in their own language. It answers what the office would say, or hands off with a card: who to see, hours, what to ask for.
  • Guardrails: crisis and Title IX routes are fixed and come first. It never names a course from memory. It routes to a person, it does not replace one.
  • Size: more than two dozen service areas and more than 100 service links.
  • Testing: students in my Design Studio course tested it, and I revised the routing and answers. It now runs as a small pilot in my program’s Discord.
  • The department form: per question, the answer, where the student goes next, the source page, and whether it needs a person. Per department, the role that keeps answers current and how often they are checked.
  • Limits, say them: no department has gone through the form yet. No sponsor yet. The rating and counter are designed, not built. No outcome numbers.
  • Pass marks when it is piloted: 80 percent of rated handoffs say right office, and no more than 15 percent of questions with no match.
  • Three gates before a college pilot: the server-side step, the accessibility audit, and department sign-off.

Never say staff wrote the answers, that it collects thumbs today, or that it is in production.

2. Campus LMS adoption Change management and adoption.

Use it when they ask: Tell us about leading a change. · How do you get people to adopt something? · Tell us about working with sponsors. · What did you learn from a rollout?

The point, in your own words: We moved a campus from everyone doing their own thing onto one learning system, and we did it without a mandate.

  • Before: course websites, handouts, Word documents, an open-source system, a discussion board tool and a shared drive.
  • My role: Director of Instructional Technology in the Innovation Center. Say “we.” The Director of Training and I ran the evaluation together.
  • Options: five. Join the district system, join another college’s system, two commercial products, or build on open source. The district system fit the requirements and the budget.
  • Sponsors: the Vice President of Administrative Services and the Vice President of Academic Affairs.
  • How: a pilot with early adopters. Others joined when they saw it working in a colleague’s course.
  • Result: nearly all faculty ended up on it. Say “nearly all,” no number.
  • The lesson: the tools and the training both sat in IT, and we lacked faculty buy-in. So the Director of Training and I visited the other teaching centers and wrote the proposal for one on our campus. It was approved.
  • Bridge to CU Anschutz: this role sits in ISS and serves offices like research administration and finance. Ask how ISS and those offices work together today.

3. The purchase nobody questioned The “procurement story.”

Use it when they ask: Why does intake matter? · Tell us about a request that should have been declined. · A time a decision was made without the right information.

The point, in your own words: I have seen what happens with no front door, and it cost a year.

  • What happened: an office bought a student chatbot platform because two peer colleges had it.
  • What was missing: nobody checked it against the real need, or asked what we already had. I had already built and tested a working version of the same thing.
  • My part: not my decision, and I was not asked. Afterward I offered my researched content so the new platform would not start from zero.
  • Result: the purchase stalled in implementation. My prototype is still the one students can use.

5. Four findings I took back Time you were wrong.

Use it when they ask: Tell us about a time you were wrong. · How do you make sure AI output is accurate? · How do you handle data quality? · Responsible AI.

The point, in your own words: In my own study, four early findings did not hold up, and I retracted them.

  • The study: AI agents walk the websites of ten colleges the way a student would, and report where they get stuck.
  • What went wrong: four early findings were based on search results. When I opened the actual pages, all four had overstated the barrier.
  • What I did: retracted them. Then I wrote a rule with two levels: what a search result can claim, and what an opened page can claim.
  • The part that matters: I applied the rule backward to everything already collected, not only going forward.
  • The line: “The study’s credibility rests more on the retractions than on the findings.”

Do not soften it. It was your error, and catching it is the story.

6. It ran, and it was wrong A mistake.

Use it when they ask: Tell us about a mistake. · What have you learned about working with AI tools? · How do you check AI work?

The point, in your own words: “It runs” is not the same as “it is correct.” I learned that by shipping something that ran.

  • What happened: I shipped a prototype that worked on screen. It contained placeholder links the AI had invented.
  • Whose fault: mine. I had not verified them.
  • What changed: the agents now verify sources as they build, and I check the output before anything ships.
  • Why it matters here: an assistant that sends a student to a dead link is worse than no assistant.

Do not blame the tool.

7. The pilot that cost more A pilot that did not pay off.

Use it when they ask: Tell us about a pilot that did not work. · How do you decide not to scale something? · A negative result.

The point, in your own words: I tried having AI direct a whole visual system, and it took more effort than doing it by hand. I wrote that down as the finding.

  • The project: a recruitment newsletter for four digital media programs, sent to feeder high schools.
  • The test: could AI direct the visual system and save effort?
  • Result: no. It cost more effort than hand work would have.
  • What was worth keeping: the research. AI went school by school and found the teachers who already have our future students: 211 named people across 50 high schools in ten districts.
  • Status: built and ready. Distribution is paused pending administrative sign-off.

Do not call it a failure. A pilot that tells you not to scale did its job.

9. The Gemini study Recent work.

Use it when they ask: A request that turned out to be training, not a tool. · Tell us about recent work. · How do you gather evidence before a decision? · How do you test a tool before recommending it?

The point, in your own words: The district approved Gemini, and nobody knew what a student under 18 could not do in it. So I tested it.

  • The question: can a student under 18 complete an assignment that uses Gemini?
  • Method: 27 example assignments built from official course competencies. Each one run in two accounts, under 18 and 18 and older, with the same prompts.
  • The control: a block only counted if the older account completed the same step.
  • Result: 26 of 27 can be completed. 18 as written, 8 with an alternate step, 1 needs another route.
  • Two kinds of blocks: missing tools (Deep Research, music generation, image editing) and refused content.
  • The fix: not a new tool. One line in the assignment: “Under 18: do this instead.” That is guidance, not software.
  • Limits: one run per step. In committee review. No minors took part.

CU Anschutz runs ChatGPT Edu, not Gemini. The method is the point: test in both conditions before you decide.

10. The course with no expert Human in the loop.

Use it when they ask: How do you keep a human in the loop? · Tell us about an experiment. · How do you take feedback? · Can AI build a course?

The point, in your own words: I ran an experiment to see how far AI gets with no subject-matter expert, then had an expert tell me.

  • The test: a panel of AI agents drafted a graduate data science course from vetted open resources, against fixed quality standards. I chose a field that is not mine on purpose.
  • The review: a business and data analyst with an M.S. in applied statistics and data science read it afterward.
  • Her estimate: about 78 percent usable as built. Say “her estimate.”
  • What held up: the simulations, seeded datasets and live defenses, because students have to show their own reasoning.
  • What she changed: the title promised more than the content. I renamed it Applied Data Science.
  • My position: I do not recommend building a course without an expert. It is a starting point for a faculty member handed a course on short notice.
  • Limits: not yet verified by the faculty member who would teach it, and not tested with students.

Do not say a faculty member signed off. That has not happened yet.
