About the Position

The Emerging Technology Program Analyst identifies, evaluates, and pilots emerging technologies that have the potential to improve county operations, employee productivity, and resident services. With a primary focus on AI, automation, and other emerging technologies, this role scans the technology landscape, explores practical applications for Jefferson County, and leads structured pilot initiatives to determine whether new solutions can deliver meaningful value.

This role requires curiosity, relationship-building, and strategic thinking to identify promising technology opportunities, engage partners across the county, and determine whether emerging technologies can deliver meaningful value through pilots and experimentation. As technologies mature from exploration to broader adoption, this position may also support the development of operational models, governance practices, implementation recommendations, and change management approaches that help the county adopt new tools responsibly and sustainably.

The ideal candidate is a self-starter who thrives in ambiguity, enjoys exploring new technologies, and can translate emerging trends into actionable opportunities for the organization while balancing innovation, risk, and practical implementation.

Openers

“Tell me about yourself.”

I’m a design thinker who finds promising technology, tests it with the people who will actually use it, and builds the buy-in to make good ideas stick, and I’ve done that inside one of the country’s largest public systems, the ten-college Maricopa district, for more than twenty years. “Why are you interested in this position, and why would you be successful?”

I’ve spent decades working with a community-college audience, and that is where my heart is. My students are exactly who this kind of work is for: close to half are food-insecure, many are veterans, single parents, or neurodivergent, and we’re a Hispanic-Serving Institution where roughly 45% of students are Hispanic. My mission every day is to make their lives a little better, one by one. I mentor them from their first course through their capstone and into their internships, which are often their first real jobs.

In the capstone I built, they prepare portfolios, write resumes, practice interviewing, and build a professional-development plan so no one graduates without a plan for what is next, and they reflect honestly on the skills they still need to grow. I built an AI tool, Render, to hold all of it in one place instead of scattered folders and documents, so a student can build and keep iterating on their plan using AI skills I teach them across the semester. I’m piloting it this fall.

Stepping outside of higher education wasn’t really on my radar until I came across this job description. What drew me is that it blends my favorite part of my job, mentoring young people, with innovating using design thinking and building with AI, and it’s for the county I live in. Building with AI is where my energy is now, and I’ve spent my whole summer, my contract is nine months, heads-down learning: I’m a League for Innovation AI Fellow, I did the Google Generative AI Leader training and an EDUCAUSE program, and I’m midway through Scrum training. Mentoring, building with AI, and solving problems for the community is exactly what your team does, and exactly what I want to keep doing.

Identify, evaluate, pilot, advance tech “Describe how you take a new technology from evaluation to a decision, with attention to feasibility, risk, and ownership.”

I start with discovery, human-centered, understanding the real workflow and what people actually need, then weigh feasibility, cost, risk, security, and governance before committing. If there is a clear problem and a way to measure success, I run a small pilot with metrics up front, then make an honest call, scale, modify, or stop, and a plan for who owns it. My cleanest completed example is a platform evaluation and RFP that started from the users’ workflow and carried through to adoption; my current builds, Render and the ten-college study, are prototypes and a study in progress, run the same disciplined way, and I am careful to call a prototype a prototype.

Stakeholder engagement “Tell us about a time you built support for a new idea people were skeptical about.”

In the ten-college study I had to bring along advisors, financial-aid staff, IT, and leadership who were wary that “AI” meant replacing people. I led with discovery and listening, framed every fix as taking routine work off their desks so they get more time with the people they serve, and proposed small pilots so they could see it before committing. The same human-centered approach works when I help faculty adopt AI through a community of practice: start from their problem, show one concrete example, and make trying it feel safe and optional. Adoption is a design problem as much as a technical one.

Translate for non-technical audiences “How do you explain complex technology to a non-technical audience?”

This is core to human-centered work; you can’t design a service or roll out a tool if people don’t understand it. I have taught in person and online for years, presented at conferences, and demoed AI to both senior leaders and frontline staff, and each audience needs a different translation. For instance, I ran a thirty-minute session for faculty on how to move a course online, breaking something that felt overwhelming into a few concrete steps. I turn how a tool works into what it means for the person in front of me and what they can do next.

As a product manager at ProQuest/XanEdu I took products from user research and requirements through usability testing to release, concept to recommendation to shipped. How AmeriCorps works (the model behind your paid campus teams)

You helped write the position description for your student’s AmeriCorps role; here is the money and the mechanics so you can speak to it cleanly.

  • What it is. National service. A member commits to a term of service, a set number of hours, with an approved program that meets a real community need. Community benefit is the whole purpose.
  • The money, two parts. A modest living allowance (a stipend) during service, since it is national service, not a market-rate salary, and then, after completing the hours, the Segal AmeriCorps Education Award.
  • The Education Award, and yes it can pay loans. It equals the maximum Pell Grant for that year and can be used two ways: to repay qualified student loans (federal Title IV and similar) or to pay current tuition and training at eligible schools. It must be used within seven years.
  • How it gets approved. AmeriCorps money flows through grants. For AmeriCorps State programs it runs through a state service commission (in Colorado, Serve Colorado, the Governor’s commission). A host organization applies for the grant and the member slots, and the member positions and their service activities are approved through that grantee. That is why you wrote the position description but someone else submitted the paperwork: the host org carries the grant.
