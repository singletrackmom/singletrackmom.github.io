# Instructional Design Study Guide
### Backward design, adult learning science, and measuring real success

_A refresher and working reference for Michelle. Use it to rebuild the vocabulary, guide the Render and AVC 100 redesigns, and speak fluently in a walkthrough. Nothing here is exotic, it's the established core of the field, the stuff an instructional designer is expected to do by default._

---

## 0. The mindset shift (start here)

Right now "success" mostly means students who don't drop out. Retention is a **lagging indicator**, it tells you something happened, but not whether learning happened or why. The instructional-design move is to define success as **demonstrated attainment of the learning outcomes**, then use retention/DFW rates as one signal among several.

The one sentence to internalize: **decide what students should be able to DO, decide how you'll SEE it, then build the learning that gets them there.** That's backward design. Everything below hangs off it.

---

## 1. Backward Design (Understanding by Design)

From Grant Wiggins & Jay McTighe, *Understanding by Design* (UbD). You design in the reverse of how we usually teach: not "what will I cover / what activities are fun," but "what's the end result, and what proves it."

**Three stages, always in this order:**

1. **Identify desired results.** What should learners know, understand, and be able to do? Write measurable learning outcomes (see §3). Distinguish the "enduring understanding" (the thing that lasts) from nice-to-know detail. Optionally frame an *essential question* the course keeps returning to.
2. **Determine acceptable evidence.** *Before* planning any activity, decide how you'll know they got there. What performance, artifact, or task would a competent person be able to produce? This is where you design the assessment and its rubric (see §4).
3. **Plan learning experiences and instruction.** *Now* build the lessons, materials, practice, and scaffolding, chosen specifically because they prepare students to succeed on the Stage 2 evidence.

**Why it matters:** most courses are built in the wrong order (content first, assessment bolted on), which is how you end up with activities that don't actually build toward the outcome. Backward design forces **alignment**.

**Constructive alignment** (John Biggs) is the companion idea: the outcomes, the assessments, and the learning activities must all point at the same target. If an outcome says "students will *evaluate*," but the assessment is a multiple-choice recall quiz, they're misaligned. In a walkthrough, "I aligned outcomes, assessment, and activities" is a sentence that signals you know the craft.

---

## 2. Adult learning science (andragogy and friends)

Your learners are adults, so design for how adults actually learn.

**Andragogy, Malcolm Knowles.** Six core assumptions about adult learners, each with a design implication:

- **Need to know**, adults want to know *why* before they invest. → State relevance and purpose up front.
- **Self-concept / autonomy**, they're self-directing. → Give choice, ownership, self-assessment.
- **Experience**, they bring a deep well of it. → Connect new material to what they already know; let them contribute it.
- **Readiness to learn**, tied to real-life roles and tasks. → Anchor learning in authentic, job-relevant situations.
- **Orientation to learning**, problem-centered, not subject-centered. → Teach through problems and application, not abstract coverage.
- **Motivation**, largely internal (growth, self-worth, career). → Emphasize meaning and progress over grades and compliance.

**Self-Determination Theory** (Deci & Ryan) explains the motivation piece and connects directly to retention: people persist when three needs are met, **autonomy** (choice/agency), **competence** (I can do this / I see progress), and **relatedness** (I belong / I'm connected to people). Deadlines and high-stakes tests often damage all three; authentic work, feedback, and community support them. This is the research spine under "human connection keeps students enrolled."

**How Learning Works** (Ambrose et al.), seven research-based principles worth knowing: prior knowledge, how students organize knowledge, motivation, developing mastery, practice + feedback, course climate, and metacognition (learning to monitor their own learning).

---

## 3. Writing measurable learning outcomes

An outcome is a statement of what the learner will be able to **do**, observably, by the end. Weak outcomes use fuzzy verbs ("understand," "know," "appreciate") you can't measure. Strong outcomes use action verbs you can see and assess.

**Bloom's Taxonomy (revised, Anderson & Krathwohl)** gives you the verb ladder, low to high:

1. **Remember** (define, list, recall)
2. **Understand** (explain, summarize, classify)
3. **Apply** (use, demonstrate, implement, produce)
4. **Analyze** (compare, differentiate, deconstruct)
5. **Evaluate** (critique, justify, assess)
6. **Create** (design, compose, develop, build)

Mines' JD literally asks for assessments "at the apply level or higher", that's Bloom's. Aim most course outcomes at **Apply and above**, since that's where real capability lives (and where AI-resistant, authentic work lives too).

**A clean outcome format:** *"By the end of this course, students will be able to [action verb] [what] [in what context / to what standard]."*
Example (AVC 248): "Students will be able to **produce** a professional portfolio and career plan that meets industry expectations for their field." (Apply/Create, observable, assessable.)

Keep it to **3–6 course-level outcomes**. Every module and assignment should ladder up to one of them.

---

## 4. Assessment: how you actually see success

**Formative vs. summative:**
- **Formative** = low-stakes checks *during* learning that inform teaching and give feedback (drafts, self-checks, peer critique, quick polls). This is where learning is actually built.
- **Summative** = the graded judgment of attainment *at the end* (portfolio, performance task, capstone artifact).

**Authentic / performance assessment** = students do a task that mirrors real-world use, rather than answering questions *about* it. Your experiential work (client projects, portfolios, The Traveler, murals) is authentic assessment, name it that way. This is also where assessment is heading in the AI era: authentic tasks and simulation, because they measure transfer and are hard to fake.

**Rubrics** turn a fuzzy judgment into measurable data. A rubric lists the **criteria** (tied to the outcomes) and **performance levels** (e.g. emerging / proficient / exemplary) with descriptors. Benefits: consistent scoring, transparent expectations for students, and, critically, **rubric scores are your learning data**. Aggregate them and you can see which outcomes students are/aren't hitting.

**Alignment matrix** (a simple, powerful artifact to show in a walkthrough): a table mapping each **outcome → the assessment that measures it → the activities that build it**. If a row is empty anywhere, you've found a gap.

---

## 5. Measuring "success" the right way

Move from a single lagging metric (retention) to a small set of measures tied to learning:

- **Outcome attainment**, % of students reaching "proficient" on each outcome (from rubric data). This is the core one.
- **Learning gain**, a simple **pre/post** (a short diagnostic or self-assessment at start and end) shows growth, not just endpoint.
- **Mastery / competency**, did they demonstrate the competency, regardless of seat time? (This is the competency-based idea.)
- **Engagement signals**, LMS analytics, completion of formative checks, participation, *leading* indicators that predict trouble early.
- **Retention / DFW rate**, keep it, but as one lagging signal, ideally correlated back to where students struggled.

**Evaluation frameworks to name-drop accurately:**
- **Kirkpatrick's Four Levels**, Reaction (did they like it), Learning (did they learn it), Behavior (do they use it), Results (did it move the outcome). Most courses only measure Reaction; instructional designers push to Learning and beyond.
- **Baseline → intervention → measure**, establish the baseline *before* you change something, then measure after. (This is exactly the language in the strategy JDs: "baseline performance before interventions and track impact post-deployment.")

The instructional-designer version of your current goal: *"I'm defining outcome-attainment and learning-gain measures so I can tell whether the redesign improved learning, not just whether students stayed enrolled."*

---

## 6. Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

From CAST. Design for learner variability from the start instead of retrofitting accommodations. Three principles, offer **multiple means of**:

- **Engagement** (the "why", options for motivation, relevance, self-regulation)
- **Representation** (the "what", present content in multiple formats: text, video, audio, visual)
- **Action & Expression** (the "how", let students show mastery in more than one way)

UDL pairs naturally with accessibility (WCAG/ADA) and with your no-deadline / competency thinking. It's also a strong, current vocabulary word for any ID conversation.

---

## 7. What you can do NOW, in your current class (low lift, high signal)

Pick even two of these and you're demonstrably "doing instructional design":

1. **Write 3–6 measurable course outcomes** at Apply level or above. Post them where students see them.
2. **Build/clean up one rubric** tied to those outcomes, with 3 performance levels. Start collecting the scores as data.
3. **Make an alignment matrix** for the course (outcome → assessment → activity). Fix any empty rows.
4. **Add one formative checkpoint** with feedback before the summative (a draft, a self-check, a peer critique).
5. **Add a simple pre/post** (even a 5-question self-rating on the outcomes) to capture learning gain.
6. **Define success metrics** for the course beyond retention: outcome-attainment % and one learning-gain measure.
7. **Tag each existing assignment** to a Bloom's level, notice if everything is stuck at Remember/Understand and push some to Apply/Create.

For **AVC 100 / Render specifically:** state the outcomes, show the rubric, show the alignment matrix, and name your measures (attainment + gain + engagement). That *is* the instructional-design work, you'll have receipts, not just a redesigned course.

## 9. A 60-second walkthrough framing (Render / AVC 100)

> "I used **backward design**. I started from the **learning outcomes**, what students should be able to do at the Apply level and above, then decided how I'd **measure** attainment with an **authentic assessment and a rubric**, and only then built the learning activities, so everything is **aligned**. I grounded the design in **adult learning theory**, relevance, autonomy, building on experience, and **UDL**, with accessibility built in. And I define success as **outcome attainment and learning gain**, tracked with rubric data and a pre/post, not just retention, though I watch engagement analytics as an early-warning signal too."

Deliver that and you sound exactly like what you are: an instructional designer doing the real work.

# Explainer video with AI: script to graphics to two voices (2026)

A practical, affordable path from a finished script to a cartoonish, consistent explainer video with two good AI voices. Built for a designer who wants the AI pieces spelled out.

## Workflow shape
Script → scene breakdown → illustrations → two voice tracks → assembly. Do it in that order. Images and voices can be produced in parallel once the script is broken down, then combined in one editor.

## 1. Script prep (do this before touching any AI tool)
Break the script into beats (one idea = one visual; a 3-minute explainer is 12 to 20 beats). Build a simple shot list, one row per beat:
- Beat number, Voice (A or B, mark every line), Narration text (exact words), Visual description, On-screen text (short), Duration estimate (~150 words/min).

Decide the two-voice logic up front and stay consistent: e.g., Voice A is the guide, Voice B is the curious student. Color-code A and B in the doc so you can see the rhythm. Rebalance if one voice carries almost everything.

Write each visual description like a brief to an illustrator who has never seen your show: subject and action, setting, mood, and a fixed style reminder. Keep recurring character descriptions identical word-for-word across scenes. That repetition is the trick.

## 2. Graphics (consistent cartoon)
Tools worth using in 2026:
- **Ideogram (Character + Style)** — top pick. Upload a reference image to lock a character's face, clothing, and art style across generations; best at text-in-image. Free tier plus a cheap paid plan (~$8-20/mo).
- **Midjourney V7 (Omni Reference, `--oref`, `--ow`)** — most polished cartoon look; subscription ~$10/mo; weaker at text-in-image.
- **A storybook tool (e.g., ToonyStory)** — built to hold one character across many panels; good if the general tools drift.

Pick one and commit. Prompting for consistency:
- Write a one-paragraph **style bible** and paste it into every prompt (flat vector cartoon, thick outlines, cel-shaded, 5-color palette with **exact hex codes**, minimal backgrounds).
- **Lock the palette by naming hex codes**, not "warm colors."
- **Anchor recurring characters with a reference image** (Ideogram Character / Midjourney `--oref`), not just words.
- Generate a **character sheet first** (neutral pose + a few expressions) as your reference and consistency check.
- Keep aspect ratio 16:9 and framing consistent.
- **Text in images:** either use Ideogram, or generate art with no text and add labels later in the editor (usually better, keeps text crisp and editable).
- **Diagrams/charts:** build in Canva/Figma/PowerPoint in your palette, export PNG. Do not fight an image generator for a clean flowchart.

Script-to-storyboard / animation (optional): Google Flow (Storyboard Studio, Veo), mStudio (script to animatic to final), Runway Gen-4.5, Google Veo 3.1 for motion. Recommendation for a first explainer: use consistent stills + light motion in the editor, not full AI animation. Save Veo/Runway for one or two hero shots.

## 3. Voices (two natural narrators)
Tools:
- **ElevenLabs** — quality leader for expressive English; easiest to audition two contrasting voices. Free tier ~10 min (testing only); $5 Starter adds commercial rights (~30 min); $22 Creator ~100k characters.
- **Google Cloud TTS (Chirp 3 HD)** — nearly as good, far cheaper (first 1M characters/month free). A little setup through Google Cloud.
- **Murf** — friendly all-in-one studio with pacing/emphasis and per-word timing; ~$20-30/mo.

Pick two that work together: contrast the timbre (not just gender), match register to roles, avoid two voices with the same pace/pitch, and audition them on the same two-line exchange.

Make them human, not robotic:
- **Feed one beat at a time**, not the whole script.
- Use pacing/emphasis controls: ElevenLabs stability/style sliders; Google/Murf accept SSML (`<break time="400ms"/>`, `<emphasis>`, rate/pitch). A few pauses are the biggest fix.
- Punctuate for breath; regenerate freely; export each voice as its own file named by scene (`S04_VoiceA.wav`).

## 4. Assembly
Editor: **CapCut (free)** is the practical pick (timeline, auto-captions, keyframes). DaVinci Resolve (free) for more control; Descript to edit by transcript.
1. Drop voice clips on the timeline in script order (A and B on separate tracks).
2. Place each illustration above its narration and stretch to match that line's length.
3. Add subtle motion (Ken Burns slow zoom/pan via two keyframes) so stills feel alive.
4. Add on-screen text/labels here in your palette fonts (not baked into images).
5. Add captions (auto, then proofread) — accessibility requirement.
6. Balance audio (consistent voice levels; optional music bed ~ -20 dB under voice; short fades).
7. Export 1080p, 16:9, MP4.

## Fastest path (the stack)
- Script prep: a spreadsheet. Free.
- Illustrations: **Ideogram** paid, Character lock + hex-locked style bible. ~$8-20.
- Voices: **ElevenLabs Starter** ($5) for two voices with commercial rights, generated line by line; or **Google Chirp 3 HD** (effectively free at this volume).
- Diagrams/labels: **Canva/Figma**. Free.
- Assembly: **CapCut**, free, with Ken Burns motion + auto-captions.

Rough total for one video: **~$15-25**, less on free tiers. Cancel monthly subs after the project if you are not making more.

**Two make-or-break moves:** lock your character with a reference image (not just words), and generate voices one beat at a time with deliberate pauses.
