# AVC 100 · Two-Tier Alignment

**Goal:** Assess the objectives I actually teach, and let the district competencies be satisfied as a consequence rather than as a separate chore.

**Audience:** Me, the faculty who copy this course, and any program review that asks how competencies are evidenced.

**Process:** Treat the five MCCCD competencies as course-level outcomes and my twelve Bloom's-verbed objectives as module-level objectives. Grade the module objectives. Map them upward once. The mapping does the compliance work.

---

## The model

This is not a workaround. **It is the standard instructional-design structure**, and both major quality rubrics assume it.

> **QM Standard 2.2:** *"The module/unit-level learning objectives describe outcomes that are measurable and **consistent with** the course-level objectives."*

**Consistent with. Not identical to.**

| Tier | What it is | Who owns it | Do I grade it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Course-level outcomes** | The five MCCCD competencies. Board-approved Feb 2023, dated, immovable. | The district | **No** |
| **Module-level objectives** | My twelve objectives. Bloom's-verbed, specific, observable, tied to real artifacts. | Me | **Yes** |

**Rubric criteria describe my objectives.** Every graded criterion in this course measures something I decided a student should be able to do. The competencies are satisfied because my objectives roll up to them, which is a one-time mapping rather than a grading burden.

**The practical consequence:** I never write a rubric criterion for "Operate a computer." I write one for *"Trace a reference shape with the Pen tool, producing closed paths with clean anchor points."* Competency 3 is evidenced by that. The district gets its evidence. The student gets a real objective.

## Bloom's distribution

| Level | Objectives |
|---|---|
| **Remember** | 2 |
| **Understand** | 7, 9 |
| **Apply** | 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11 |
| **Analyze** | 2, 6, 9 |
| **Evaluate** | 1, 3, 5, 12 |
| **Create** | 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 10, 12 |

**The distribution is right for a 100-level studio course.** Weighted toward Apply and Create, which is what a production course should be, with Analyze and Evaluate carried by the critique rather than by a quiz. **Remember appears once**, in a place where terminology genuinely has to be learned before anything else works.

**No objective sits at Remember alone.** That matters: a 100-level course that never got above Remember would be a vocabulary course wearing a studio course's name.

## How to build this in Canvas

Canvas supports **outcome groups**, which is exactly the two-tier structure.

1. **Outcomes** → **+ Group**. Create five groups, one per competency, named for the district text:
   *1 · Operate a computer* · *2 · Create digital art and design projects* · and so on.
2. Inside each group, **+ Outcome** for each of *my* objectives that rolls up to it. Objective 2 lives in two groups; Canvas allows an outcome in one group, so **file each objective under its primary competency** and note the secondary in the description.
3. On each assignment's rubric: **Find Outcome** → pick **my objective**, not the competency.
4. **The gradebook now reports mastery per objective, and per group.** Per objective for teaching. Per group for the program review.

**The competency reporting comes free.** That is the entire point of the two tiers.

---

## Why this is the better answer

**Grading the district's competencies directly would be bad teaching.** "Operate a computer" cannot be scored meaningfully, gives a student nothing to aim at, and would not tell me anything I want to know about their work.

**Grading my objectives is good teaching, and it satisfies the district anyway.** *"Trace a reference shape with the Pen tool, producing closed paths with clean anchor points"* is observable, gradeable, and something a student can act on. Competency 3 is evidenced by it whether or not anyone says the word "competency" out loud.

> **The competencies are a floor, not a curriculum.** The roll-up table proves the floor is met. The objectives are what the course is actually about.
