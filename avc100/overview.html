Intro to Digital Arts redesign

Case study · Learning design

A ground-up redesign of the program’s highest-enrollment, lowest-success course, built around student support, connection, and evidence that students actually learned something and want to keep going.

Goal

Design an entry course around the real reasons students struggle in it, then measure what students actually learned, rather than measuring how much content got added. The question is not how many bodies finished. It is whether a student who had never opened Illustrator walks out having made something real, knowing where to get help, and wanting to take the next class.

A ground-up redesign of AVC 100, Introduction to Digital Arts, the program’s highest-enrollment entry course and one of its lowest in student success. It is a one-credit, half-semester foundation course, rebuilt around student support, connection, and belonging, and packaged as a clean IMSCC file that imports straight into Canvas with accessibility built into every page.

Audience

First-time digital arts students, many of them new to college, who need an on-ramp that keeps them enrolled and moving through the high-risk early weeks.

Process

The course runs on a single project carried across three tools, Illustrator to Photoshop to After Effects, so students build one real piece instead of disconnected exercises. Every project closes with the same four-step critique on Discord: post early, give feedback to at least four classmates, log the feedback given, then submit the final board. Student-support touchpoints are woven into the first weeks as short lessons that walk students into advising, tutoring, disability services, and the campus basic-needs hub. The design draws on the persistence literature, Tinto on integration and Astin on involvement, and on Qualtrics data about why students leave: optional support is least used by the students who most need it, so it is built into the course rather than offered alongside it.

Tools

Built as an IMSCC package that imports into any Canvas shell, so another instructor can adopt it without rebuilding. Students work in Adobe Illustrator, Photoshop, and After Effects across the one carried project; critique runs on Discord with OBS and YouTube for screen-recorded feedback; and the support touchpoints and assessment live in Canvas.

Institutional learning outcomes

The student-support sequence carries the college’s Personal and Community Well-Being outcome, whose criterion asks that a student demonstrate how to access institutional resources. That is precisely what those touchpoints already require, so the rubric simply states what the work was for. It is worth doing here rather than in a capstone: an outcome assessed only inside the major produces data describing majors alone, and this course is full of students who will take one art class and never take another.

Evidence

The claim is not that this worked. The claim is that it is being measured, and measured against the thing the course is actually for.

An end-of-course survey went to the first section in its final week. It asks whether the software still feels intimidating, whether a student made something real in each program, whether they want to take another class that uses it, whether they knew where to go for help, whether they ever walked into a campus service because of something in the course, and whether the work fit inside the hours a one-credit course is allowed to ask for. A week-one baseline runs in the fall, so the next cohort can be measured against itself instead of against memory.

The first section’s responses point exactly where the redesign aimed. The two things it set out to move, the fear of the software and knowing where to find help, both shifted in the right direction between week one and the end of the term.

Several students had already contacted or looked into a campus service because of the course, which is exactly what the support sequence is built to produce. They rated instructor feedback and the Discord community the most helpful parts of the class. Ahead of any of that, a student in the first section published this in their own portfolio. They were not asked for feedback and there was no prompt. Quoted with their permission, anonymously.

I felt such a sense of community within the Discord and I enjoyed making art with my peers. I’ve always wanted to get into After Effects for video editing, but never quite knew how to start. All I had was Clipchamp and iMovie. This was a great way to get me started on that path, and I look forward to learning more.

I’m very eager to keep pursuing the Visual and Performing Arts in the future, even if I’m currently in Computer and Information Technology. Art and animation have been my true loves for a long time, and I’m happy that I’m getting closer and closer to becoming the animator and game developer that I want to be.

One student is not a finding. But unprompted, and to an audience that was never me, they name the three things this redesign was built to move. They felt connected to people. The software stopped being a wall. And they want to keep going, far enough to say out loud where they are headed.
