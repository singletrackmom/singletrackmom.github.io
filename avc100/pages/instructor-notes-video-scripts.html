Video Scripts

Instructor notes. Unpublished. Travels with the course copy.

How to record: OBS. OBS Studio is the department standard, it is free, and it is what we all use. It records your screen and your face at once, which is what a software demo needs.

New to OBS? Start with Casey’s setup video. [LINK TO CASEY’S OBS SETUP VIDEO] It walks through the whole configuration and it will save you an hour of guessing at bitrates.

Then upload to Canvas Studio. Record in OBS, upload the MP4 to Studio, embed it in the page. Studio is where the captions live, so the video has to end up there.

Say lines are narration and you should paraphrase freely. Do lines are what you click. Do not aim for polish: a three-minute video with a stumble in it beats a perfect one you never make. Mistakes you fix on camera are good teaching , students need to see that the Pen tool fights everybody.

Then caption them. Studio → Captions → Request → and then edit the result. Auto-captions run 70 to 90 percent accurate and they fail hardest on exactly the words you are teaching. Keyframe becomes key frame. Bézier becomes anything at all.

Fifteen videos, and only four are long. Sketch steps and exercises run 3 to 5 minutes. Build steps run 8 to 9. Refine is one script, used three times , the process is identical for the poster, the postcard, and the animation, and recording it once is the point of making all three projects the same shape.

3 · How critique works here  ·  3 to 4 min  ·  Getting Started

Why it matters: Critique is a fifth of the grade and it is where the design vocabulary actually gets used. Students who do not understand it write “looks good!” four times and learn nothing.

[Screen: a Miro board with thumbnails, then Discord.]

Every project step ends the same way. You post your work to Discord. You give feedback to at least four classmates. You put the feedback you received onto your Miro board. Then you submit.

“Looks good” is not feedback. It costs nothing to write and it gives the other person nothing to do. Here is what feedback looks like.

Say what you see, before you say what you think. “My eye goes to the mountain first, then the type, then the bird.” That is an observation. It is not a judgment, and the person cannot argue with it, because it is a fact about your eye.

Then name the element doing it. “The mountain is the biggest shape and it is the only warm colour, so it wins.” Now you have used scale and colour, and you have explained an effect with a cause.

Then ask one question. “Did you want me to look at the bird?” If the answer is yes, they have a problem to solve, and you found it for them.

That is the whole method. Observe, name, ask. Ninety seconds per person, and it is worth more than an hour of me talking.

One rule about receiving it. You do not have to take every note. You do have to be able to say why you did not. In the Refine step you tell me what you changed and what you left alone, and the second half of that is just as good an answer as the first.

And one practical thing that costs people points every single term. If your classmates cannot see your work, they cannot critique it. Which is the next video.

4 · Photographing your thumbnails  ·  2 min  ·  Poster, Step 1

Why it matters: This is the Sketches captured for critique criterion, worth 2 points on every Step 1. It is Competency 4, input devices. It is also the single most common reason critique fails.

[Screen: two photos side by side. One dark and skewed. One clean. Then a phone, a window, a sheet of paper.]

This is a real thumbnail sheet, photographed badly. [Show it.] Dark, crooked, half of it in shadow. I cannot see the linework. Neither can the four classmates who are supposed to critique it.

Here is the same sheet, thirty seconds of effort later. [Show it.]

Daylight, not overhead light. Put the paper near a window. Overhead light puts your own shadow on the page.

Square to the page. Phone flat above the paper, parallel to it. If the rectangle looks like a trapezoid, move.

Fill the frame. Get close. Do not photograph your desk.

Tap to focus. Then look at it at full size before you post. If you cannot read your own linework at 100 percent, nobody else can either.

A scanner works too, and the Library has one. Your phone is fine.

This is worth two points on every Step 1, and I am not being fussy. Capturing physical work so a digital audience can read it is one of the five things this course is officially required to teach you, and it is the part of the job nobody warns you about until you get it wrong in front of a client.

6 · Lesson and Exercise: the Pen tool  ·  6 to 8 min

Why it matters: Objective: Define the core Illustrator terms. Trace a reference shape with the Pen tool, producing closed paths with clean anchor points.

[Screen capture. New document, a placed reference photo on a locked layer.]

Five words first, because the rest of this makes no sense without them.

The artboard is the page. The path is the line you draw. An anchor point is a point on that path where it changes direction. The stroke is the colour of the line. The fill is the colour inside it. [Show each on screen as you say it.]

That is the whole vocabulary. Now the tool.

The Pen tool is P. Every click makes an anchor point, and Illustrator connects them with a path.

Click, click, click. [Trace a straight-edged shape.] Three clicks, three anchor points, straight lines between them. To close the shape, come back to the first anchor point and watch for the little circle beside the cursor. That circle means “this will close the path.” Click it. A closed path can hold a fill. An open one cannot.

Now curves, and this is the part everyone hates for about a week.

Instead of clicking, click and drag. [Do it slowly.] Two handles come out of the anchor point, and those handles control the curve. Drag in the direction the line is already travelling as it leaves the point. Not toward where you want it to end up.

Watch what happens if I drag too far. [Overshoot.] The curve bulges. Shorter handles, gentler curve.

Here is the rule that will save you an hour: use fewer anchor points than you think you need. Beginners click twenty times around a circle. A circle needs four. Put an anchor point where the curve changes direction, and nowhere else. [Show a shape traced with too many points, then the same shape with four. The four-point version is visibly smoother.]

To fix a point, hold Command , Control on Windows. It temporarily becomes the Direct Selection tool. Grab the anchor point, move it. Grab a handle, reshape the curve. Let go and you are back in the Pen.

To break a curve into a corner, hold Option , Alt on Windows , and click the anchor point. One handle retracts. That is how a shape curves in and then turns sharp.

Your exercise: trace the reference shape. Closed path. Clean anchor points. Fewer points than you think. Nobody’s first one looks good. Mine did not. Do it three times and something clicks.

Why it matters: Objective: Construct an 18 x 24 vector travel poster, applying a defined palette, limited typography, and a clear focal hierarchy. Simplify source imagery into clean vector shapes.

  • Intro (20s) Say: “Using the Step 1 feedback, we build the 18 by 24 poster. Keep it simple. A few bold elements you can draw as vector art.”

Photoshop  ·  Photographic Postcard

9 · Lesson and Exercise: selecting a subject  ·  6 to 8 min

Why it matters: Objective: Isolate three subjects from their backgrounds using Select Subject and the Object Selection tool, and refine the edges.

[Screen capture. A photo of an animal against a busy background.]

You need to get this animal out of this background and onto your postcard. Ten years ago that took an hour. Now it takes about four minutes, and the four minutes are all in the edges.

Start with Select Subject. Top toolbar, or Select → Subject. [Click it.] Photoshop decides what the subject is and selects it. It is right about eighty percent of the time, and wrong in interesting places.

Look at where it failed. [Zoom in.] It caught the body and lost the legs, because the legs are the same colour as the grass behind them. That is the pattern. It fails where contrast fails.

Fix it with the Object Selection tool , W. Hold Shift and drag a box around what it missed: it adds. Hold Option , Alt on Windows , and drag around what it wrongly included: it subtracts. [Do both.]

Now the part that separates a good cutout from an obvious one. Select and Mask. [Click it in the top toolbar.]

Set View to On Black or On White, whichever fights your subject. Now you can see the fringe , that halo of leftover background stuck to the edge. That halo is what makes a cutout look cut out.

Three sliders do almost all the work. Smooth takes the jagged staircase off the edge , a little, not a lot, or your animal turns into a balloon. Feather softens the edge a pixel or two so it does not look razored. Shift Edge, pulled slightly negative, pulls the selection inward and eats the halo. [Show each exaggerated, then dialled back.]

If your subject has fur, use the Refine Edge Brush and paint along the fuzzy edge. [Do it.] Photoshop reconsiders just that area and keeps the strands. This is the best thing in the program.

Output To: Layer Mask. Not Selection. Not New Layer. Layer Mask. [Choose it. OK.]

Here is why that matters more than anything else in this video. A layer mask hides the background. It does not delete it. [Paint on the mask with black and white to show it coming back.] Black conceals, white reveals. You can always change your mind. Delete the background instead and you cannot.

Your exercise: three subjects, three backgrounds, three layer masks. Keep the file.

After Effects  ·  Social Media Animation

12 · Lesson and Exercise: two keyframes  ·  5 to 7 min

Why it matters: Objective: Animate a single layer into frame using two Position keyframes. Explain in your own words what a keyframe does. Terminology checked against Adobe’s documentation.

One idea. If you get this one idea, the rest is buttons.

A keyframe records where something is at a moment in time. Put down two , here it is at zero seconds, here it is at one second , and the software draws every frame in between. That is animation. You describe two moments and the computer invents the rest.

Import your postcard PSD. File → Import → File. And here is the important part: Import Kind: Composition to Retain Layer Sizes. [Choose it.] That brings the PSD in as a composition with every layer separate. Choose “Footage” and it flattens into one image and you cannot animate anything. This is where people lose an evening.

[Open the composition. Show the layers.] Background. Animal. Type. All separate. That is why the layer mask mattered.

Select the animal layer and press P. P for Position. One property. That is the only one we are touching today.

Playhead to zero. Click the stopwatch beside Position. [Click it.] That stopwatch is the whole thing. Turning it on says: I am going to animate this property, and I want you to record where it is right now. A keyframe appears. A little diamond.

Now drag the animal off the side of the frame. The keyframe at zero now says: at zero seconds, the animal is off-screen.

Playhead to one second. Drag the animal to where it should land. A second keyframe appears automatically , you did not click the stopwatch again. Once it is on, moving the layer at a new time makes a new keyframe.

Spacebar. [Play.] The animal slides in. You animated something. Two keyframes, one property.

Watch it again though. [Play.] Same speed the whole way, then it stops dead. Nothing in the physical world does that. It looks robotic because it is.

Select both keyframes. Right-click → Keyframe Assistant → Easy Ease. Or press F9. [The diamonds become hourglasses.]

Play it now. It accelerates in and decelerates to a stop. Same two keyframes. Same one second. It just looks like it has weight. That is the difference between animation that looks amateur and animation that does not, and it is one keystroke.

Your exercise: one layer, two Position keyframes, Easy Ease, export an H.264 MP4.

And in your reflection, answer this plainly: what does a keyframe actually do? If you can say it in your own words, you understand animation. Everything else in After Effects is that same idea with more properties.

14 · Animation, Step 2: Build  ·  8 min

Why it matters: Objective: Import a layered PSD as a Composition. Animate two layers with paired Position keyframes, matching the storyboard’s timing. Export an H.264 MP4.

  • Intro (20s) Say: “Everything you need is already made. You are animating the postcard you already built.”
  • Import (1.5 min) Do: File → Import → File → your PSD → Import Kind: Composition to Retain Layer Sizes. Say: “If your layers are not separate here, your PSD was flattened. Go back and fix the postcard , you cannot animate a flat image.”
  • Comp settings (1 min) Do: Check the composition size and duration. Vertical for social. A few seconds.
  • Animate layer one (2 min) Do: Select the animal. P. Playhead to 0. Stopwatch. Drag off-frame. Playhead to 1 second. Drag into place. F9.
  • Animate layer two (1.5 min) Do: Select the type. P. Same two keyframes, offset to the timing on your storyboard. F9. Say: “Stagger them. Everything arriving at once looks like a mistake.”

One script, three uses

15 · Step 3: Refine  ·  5 min  ·  Poster, Postcard, and Animation

Why it matters: Objective: Revise in response to at least two specific peer critiques. Articulate what changed and why, naming the design principle involved. Prepare the file for output. Record this once. The process is identical in all three projects, and that is the entire reason the three projects now have the same shape.

[Screen: a Miro board with critique comments on it, beside the v2 file.]

This step is not “make it prettier.” It is a specific job, and it has two halves.

Half one: respond to the critique

Open your board and read the feedback you were given. Pick at least two specific notes and act on them. Not “polished it up.” Two notes, from two people, with names on them.

Then tell me what you changed and why, and name the principle. “Three people said their eye went to the type before the mountain. The type was the only warm colour, so it was winning. I cooled the type down and warmed the mountain. That is contrast doing the work I wanted hierarchy to do.”

That paragraph is worth more points than the change itself, because it is the thing that proves you understood why it was a problem instead of just doing what you were told.

And you are allowed to disagree. If two people told you to move something and you think they are wrong, keep it and say why. “I left the bird where it is. It is supposed to be a surprise you find second, not first.” That is a full-credit answer. A designer who takes every note has no point of view.

Half two: prepare the file for output

This is the part nobody does and it is the part a client notices. Your file has to actually work in the place it is going.

Poster: export a PDF at 18 by 24, at print resolution, in the right colour mode. Would this print? Open the PDF and look at it at 100 percent. If the edges are soft, your source was too small.

Postcard: your JPG at delivery size, and the PSD with layers still separate. The animation project needs it.

Animation: H.264 MP4. Play it back before you submit it. Watch the whole thing. Every term, somebody submits a five-second video that is four seconds of black because the work area was never trimmed.

“It exported” is not the same as “it works.” Open the file you are about to hand in, and look at it, as if you were the person receiving it. That is a professional habit and it takes ninety seconds.

Then submit

Final file on the Miro board, alongside the critique that produced it and your paragraph about what you changed. Post to Discord, critique four classmates, submit the board.

And keep it. All three of these go into your Behance portfolio in the last week, and the portfolio is the thing you can actually show someone.

Launch It  ·  Behance Portfolio

16 · Publishing to Behance  ·  7 min  ·  Launch It

Objective: Curate three finished projects into a single published Behance project, sequencing them strongest work first. Compose a project description naming the tools used and one design decision you would defend.

Why Behance, and not a folder on your laptop

[Screen: a working designer’s Behance profile. Not yours. Someone employed.]

This is the first thing in this course that a stranger will see. Everything else you made this term, you made for me. This one is not for me.

Behance is owned by Adobe, it is where design employers actually look, and you already have an account, because you have an Adobe ID from the college. No sign-up. No hosting bill. No domain to buy. You are already on it. You just have not put anything there.

And the honest reason it matters more than the grade: in eight weeks you have made three finished pieces with one consistent identity across print, photo, and motion. That is a small portfolio and it is a real one. Most people in your position have a folder of unfinished files and nothing to show anybody.

First: fix your URL. Before anything else.

Nobody tells you this, and it is the part you cannot easily undo.

Behance hands you a URL that looks like behance.net/michelle_blomberg_a4f9c2. [Show one.] That is unusable. You cannot say it out loud. You cannot put it on a resume. You cannot read it to someone at a career fair without spelling it.

You want this: behance.net/michelleblomberg. First name, last name. No underscores, no numbers, no dots. Something you can say in a sentence.

[Screen: click through it slowly.] Your avatar, top right → Settings → Account. There is a username field, and the URL is built from it. Change it. Do it now, with me, in this video.

If firstnamelastname is taken , and it might be , go in this order. Add your middle initial. Then add a word for what you do. michelleblomberg, then michellejblomberg, then michelleblombergdesign.

Do not add a number. A number tells a stranger you were the second-best person with your name, and it is the one thing that looks amateur before they have seen a single image.

Then use that same handle everywhere else. LinkedIn, Instagram, wherever you post work. One name, so a person who finds you in one place can find you in the others. That is what a professional identity actually is, and today it costs you five minutes.

Second: a project is not an image

This is the mistake everybody makes. They upload three JPGs as three separate posts and it looks like a camera roll.

On Behance a project is a case study. One project, containing all three of your pieces, in an order you chose, with words around them. [Show a good one. Then show a camera roll.]

Create → Project. Drag your images in.

Strongest work first. Not chronological. Not the order you made them in. Whatever you are proudest of goes at the top, because most people look at the first image and nothing else. Curating is a design decision and it is graded.

Third: the cover image is the whole thing

The cover is the only thing most people will ever see , it is the thumbnail in the feed, in search, and in the grid on your profile.

Behance wants roughly 808 by 632 pixels. Crop it yourself, deliberately. Do not let it auto-crop your poster and cut the headline in half. [Show that happening. It is funny, and it is a real problem.]

Pick the image that reads at the size of a postage stamp. If you cannot tell what it is when it is tiny, it is the wrong cover, no matter how much you like it.

Fourth: the description. This is the graded part.

Every project has a description field and almost nobody uses it. Which is exactly why using it well is worth doing.

Not this: “Travel poster for Zion National Park. Made in Illustrator.” That tells a stranger nothing they could not already see.

This:

“A travel poster, postcard, and animation for Zion, carried by one typeface and one five-colour palette across print, photo, and motion. I built the palette warm and dusty so it feels like the place rather than a brochure about the place. In critique, three people said their eye went to the type before the cliff, so I cooled the type and warmed the rock. The cliff wins now, which is what I wanted.”

Look at what that does. It says what you made. It names the tools. It defends one decision, and it admits one thing you changed and why.

A stranger reading it learns that you can take feedback and articulate a choice , which is the thing employers are screening for and cannot see in a JPG.

That is the assignment. Name the tools. Defend one decision. Four sentences will do it.

And fill in the Tools Used field , Illustrator, Photoshop, After Effects. Behance uses it for search, and it is three clicks.

Last: publish it

Publish, not Save Draft. [Show the difference. It catches somebody every single term.] A draft is invisible. If your link shows a 404 to everyone but you, you saved a draft.

Then open your own URL in a private window. That is what a stranger sees. Look at it as a stranger before you submit it. Thirty seconds, and it is the difference between a portfolio and a folder.

Submit the URL to Canvas. And then keep it. You are going to add to this for the next two years, and the students who keep it are the ones with something to show when it counts.
