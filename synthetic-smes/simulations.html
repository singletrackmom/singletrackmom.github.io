Authentic assessment by simulation

Case study · Synthetic SMEs

Overview The case The build skill Course overview The course Instructor prep

The summative architecture of the course, and the strand that confronts the academic-integrity problem most directly. Every graded task is a professional simulation whose object of assessment is the student’s live reasoning over a private artifact, a construction that a generative model cannot complete on the student’s behalf.

In one line

Eight simulations, each replicating a genuine professional task in data science, scored on process and defended reasoning rather than on a producible final answer, culminating in a fifteen-week consulting engagement and a live stakeholder defense.

Why simulation, and why it resists AI

A written or selected-response examination exists to certify that a student can perform the work of the discipline. A remote student with a text field and a generative model can now satisfy such an instrument without demonstrating the underlying competence, so it no longer licenses the inference it was built to license. The durable remedy is not intensified proctoring but a change in the construct being assessed. Every summative task in this course is a simulation whose graded object is live reasoning about a private artifact, three conditions a model cannot supply on a student’s behalf:

  • A hidden ground truth. Core datasets are generated from a data-generating process known only to the instructor and seeded per student, so no answer key exists in the public corpus and the grade rests partly on how closely a method recovers the true parameters or the true number of clusters.
  • Injected incidents. Leakage, bias, missingness, and a mid-project data-drift event are planted, requiring the student to detect, diagnose, and adapt, the actual labor of a data scientist rather than a producible text.
  • A live defense. Each major deliverable is defended in a simulated stakeholder meeting in which a role-played executive, compliance officer, and engineer pose unscripted, decision-relevant questions.

Reframed in this way, generative AI ceases to be the vector of compromise and becomes the instrument of instruction: the simulated client that reacts, the reviewer that pushes back, the scenario that adapts to the learner. It is a defensible, and overdue, use of AI in a technical course.

The eight simulations

Each names a genuine professional task, the artifact the student produces, and the reason it cannot be fabricated. The full design, with materials, rubrics, and the week-by-week schedule, is in the course.

  • The Provenance Incident. Assemble data from four sources including a live, rate-limited API whose schema changes mid-window; deliver a reproducible pipeline and a datasheet-style provenance log. The API is private and moving, so the lineage only the student’s own run produced is the evidence.
  • Recover the Signal. A personalized messy dataset corrupted from a clean process the instructor holds privately; cleaning is scored numerically by how close the recovered statistics come to the hidden truth.
  • The Executive Brief. A non-technical VP sends a real decision and five minutes; the student delivers a jargon-free brief and defends it live against unscripted follow-ups about this dataset.
  • The Private-Leaderboard Competition. A hosted competition with a hidden test set; the grade weights the private score, the public-private overfitting gap, and a defended model-selection rationale.
  • The Data-Incident Autopsy. A “production-ready” model with planted defects, target leakage, contamination, a wrong metric, a collapsing subgroup, that the student must catch, correct, and sign off or reject.
  • How Many Segments, Really? A dataset from a known mixture model; scored partly on agreement with the private labels and partly on the rigor of the cluster-count argument.
  • The Model-Governance Review Board. A role-played NIST AI RMF review of a model with disparate impact; the student completes a governance dossier and defends an approve, reject, or condition vote.
  • The RiverCity Consulting Engagement. The capstone: a fifteen-week client relationship with a hidden ground truth and a mid-project data-drift event that breaks the earlier models, ending in a live stakeholder defense.

Grounding

The design rests on the authentic-assessment literature and on validated practice, not on preference.

  • Wiggins, G. (1990, 1998). The case for authentic assessment; Educative Assessment. ERIC ED328611The founding argument: a true test measures performance on the real criterion task, with criteria known in advance and room to iterate.
  • Gulikers, J., Bastiaens, T. & Kirschner, P. (2004). A five-dimensional framework for authentic assessment. ETR&D. SpringerAuthenticity as a continuum across task, physical and social context, result, and criteria, defined by the student’s perceived resemblance to real practice. The course is designed against all five.
  • Ashford-Rowe, K., Herrington, J. & Brown, C. (2014). Establishing the critical elements that determine authentic assessment. Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education. RepositoryEight design criteria, including transfer, metacognition, real fidelity, and a defended product, used here as a build checklist.
  • Dawson, P. (2021). Defending Assessment Security in a Digital World. Routledge. PublisherReframes integrity as a validity problem: whether an assessment still licenses a valid inference about competence. That is exactly the case for a private-ground-truth simulation.
  • Shute, V. & Ventura, M. (2013). Stealth Assessment. MIT Press (open access). MIT PressThe evidence-centered-design basis for inferring competence from what a student does inside a simulation.
  • Working examples of assessment as performed work, fully online: Forage employer-designed job simulations, Georgia Tech OMSCS at graduate scale, and PhET browser-based simulations from CU Boulder.Proof that rigorous, assessed, simulated work already runs entirely in a browser, the constraint fully online programs live under.
