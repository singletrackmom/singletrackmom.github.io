The case for synthetic SMEs

Case study · Synthetic SMEs

Overview The case The build skill Course overview The course Instructor prep

The claim this project sets out to prove: a rigorous online course can be built by AI agents cast as subject-matter experts, orchestrated by a skill that enforces the learning science on every pass, and finished by the human faculty member who will teach it. It is not a template re-dressed with new content. Every decision in the course traces to a named standard or framework, because those standards are written into the skill itself.

What the project sets out to prove

Generative AI can perform the tireless first-draft labor of course construction; the open question is whether the result is rigorous or merely rapid. The demonstration is therefore built around a methodological control. Its subject, a graduate discipline outside the designer’s own domain expertise, data science, holds content knowledge constant, so that the quality of the finished course is attributable to the method and its embedded standards rather than to familiarity with the subject. It is the more demanding test of the approach, and the more defensible one.

The answer the project argues for is yes, on one condition: the rigor cannot be improvised. It has to be built into the process so it happens every time, on every module, whether or not anyone remembers to check. That is what the skill is for.

The skill is where the rigor lives

The most important part of this project is not the data-science course. It is the skill that produced it. The skill orchestrates AI agents, a subject-matter expert, an instructional designer, and a synthetic student modeled on the incoming learner, and holds their work to a fixed checklist of online-quality standards: measurable objectives, outcome alignment, authentic assessment, AI resistance, seat time, Universal Design for Learning, accessibility, interaction, and feedback. The agents draft, the skill enforces, a human finishes. Point the skill at a different subject and the same standards travel with it. The course is the evidence; the skill is the claim.

Grounded, not improvised

Every structural decision in the course rests on an established framework, and each one is encoded in the skill so it is applied the same way every time:

  • Backward design. Outcomes first, then evidence, then instruction, so nothing is included that no outcome claims (Wiggins & McTighe, Understanding by Design).
  • Constructive alignment. Outcome, activity, and assessment are made to cohere, not merely coexist (Biggs).
  • OSCQR. The SUNY open online-course-quality rubric, fifty standards governing structure, clarity, and accessibility.
  • Regular and Substantive Interaction. The federal requirement (34 CFR 600.2) for weekly, instructor-initiated presence, designed in from week one rather than bolted on.
  • Universal Design for Learning. Multiple means of engagement, representation, and expression (CAST), kept distinct from accessibility.
  • Authentic assessment. The grade rests on performance of the real task, not recall (the literature below).

Authentic assessment: one strand, and how it resists AI

Authentic assessment is one of those frameworks, not the whole story, but it is the one that answers the AI-integrity worry head on. A written or multiple-choice exam exists to certify that a student can do the work; a remote student with a chatbot can now pass one without demonstrating anything. The durable fix is not tighter policing, it is changing what we ask students to do. Every summative task in the course is a simulation whose graded object is live reasoning about a private artifact, three things a model cannot supply on a student’s behalf:

  • A hidden ground truth. Core datasets come from a data-generating process known only to the instructor and seeded per student, so the answer key is not on the web.
  • Injected incidents. Leakage, bias, missingness, and a mid-project data-drift event are planted, so the student must detect, diagnose, and adapt.
  • A live defense. Each major deliverable is defended in a simulated stakeholder meeting where a role-played executive, compliance officer, and engineer ask unscripted questions.

Turned this way, AI stops being the cheating vector and becomes the engine: the simulated client that reacts, the reviewer that pushes back. The full set of eight simulations is in the course. The approach also extends established work on personal learning environments, the principle that learning is strongest when organized around the individual learner; personalized, performance-based assessment carries that principle into how competence is measured.

The human stays accountable

The skill never ships a course a human has not reviewed, and that guardrail is the whole point of the case. The synthetic SMEs draft, a named faculty subject-matter expert confirms the technical content, and the person who will teach the course owns the result. The failure mode it exists to avoid is the one where faculty content is repackaged without consent or review; here the human is never taken out of the loop.

Grounding

The method rests on documented practice in agentic instructional design and on established learning science, not on preference. Authentic assessment is one building block within it, not the foundation.
