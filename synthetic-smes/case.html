The case for synthetic SMEs

Learning design · Course case study

Where online assessment is going: the exam replaced by an authentic task the student performs, personalized to them, with the proof of competence in the performance itself. To make the argument concrete, I designed and built a complete graduate course as the demonstration, a data-science course assembled from open educational resources and assessed entirely through professional simulations a student cannot fake.

The demonstration

Advanced Data Science, a 15-week, 3-credit graduate course, engineered backward from the published outcomes of a real graduate course at a leading STEM university, built from vetted open and interactive resources, designed to the OSCQR quality standard and to Regular and Substantive Interaction, and assessed through eight professional simulations judged like real client work, ending in a live stakeholder defense.

The traditional final exam assumes a room, a clock, and a single correct answer. Fully online programs have none of the first two, and generative AI has dissolved the third. So the question is not how to defend the old exam, it is what replaces it: an authentic professional problem, personalized to the learner, where the grade rests on the process and the reasoning rather than a final answer a model could hand back.

This continues my own scholarship rather than starting a new interest. My master’s research was on personal learning environments, the principle that learning is strongest when it is organized around the individual learner rather than a one-size-fits-all container. Personalized, performance-based assessment is that principle carried into how we measure competence: if the learning is built around the person, the proof of it should be too.

How the course was built

Outcomes first, and every decision traceable to them. I started from the published outcomes of the source course and worked backward (Wiggins & McTighe), using constructive alignment (Biggs) so that each reading, interactive, and assessment serves a stated outcome and nothing is included that no outcome claims. Universal Design for Learning governs access, most deliverables accept a written or a recorded form, and the build meets WCAG 2.1 AA. The two online-specific standards are designed in from the first draft, not bolted on: the OSCQR rubric for course quality, and Regular and Substantive Interaction for the weekly, instructor-initiated presence federal rules require. Materials are open educational resources, each one verified live and sorted by license into what may be adapted and what may only be linked. It is a course engineered backward from what a graduate must be able to do, which is the opposite of changing the names and scenery on a template.

Why simulation, and why it resists AI

A multiple-choice or written exam exists to certify that a student can do the work. A remote student with a text box and a chatbot can now pass one without demonstrating anything, so it no longer certifies. The durable answer is not to police AI harder; it is to change what we ask students to do. Every summative task in this course is a simulation whose graded object is live reasoning about a private artifact, three things a model cannot supply on a student’s behalf:

  • A hidden ground truth. Core datasets are generated from a data-generating process known only to the instructor and seeded per student, so the answer key is not on the web and the grade rests partly on how well a method recovers the true parameters or the true number of clusters.
  • Injected incidents. Leakage, bias, missingness, and a mid-project data-drift event are planted, so the student must detect, diagnose, and adapt, the actual work of a data scientist rather than a producible text.
  • A live defense. Each major deliverable is defended in a simulated stakeholder meeting where a role-played executive, compliance officer, and engineer ask unscripted, decision-relevant questions.

Turned this way, AI stops being the cheating vector and becomes the engine: the simulated client that reacts, the reviewer that pushes back, the scenario that adapts. That is a defensible, and overdue, use of AI in a technical course.

The eight simulations

Each names a real professional task, what the student produces, and why it cannot be faked. The full design, with materials, rubrics, and the week-by-week schedule, is in the course case study.

  • The Provenance Incident. Assemble data from four sources including a live, rate-limited API whose schema changes mid-window; deliver a reproducible pipeline and a datasheet-style provenance log. The API is private and moving, so the lineage only the student’s own run produced is the evidence.
  • Recover the Signal. A personalized messy dataset corrupted from a clean process the instructor holds privately; cleaning is scored numerically by how close the recovered statistics come to the hidden truth.
  • The Executive Brief. A non-technical VP sends a real decision and five minutes; the student delivers a jargon-free brief and defends it live against unscripted follow-ups about this dataset.
  • The Private-Leaderboard Competition. A hosted competition with a hidden test set; the grade weights the private score, the public-private overfitting gap, and a defended model-selection rationale.
  • The Data-Incident Autopsy. A “production-ready” model with planted defects, target leakage, contamination, a wrong metric, a collapsing subgroup, that the student must catch, correct, and sign off or reject.
  • How Many Segments, Really? A dataset from a known mixture model; scored partly on agreement with the private labels and partly on the rigor of the cluster-count argument.
  • The Model-Governance Review Board. A role-played NIST AI RMF review of a model with disparate impact; the student completes a governance dossier and defends an approve, reject, or condition vote.
  • The RiverCity Consulting Engagement. The capstone: a 15-week client relationship with a hidden ground truth and a mid-project data-drift event that breaks the earlier models, ending in a live stakeholder defense.

Grounding

The design rests on the authentic-assessment literature and on validated practice, not on preference.

  • Wiggins, G. (1990, 1998). The case for authentic assessment; Educative Assessment. ERIC ED328611The founding argument: a true test measures performance on the real criterion task, with criteria known in advance and room to iterate.
  • Gulikers, J., Bastiaens, T. & Kirschner, P. (2004). A five-dimensional framework for authentic assessment. ETR&D. SpringerAuthenticity as a continuum across task, physical and social context, result, and criteria, defined by the student’s perceived resemblance to real practice. The course is designed against all five.
  • Ashford-Rowe, K., Herrington, J. & Brown, C. (2014). Establishing the critical elements that determine authentic assessment. Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education. RepositoryEight design criteria, including transfer, metacognition, real fidelity, and a defended product, used here as a build checklist.
  • Dawson, P. (2021). Defending Assessment Security in a Digital World. Routledge. PublisherReframes integrity as a validity problem: whether an assessment still licenses a valid inference about competence. That is exactly the case for a private-ground-truth simulation.
  • Shute, V. & Ventura, M. (2013). Stealth Assessment. MIT Press (open access). MIT PressThe evidence-centered-design basis for inferring competence from what a student does inside a simulation.
  • Working examples of assessment as performed work, fully online: Forage employer-designed job simulations, Georgia Tech OMSCS at graduate scale, and PhET browser-based simulations from CU Boulder.Proof that rigorous, assessed, simulated work already runs entirely in a browser, the constraint fully online programs live under.
