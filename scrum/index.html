1. Empiricism, lean thinking, and the Scrum values

Scrum is founded on empiricism (knowledge comes from experience and decisions are based on what is observed) and lean thinking (reduce waste, focus on the essentials).

The three pillars of empiricism

  • Transparency: the work and process are visible to those doing and receiving the work. Low transparency leads to decisions that decrease value and increase risk.
  • Inspection: Scrum artifacts and progress are inspected frequently to detect problems. Inspection enables adaptation.
  • Adaptation: if something deviates outside acceptable limits, adjust as soon as possible.
Remember the chain: the five events exist so the team can inspect and adapt the artifacts. Each artifact has a commitment that creates the transparency needed to inspect against it.

The five Scrum values

CommitmentFocusOpennessRespectCourage

The values give direction to the team’s work, actions, and behavior. When the values are embodied, the pillars come to life and trust is built.

2. The Scrum Team

One team of 10 or fewer people, with no sub-teams and no hierarchies. It is a cohesive unit focused on one objective at a time, the Product Goal. The Scrum Team is cross-functional (has all skills needed) and self-managing (it decides who does what, when, and how).

The whole Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every Sprint. There are three specific accountabilities:

AccountabilityAccountable for
Product OwnerMaximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team. Manages the Product Backlog.
Scrum MasterEstablishing Scrum and the team’s effectiveness. A true leader who serves the team and organization.
DevelopersCreating a usable Increment each Sprint. Owns the Sprint Backlog, instills quality via the Definition of Done, and adapts the plan daily.
Trap: they are accountabilities not job titles or sub-teams. A Product Owner or Scrum Master can also be a Developer. The whole team, not just Developers, is accountable for the Increment.

3. The Product Owner (study this hardest)

The Product Owner is one person, not a committee accountable for maximizing value. They represent the needs of many stakeholders in the Product Backlog. For Product Owners to succeed, the whole organization must respect their decisions.

Product Backlog management (the PO does or delegates, but stays accountable)

  • Developing and explicitly communicating the Product Goal.
  • Creating and clearly communicating Product Backlog items.
  • Ordering Product Backlog items.
  • Ensuring the Product Backlog is transparent, visible, and understood.
Delegation rule: the PO may have the Developers or others do backlog work, but the Product Owner remains accountable. Only the Product Owner can change the order. Others can only change priority by convincing the Product Owner.

Authority and the single source

  • The PO’s decisions are visible in the content and ordering of the Product Backlog and in the inspectable Increment at the Sprint Review.
  • No one tells the Developers to work from a different set of requirements, and the Developers are not allowed to act on what anyone else says.
  • One Product Owner per product. If multiple Scrum Teams work on one product, they share one Product Backlog, one Product Goal, and one Product Owner.
  • Only the Product Owner has the authority to cancel a Sprint.

4. The events

The Sprint is the container for all other events. Each event is a formal opportunity to inspect and adapt. All events are time-boxed. Skipping events reduces transparency and is a lost chance to inspect and adapt.

The Sprint

  • Fixed length of one month or less a new one starts immediately after the previous ends.
  • No changes that endanger the Sprint Goal; quality does not decrease; scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the PO as more is learned.
  • Only the Product Owner can cancel a Sprint (if the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete).
EventTime-box (1-month Sprint)Purpose / commitment
Sprint PlanningMax 8 hoursWhy (Sprint Goal), What (items selected), How (plan). Whole team plans; PO ensures attendees discuss value and the Product Goal.
Daily Scrum15 minutesFor the Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the Sprint Backlog. PO and SM attend only if they are working as Developers.
Sprint ReviewMax 4 hoursInspect the outcome with stakeholders and determine future adaptations. A working session, not just a demo. The Product Backlog may be adjusted.
Sprint RetrospectiveMax 3 hoursThe team inspects how the last Sprint went (individuals, interactions, process, tools, Definition of Done) and plans improvements. Closes the Sprint.
Trap: the Daily Scrum is not a status report to the PO or SM, and changing its time or place is fine as long as it is daily, same time and place, 15 minutes, owned by the Developers.

5. Artifacts and their commitments

Each artifact contains a commitment to ensure transparency and focus, something to measure progress against.

ArtifactCommitmentNotes
Product BacklogProduct GoalEmergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product. The single source of work for the team. The Product Goal is the long-term objective; the team fulfills (or abandons) one before taking the next.
Sprint BacklogSprint GoalThe Sprint Goal (why) + items selected (what) + plan to deliver (how). By and for the Developers. Highly visible, updated throughout the Sprint.
IncrementDefinition of DoneA concrete stepping stone toward the Product Goal; must be usable. Work is not part of an Increment unless it meets the Definition of Done. Multiple Increments can be created in a Sprint.
Definition of Done: the formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets quality measures. If there is an organizational standard, all teams must follow it as a minimum. If there is none, the Scrum Team creates one. It is not the same as acceptance criteria for a single item.

Product Backlog refinement

An ongoing activity, not an event: breaking down and further defining items into smaller, more precise ones. The Developers who will do the work are responsible for the sizing; the PO may influence by helping understand and select trade-offs.

6. Value, stakeholders, and forecasting

Driving value

  • Scrum does not prescribe how to measure value. A PO uses outcome and value measures, not just output. Evidence-Based Management (EBM) is Scrum.org’s framework for this: four Key Value Areas, Current Value, Unrealized Value, Time-to-Market, and Ability to Innovate.
  • Release frequently. A valuable Increment can be released to stakeholders before the end of the Sprint; the Sprint is not a release cycle.
  • An MVP and frequent feedback reduce the risk of building the wrong thing.

Stakeholders

  • The PO is the single voice connecting the Scrum Team and stakeholders, and is accountable for that engagement, especially at the Sprint Review.
  • Stakeholders attend the Sprint Review by invitation to collaborate on what to do next. They do not direct the Developers mid-Sprint.

Forecasting and estimation

  • Scrum does not prescribe story points, velocity, burndown charts, or any estimation technique. These are complementary practices, not Scrum.
  • Trends like a burndown or burnup can be used to forecast, but they never replace the importance of empiricism.
  • Total work remaining can be summed to assess progress toward the Product Goal at any time.

7. Quick-fire facts and common traps

  • The Scrum Master is a true leader who serves not a project manager and not the team’s boss.
  • Only the Product Owner can cancel a Sprint.
  • Scrum has three accountabilities, five events, three artifacts. There is no “project manager” in Scrum.
  • A Sprint always has a Sprint Goal; the work should be coherent toward that one goal.
  • The Product Backlog is never “complete”; it is emergent and evolves with the product.
  • Estimation, refinement detail, and how to write items are up to the team; Scrum does not dictate them.
  • You can release multiple times per Sprint, or hold a release, but holding it does not change the cadence of inspect-and-adapt.
  • If a question says the PO “must get sign-off,” “runs the Daily Scrum,” or “assigns tasks to Developers,” it is almost certainly wrong.
  • Maximizing value is about outcomes for users and the business not the volume of features shipped.
