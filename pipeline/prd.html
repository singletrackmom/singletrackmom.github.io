AI Opportunity Pipeline

1. Summary

The AI Opportunity Pipeline is a front door for AI ideas and a consistent way to decide what happens to each one. A department describes a problem in one form. The request lands as one row in a shared Sheet, is checked against three gates, scored with RICE, ranked, and answered with a written reason. A scored row holds what a requirements document starts from, so the highest-ranked request can move straight into a PRD. The intake is in pilot. An inventory of the AI tools colleges already run sits beside it, with three records from real reporting. The dashboard and the closing of inventory records are designed and not yet built.

2. Goal

Every AI idea that reaches the Student Support and Success domain leaves with an answer and a reason, reached the same way each time.

Four rules follow from that goal.

  • Inventory comes before requests. The cheapest fix is usually something another college already runs.
  • Gates come before scores. A request that fails privacy, security or accessibility is not scored.
  • “Not an AI problem” is a real answer. A process, policy, or staffing matter is documented and routed to whoever owns it.
  • Declines are recorded as visibly as approvals.

3. Users and context

Two groups use it. Student services staff and leaders at the ten Maricopa colleges submit what they have and what they need. Most are advisors and support staff with no time for a long form, so the form takes about ten minutes and accepts “I do not know” as a useful answer. Members of the domain check, score and answer what comes in.

The context is a district where the same service carries a different name at every college, where several colleges may license the same product without knowing it, and where there is no shared place to ask for help with AI. The requests it is built to take in are problems like these, the barriers students hit when they try to reach support:

  • Advising takes about three days to answer an email.
  • Students never find disability resources, because nobody who needs them thinks of themselves as disabled.
  • Tutoring has limited hours and lives in Discord, and nobody knows it is there.

4. How it works

The request form

The form starts from the problem, not the technology, and it doubles as the notes page for a discovery meeting. A department is not expected to know what AI can do. It has five sections.

SectionWhat it asks
About youName, email, college, department
The problemThe goal, what is getting in the way, how the work is done today, what is driving this now
Who it touchesWho is served, how many people, how often and how long each time, who does the work, who is affected, who approves a change, whether leadership knows
What success looks likeThe measure, where it stands today, the target
Before we talkWhat kind of information is involved, what is already in place or tried, and the kind of help if the requester already knows

The walk through the current process is how an AI problem is told apart from a process problem. The assessment, not the requester, says whether AI fits.

The request record

Each submission becomes one row in one Sheet. The first columns are the requester’s answers. The columns after them belong to the domain: three gates, impact, confidence, effort, the RICE score, the rank, the answer, the reason, an owner, a decision date and a link to the PRD.

Assessment

Privacy, security and accessibility are checked first, each as pass or fail. A request that fails one is not scored. A request that passes all three is scored with RICE: reach, multiplied by impact, multiplied by confidence, divided by effort. Reach comes from the form. Impact, confidence and effort are chosen from fixed lists, so two assessors use the same scale. The Sheet calculates the score and the rank. The score sorts the list. It does not make the decision, which comes from the gates and the conversation.

Decision and handoff

Each request ends at one of six answers: pilot, more discovery, use what already exists, training or process change, not an AI problem, or declined, with the reason written beside it. A filter on the answered rows is the decision log. The highest-ranked request moves into a PRD, started from its own row.

Track one, what exists

Each reported tool or automated workflow is logged with its college, category, license, and how it is reported to be working. Each ends at one of five answers: share across colleges, consolidate to a district license, replace with something built, retire, or leave as is. The inventory holds three records from real reporting. Closing a record and the dashboard are designed and not yet built.

5. Data, privacy, and governance

  • The product collects no student data. The form asks about a department’s problems, tools and processes, and tells the requester not to enter student names or ID numbers.
  • The kind of information involved is a required question, so the privacy gate has the answer before it is checked.
  • Requests hold staff names, emails and departments. They are stored in a Google Sheet shared with named members of the domain only.
  • The Sheet version history records who changed an assessment or an answer, and when.
  • The Sheet should live in a location the district owns before it holds requests from other colleges. That decision is open, and it is listed in section 10.
  • A hidden field on the form catches automated submissions.

6. Build and portability

  • A static HTML request form in vanilla JavaScript, with no framework.
  • A Google Apps Script web app that receives each request, writes the row, calculates the hours per week and emails the owner.
  • A Google Sheet for the record, with dropdowns for status, gates, impact, confidence and the answer, and formulas for the gate result, the RICE score and the rank.
  • No AI model and no API key. People do the assessing.
  • Hosted on GitHub Pages.
  • The operating model is the portable part. In an enterprise information technology environment this function lives in the service management platform, as a catalog item with a workflow, an assessment record, and a reporting layer. ServiceNow, TeamDynamix, and Jira Service Management all do this. This build demonstrates the model, not the tooling.

7. Pilot and testing

The model is calibrated before its scores are trusted, in this order.

  1. Run requests through the form and the Sheet, starting with advising, and confirm each lands as a complete row with its ID.
  2. Score the first requests together with domain members and write down where the scales need examples.
  3. Calibrate on eight to ten real past requests. Score each one after the fact and compare the result to the decision that was made. Each disagreement is either a flaw in the model or a decision worth revisiting.
  4. Name an owner for each gate.
  5. Run the inventory round with the ten colleges.
  6. Take at least one request through to a decline, with its reason.

8. Definition of success

  • Every request leaves with an answer and a written reason.
  • On the calibration set, the model’s result and the real decision agree, or the disagreement is explained.
  • A department can complete the form in about ten minutes without help.
  • A request cannot become a pilot unless the requester names one measure and its current baseline.
  • A decline is accepted because the criteria were published before the request arrived.
  • The platform question is answered: keep this build, or configure the model in a system the district already owns.

9. Rollout

  • Phase one. The request form and the Sheet, taking requests from departments that already know their problem.
  • Phase two. The inventory round with the ten colleges, and the dashboard.
  • Out of scope. Building the solutions, making purchasing decisions, and anything that turns out not to be about AI. Those are routed to their owners.

Out of scope

  • Building the solutions that a request leads to.
  • Making purchasing decisions.
  • Resolving anything that turns out not to be about AI, such as a process, policy or staffing matter; these are documented and routed to their owners.
  • Collecting any student record or other student data.
  • Using an AI model to assess entries; people do the assessing.
  • Asking the requester to decide whether AI fits the problem; the assessment decides that.
  • Serving as the long-term tooling; this version demonstrates the operating model, and the platform decision is still open.

10. Open questions and risks

  • Where the Sheet lives. It is in one person’s Google account. Before requests arrive from other colleges it should move to a location the district owns, with named editors.
  • Scores need anchors. Impact, confidence and effort are judgments. Without worked examples and calibration against past requests, the rank looks more precise than it is.
  • Risk should score its worst axis. A tool that is fine on six kinds of risk and severe on one should not average out to medium. The gates catch privacy, security and accessibility. Other risks are not yet scored.
  • Missing dimensions. Strategic alignment and urgency are asked for on the form but not yet part of the score.
  • The answers are still too few. Missing: refer to another owner, defer with a named blocker and a revisit date, and what happens after a pilot (scale, sustain, or sunset).
  • Turnaround. No response time is promised yet, and a requester who hears nothing stops asking.
  • Build or buy. If the district already licenses a service management platform, a homegrown intake tool is the kind of request this pipeline would redirect.
  • One maintainer. A tool kept up by one faculty member on top of a full teaching load is a risk in itself.

Questions to settle before the next build

  1. Which Google account owns the Sheet, and who in the district decides when it moves to a location the district owns?
  2. Who signs each of the three gates, and what does each person check before marking pass?
  3. How are impact, confidence and effort anchored, with worked examples, so that two assessors score the same request the same way?
  4. What turnaround is a requester told to expect after they submit, and who is responsible for meeting it?
  5. How does an inventory record get into the product: who enters it, through what screen, and what are the allowed values for category, license, and how it is reported to be working?

11. Roadmap

  • Now. Request form and Sheet open. First requests scored with the domain.
  • Next. Worked examples for each scale. Calibration on past requests. A named owner for each gate.
  • After calibration. Inventory round with the colleges, then the dashboard.
  • Before wider use. Move the Sheet to a district-owned location, and decide the platform.
