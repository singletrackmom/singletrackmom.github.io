A faculty practitioner’s path to agentic AI, and a plan to teach it
Michelle Blomberg · Glendale Community College, Digital Media Arts · Living document
This is a living document, updated as the work evolves. It is an honest, in-progress account meant for a conference session or a practitioner paper, not a finished study. Tools described as prototypes are prototypes.
Abstract. This paper describes how I am learning to design and run agentic AI, software that watches, decides, and acts on its own, by building real projects rather than studying from a distance. It traces the method (learning by building, with reusable instructions so the practice compounds), the agents and tools I built, and where this is going next: mentoring faculty and student-services staff to build their own agents that take routine work off their plates so they can spend more time with students. The throughline is a design principle I hold to in every build: collect no student data.

1. Why agents, and why a teacher is building them

The creative tools my students use have always changed. What lasts is the thinking underneath. AI is the largest of those shifts so far, and the part moving fastest right now is agentic AI: assistants that do not just answer a single question but run on their own, check things, and take action. Employers are beginning to expect these skills, which means my students need exposure to them, and I did not feel I could teach them honestly without building real fluency myself.

There is a second reason, closer to home. As co-chair of the Student Support and Success domain of my district’s AI committee, I keep seeing the same pattern: support staff spend hours on routine, repetitive work (the easy factual question, the “we will get back to you in a few days” booking email) that pulls them away from the students who need a human. Agents are well suited to exactly that routine layer. If I can learn to build them, I can teach others to build them too.

2. The method: learning by building

I learn this best by making things. Instead of collecting courses, I set out to build a working set of small agents and tools, use them daily, and let each one teach me the next. Two habits made the practice stick:

  • Reusable instructions. Each agent has its own set of plain-language Markdown instructions, so I am refining a recipe rather than starting from scratch every time. That is how a pile of one-off experiments became a dependable daily practice.
  • Real problems only. Every build solves something I (or someone I know) actually needs. That keeps the learning honest and the result useful.

This is the same stance I bring to teaching: model the work, do not lecture about it from a distance.

3. What I built

These range from student-facing tools to small scheduled agents I run for myself as practice. The student and teaching tools are the heart of it; the personal agents are the practice ground.

Render prototype, pilot Fall 2026

An AI-supported career-launch environment woven through my AVC 248 capstone. Students build their materials all semester anchored to one real reach job, and Render ends by producing a portable career agent and a personal learning plan they keep after the course. Its newest piece is a gap-to-training agent: it takes the requirements from the real job postings a student collects all semester, compares them to what the student has, names the gaps, and builds a sequenced training plan to close them. (See the training-plan agent prototype.)

CopaMigo prototype, in testing

A routing agent for student support. A student describes a problem in plain language, in English or one of several built-in languages, and CopaMigo routes them to the right human service with a handoff card showing contact, hours, and what to ask for. It grew directly out of my Student Support and Success committee work, and it collects no personally identifiable information.

A suite of scheduled agents personal practice

To learn agentic patterns, I run several small agents that wake on their own each morning, check a source, and act: a calendar-native watcher that catches rare campground cancellations and drops the find on my calendar, and daily research agents that publish to living pages. These are personal, but they are where I learned how scheduling, autonomy, and reusable instructions actually behave. The lesson that transferred: a good agent watches and proposes, and leaves the irreversible decision to a person.

Cultivate v1.3

My first AI build and the on-ramp for all of this: a personal learning environment that gathers my professional development into one organized, offline-first place. It is the working model of the connectivist personal learning environment my graduate research described.

4. From my practice to mentoring others

Goal: Help faculty and student-services staff build their own simple agents that absorb routine work, so they can focus on students.

Audience: Campus faculty through the new AI Community of Practice, and district student-services staff through the Student Support and Success domain.

Process: Short, hands-on sessions where people build a small, no-data agent for a real piece of their own routine work, using reusable instructions they can keep and adapt.

This is the next phase. Through the Faculty AI Community of Practice I am launching with our Center for Teaching, Learning and Engagement, I will share what I have learned, including how to build agents, starting this fall. Through the district committee, one planned initiative is training student-services personnel, with at least one session on building agents. The aim is not to replace anyone. It is to take the dull, repetitive layer off people’s plates so the human part of their work, the part only a person can do, gets more of their time.

5. Open questions

  • Data sovereignty. My hard line is that these tools collect no student data. The preferred path is building data-sovereign tools over buying data-harvesting ones. How far can that line be held as agents do more?
  • Sustainability. What does it take for staff to maintain their own agents after a workshop, without a developer on call?
  • Equity and trust. How do we keep students and staff in control, and make sure automation widens access rather than adding a new barrier?

These are not solved. Naming them honestly is part of the work, and part of what I want the Community of Practice to wrestle with together.
