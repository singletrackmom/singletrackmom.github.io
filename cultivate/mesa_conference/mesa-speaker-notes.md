# Mesa AI Summit — Speaker Notes

**Building Career Tools That Outlast the Semester**
May 18, 2026 · 50-minute virtual session · 12:30 to 1:20 pm · 24 slides

Read these off the iPad while scrolling the deck. Bold times in brackets are "you should be around here" cues, glance at the clock and adjust. New or unrehearsed slides have fuller notes; the rest are short because you know the material.

**The four learning outcomes the conference description promised:**
1. Assigning AI use vs. designing AI tools around student needs.
2. Identify one way a structured AI interface in your own discipline could reduce friction.
3. How embedding AI in a purposeful workflow changes the student experience vs. prompting from scratch.
4. How interface design choices shape whether students engage with AI or avoid it.

Slides 8, 10 and the walkthrough cover 1 and 3. Slide 20 names all four out loud. Slide 21 delivers 2.

## 20 · The idea underneath all of this &nbsp; **[1:07]**
**(The thesis. Slow down. This covers outcomes 1, 3, 4.)**
"I've shown you Render, but Render is not the point. An empty AI chat asks the student to already know what to ask. For a lot of students that blank page is where they freeze, or decide AI isn't for them. A structured tool makes the first move. It asks the question, holds their goals and saved work as context. That's the difference between assigning students to go use AI and designing something that meets them where they are. The interface is the difference between confidence and avoidance. And building one is not a coding skill, it's prompting. If you can describe what you want, you can do this."
Pause. Let it land.

## 21 · Your turn &nbsp; **[1:09]**
**(Outcome 2. Virtual, use the chat. Keep it to about 2 minutes.)**
"Quick thing. Think of one thing you paste into an AI chat over and over, the same prompt, every week. That repetition is the signal. Anything you prompt from scratch again and again is a structured tool waiting to be built, in your discipline, for your students or just for you. You do not need to be a programmer. I'm not one."
Then: "Drop one in the chat, what's yours?" Give it a minute, read two or three aloud, react briefly.
**If the chat is quiet:** give your own, "Here's mine, I was constantly pasting the same prompt to reformat student feedback into the same structure, that could be a tool." Then move on.
