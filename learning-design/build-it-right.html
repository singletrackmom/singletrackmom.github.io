Build It Right

Workbook · Learning Design · UX Design

How to build a website with an AI assistant so it is correct from the first page instead of corrected on the hundredth. Written from the other order: a design system written early and written well, and then years of slow drift because nothing enforced it.

Goal

Give someone starting from nothing a build order that makes broken pages hard to create, so accessibility, consistency and file organisation are handled by the process rather than by remembering.

Audience

A student building a first real site, and anyone working with an AI assistant on a project big enough to lose track of. No coding background assumed. Every technical step says what it is and why it exists.

Process

Seven parts, in order. Parts 1 to 4 happen before you build a single page, which feels backwards and is the entire point.

The One Idea

A rule that is not checked is not a rule. It is a wish.

You can write “every page must be accessible” in a style guide. You can put it in bold. You can tell an assistant at the start of every session. And you will still end up with pages that are not accessible, because a written rule is something a person or an assistant has to remember, and remembering degrades over hours and over months.

A program does not degrade. It re-reads its rules on every single run, forever, and it fails loudly when they are broken.

So the whole method is: take the rules you care about and turn them into programs that fail. Everything below is that idea applied in order.

Why this comes first

If you understand only this, you will make good decisions about everything else. If you skip it, the rest reads as unnecessary work, and you will skip that too, and you will end up auditing a hundred pages by hand.

Part 1, Decide What You Are Making

Step 1

Write down goal, audience and process

Three short paragraphs, before anything else. Who is this for. What must it do for them. How will it work. Write them in a plain text file and keep them where you will see them.

Why

Every later decision resolves against these. When you cannot decide whether a page needs a feature, the audience line answers it. When an assistant asks you a question, this is what you answer from.

It also stops the most common failure in a first project, which is building something impressive that nobody asked for.

Step 2

Decide what “done” will mean, in writing

Before you build, write the list of things that must be true of every page. Not aspirations. Checkable facts. For a website that list starts here:

  • The page declares its language.
  • It has one main heading, and only one.
  • It has a unique, descriptive title.
  • It has a “skip to content” link that actually goes somewhere.
  • Every image has alternative text describing it.
  • Every link says where it goes. Never “click here.”
  • Every form field has a label.
  • Every link that opens a new tab is marked safe.
  • Colours meet contrast requirements.
  • The page works on a phone.
Why

You cannot check against a standard you have not written. This list becomes the checker in Part 4, almost line for line.

Accessibility belongs here, at the beginning, not in a cleanup phase later. Retrofitting it across a finished site takes days. Building it in costs nothing, because the template carries it.

Part 2, Write the Standard

Step 3

Make one design system file and never decide twice

One document that names every value the site is allowed to use. Two typefaces, one for headings and one for everything else. A short list of colours, each with a job: this one is for links and only links, this one is for headings, this one is the one box fill. Exact sizes for each heading level. One page width. One header. One footer.

Say what is banned as clearly as what is allowed. No gradients. No em dashes. Curly quotes only. Body text never justified.

Why

Without this, every page is a fresh set of decisions, and decisions made on different days do not match. That mismatch is what makes a site look amateur, and it is invisible to you because you only ever look at one page at a time.

It is also the only way an assistant can help without introducing drift. It cannot invent a colour if the palette is closed.

Step 4

Name one real page as the page to copy

Pick the cleanest page you have and declare it canonical. Everything new is a copy of that page with the content swapped.

Why

“Follow the style guide” requires interpretation. “Copy this file” does not.

Part 3, Build the Templates

Step 5

Make a template that is already correct

Build one blank page that already contains every item from your Step 2 list: the language declaration, the title, the description, the social sharing tags, the skip link, the header, the single main heading, the content area, the footer. Fill it with obvious placeholder text.

Make a second one for interactive tools, which follow different rules, and say plainly which is which.

This is the highest-value step in the whole workbook. A page copied from a correct template cannot be born broken, so there is nothing to find in an audit later.

Why

Prevention beats detection. Every page I had to repair had one thing in common: it was hand-built from scratch by someone starting from an empty file. Not one of them was missing something hard. They were missing a skip link, a heading, a wrapper. Things a template would have supplied for free.

Part 4, Build the Checkers

This is the part people skip, and it is the part that saves the project. A checker is a small program that opens every page, looks for the things on your Step 2 list, and prints what is missing.

First, what a checker actually is, and what it cannot do

The checkers here are written in Python, which is a language for writing short lists of instructions a computer follows. If you have never programmed, this is the moment it sounds like the project just got away from you. It did not, and the reason is worth being precise about.

What each part of the system is allowed to do
WhoDoes what
YouDecide what the site says, how it looks, and what counts as right.
The assistantWrites the pages and writes the checkers. This is the part that can make mistakes.
The checkerOpens files, reads them, prints a list. That is all it does.

A checker cannot change your website. Not as a promise, as a property. There is no instruction in it that writes a file, deletes a file, or moves a file. It opens things to read and it prints. If a checker had a bug, the worst it could do is print something wrong.

You can verify that yourself without knowing the language. Open the file and search it for the word write. If a checker contains no write instruction, it cannot write. That is a thing you can confirm in ten seconds and it is worth confirming, because a tool you have verified is a tool you will trust, and a tool you trust is the only kind that works.

Why this matters more than it sounds

The nervous instinct is to point the worry at the programming. Point it at the right place instead. The assistant is the part that edits your files, and the assistant is the part that makes mistakes. The checker exists to watch the assistant.

So the checker is deliberately written in a way that cannot cause damage. It is a supervisor with no hands.

What you have to learn

One line. The last line the checker prints:

  • PASS, which means carry on.
  • BLOCKED, followed by what is wrong, which means fix it or hand the message to your assistant and ask it to.

You never open the program. You never edit it. You never learn the language. Reading one word is the entire skill, and that is not a simplification, it is the actual job.

Why write it at all, then

Because the alternative is checking a hundred pages by hand every time you change something, which nobody does, which is why sites drift.

Writing a checker is also the single best use of an AI assistant, because the rules are already written down in Step 2 and the output is verifiable. You describe a rule in plain language, it writes the check, you run it and see whether the answer is true. You are not learning to program. You are describing what correct means, precisely enough that a machine can test it, and that is a design skill rather than an engineering one.

You do not have to write it yourself. This is exactly the kind of work an AI assistant is good at, because the rules are already written down and the output is verifiable. You describe the rule, it writes the check, you run it and see whether the answer is true.

Step 6

The design checker

Checks the things from Step 3. Are the widths the same on every page. Is the navigation identical. Is anyone using a colour that is not in the palette, or a gradient, or a straight quote, or an em dash.

Step 7

The accessibility checker

Checks the Step 2 list. One heading. Alt text on images. Labels on form fields. Descriptive link text. Headings that go in order without skipping a level. No duplicate identifiers. Every new-tab link marked safe.

Step 8

The link checker

Opens every link on the site and confirms the thing it points at exists.

Why this one specifically

Dead links are the fastest way to look careless, and they appear on their own whenever a file is renamed or moved. A person will never catch them all. A program catches all of them in a second.

Step 9

The tidy checker

Checks where files live. No stray documents at the top level. One working document per project, in that project’s own folder. No files named backup or old or temp.

Why a program and not discipline

File placement looks like something you can just be careful about. You cannot, and neither can an assistant, over months. Turning “keep it tidy” into a check is the difference between a folder you can find things in and a folder you dread opening.

When a checker reports something that is actually fine, fix the checker, not your patience. The first run of my accessibility checker reported 579 failures. Almost all were the checker misunderstanding valid code. If you start ignoring output, the checker is dead and so is the system. A checker you argue with is worthless; a checker you trust is the whole point.

Part 5, Wire the Gate

Step 10

Put every checker behind one command

Four checkers is four things to remember, which means it is zero things you will actually run. Wrap them in one command with one answer at the bottom: pass, or blocked and here is why.

Step 11

Make it run automatically when you save your work

Most tools that store versions of your project can run a check automatically before saving. Wire your one command into that. Now broken work cannot be saved, whether the mistake came from you, from an assistant, or from a session six months from now that never read your style guide.

Why automatic and not manual

A check you have to remember to run is a wish again. The gate has to be in the path of the work, not beside it.

Step 12

Build the escape hatch at the same time

Any gate that can block you must have a way out that you can actually reach. Mine is a file: if a specific empty file exists, the gate warns instead of blocking. I can create it by hand in seconds, without a command line.

Why this is not optional

A safety system that can lock you out of your own work will be torn out the first time it does. Build the exit at the same time as the gate, and the gate survives.

Also: make the gate block only on things that are truly broken. If it stops you over a missing description, you will start ignoring it, and then it protects nothing.

Part 6, Now Build Pages

Step 13

The loop, and it never changes

  1. Copy the template.
  2. Replace the placeholder content.
  3. Run the one command.
  4. Fix anything it names.
  5. Run it again.
  6. Save.
Why the loop is boring on purpose

“Done” now has a definition that does not depend on anyone’s judgement, mood, or how late it is. That is the entire goal. Boring is the feature.

Part 7, Working With the Assistant

An AI assistant will do enormous amounts of good work and will also drift over a long session, exactly like a tired person. The gate is what catches the drift. These three files are what keep it pointed in the right direction in the first place.

Why split them at all

Because whatever is in the context file gets read every session, and whatever is long gets skimmed. Keep it short and it stays true. Let it grow into a manual and it becomes wallpaper.

And never write the same rule in two places. Two copies drift apart, and then a session follows the wrong one. If a rule needs changing, change it in the single file that owns it.

On backlogs

The backlog is a first-class artifact, not clutter. Scrum is right about this: work that is not written down and ordered does not get done, it gets rediscovered. When a checker finds forty things, those forty things are a backlog, and the value is that they are visible, ordered, and can be pulled from deliberately instead of all at once.

The only rule is where it lives. A backlog belongs in its own named file, next to the tool or project it serves. Pasted into a context file it becomes wallpaper nobody reads. Pasted into a task list it buries this week’s actual work, which is how a task file stops being usable.

Keep it as a queue, not a diary. Things that are finished leave it. If it needs a record of what was done, that is a different document with a different job.

What I Got Wrong

I did not skip the standard. I wrote a CSS design system early, defined the palette and the type scale, and kept a style guide. That is the right instinct and it is further than most projects ever get. It is also why the site looks consistent.

What I never built was anything that enforced it. So the standard was true on the day it was written and then drifted, one page at a time, across years and across many separate work sessions. Nobody was careless. There was just nothing in the path of the work that could say no.

Here is what that cost, so you can decide whether Parts 4 and 5 are worth doing before your first page rather than after your hundredth.

  • Twelve pages missing basic structure. No main content area, no heading, or a skip link pointing at something that did not exist. Each was hand-built by a different session. A template would have prevented every one.
  • Two hundred and fifty-seven links opening new tabs without the safety attribute, spread across fifty-two files.
  • A broken heading inside an exported file that students downloaded and kept. It opened one heading and closed another, and it shipped in every copy.
  • A reference document written into my task file, which pushed the actual week’s work down the page and made the file useless, which is exactly why it needed reorganising in the first place.
  • A rule I wrote in the morning, broken by me the same afternoon.

Every one of those rules already existed in writing. The only rule never broken was the one enforced by a program.

The lesson is not “be more careful.” I was careful. The lesson is that care does not survive contact with a hundred files and a long afternoon, and a checker does. Build the checker first.

Status

The gate described in Parts 4 and 5 is built and running on this site. Templates are in progress. The accessibility backlog it produced is being worked through deliberately rather than in one pass, because some of it involves design decisions that should not be automated.
