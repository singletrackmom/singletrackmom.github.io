1. Summary

Planning a multi-state road trip means holding the route in your head, the reservations in your email, the fuel estimate on a napkin, and the list of things you want to see in a phone note. By day three nothing matches anything, and the one document you made has been out of date since the first campsite got booked.

Wayfinder is one product that does two things. It is a living single-page itinerary that keeps weather, fuel strategy, road and vehicle clearance, campsites, site logistics, and backup plans in one place that stays usable on the road, and it is a builder that takes a traveler’s own interests, constraints, and reservations and produces a tailored itinerary in that same format. The planner proves the format works. The builder makes the format available to someone who is not me.

One real trip is planned end to end as the working example: a 15-day van loop from Golden, Colorado out to Yosemite and home through the Eastern Sierra, southern Nevada, Zion, Bryce, Capitol Reef, and Moab, about 3,000 miles across five states and six national parks. It was built before the trip, used during the trip, and rewritten afterward to the route actually driven, which is the real test of a living document.

Honest status: this is a personal prototype. The planner is built and has been used on a real trip. The builder is built and live as a static page. Nothing here is a production service, there is no backend, no account, and no user data of any kind.

2. Goal

Replace the pile of half-remembered logistics with one calm source of truth that survives contact with the road. The planning has to hold up when the traveler is tired, off-signal, and standing at a trailhead, not just when they are at a desk with twelve tabs open.

Three things follow from that goal, and they drive most of the design decisions in this document.

  • The itinerary is a document, not a chat log. It reads the same the week before the trip, on the road, and a year later as a trip record.
  • Everything a traveler needs at a given moment lives inside the day it belongs to. Nobody should have to scroll to a reservations section at the bottom of the page to find a confirmation number.
  • The tool links out rather than replaces. Recreation.gov still takes the booking, AllTrails still shows the trail, Mountain Project still shows the route. Wayfinder is the connective tissue.

4. How it works

Wayfinder is one product with two capabilities. The planner is the format. The builder produces the format. They share the same data model (days, stops, overnights, activities, constraints) and the same rules about what belongs on a day card.

The planner

A single static HTML page holding one trip. The page is organized around day cards, each with a drive time, a live weather chip, an icon-led title, the day’s stops in the order the traveler would actually encounter them, and one overnight card.

ElementWhat it does
Route mapA hand-drawn SVG state-outline map with numbered stops at true geographic position, a ranger-badge marker on each national park, and a legend distinguishing overnight stops from day visits. No tile server, no map token, so it renders with no connection. Clicking a marker scrolls to that day.
Color ruleOne rule governs color: where you sleep that night sets the day’s color, and that color is shared by the map marker, the day-jump bar dot, and the itinerary’s left day-bar. A reader can trace any dot back to its day by color alone.
Overnight cardOne card per night. Site name and map link on top, then a persistent logistics list: fire and burn-ban status first (with a link to the agency that actually sets the rule for that jurisdiction), then water, showers, cell and satellite sky view, bear storage, and dog restrictions. Booking confirmation and backup campgrounds fold into a single collapsible drawer.
Weather chipLive high and low from Open·Meteo, fetched in the browser, no key and no backend. Inside the 16-day window the chip shows a forecast. Outside it, the chip falls back to a five-year historical average for the same date and is styled to make clear it is climatology, not a forecast. If both fail, it shows a neutral placeholder rather than something wrong.
Fuel strategyCallouts on the long legs naming the fuel deserts ahead and the towns to top off in, so the driver is never caught between stations.
Clearance checkA one-line vehicle-clearance verdict where it matters. The Zion to Mount Carmel tunnel posts a wall of size limits on the approach with no good way to check mid-drive, so the day card states the restriction, the van’s actual dimensions, and the verdict in advance.
BackupsAlternate campgrounds live in the day they belong to, not in a list at the bottom. When a plan falls through mid-trip, the traveler is already on the right day card and the backup is one tap away.
Direction pillsEvery stop, hike, spring, and campground carries a map pill that opens turn-by-turn directions from the traveler’s current location, not just a pin. A pin is useful at home and useless on the road.
Geology cardsA collapsible section on each driving day covering the formations visible from the road, keyed to named milestones. Collapsed by default: there for the curious reader, invisible to the one who just wants the confirmation number.
Packing listA separate tab with sub-lists per traveler and per vehicle, grouped by category, one checkbox per line. Check state persists in the browser.
BudgetPer-category totals and a single trip total built on the fuel prices actually paid, with a state-by-state breakdown so the assumptions can be checked.
Cancellation watcherA companion scheduled task that checks target campgrounds each morning for openings and pushes the result to a calendar notification. It runs on the release schedule of the booking platforms, which drop cancelled sites first thing in the morning. It caught a Tahoe shoreline cancellation three days before departure and upgraded the leg.

The builder

The builder is a static form that turns a traveler’s constraints into the same day-by-day structure. It has two front doors, because two very different people show up: the one who wants a campground in an area for three nights, and the one who wants a whole multi-stop route planned around a reservation they already hold.

LayerWhat it doesWhere it runs
The formCollects the trip frame, three pace controls (daily drive cap, days before a rest day, total rest days), a public-lands lean, repeatable reservation and destination rows with priorities and notes, and checkboxes for activities, sights, food, logistics, and camp style.Browser
The planner logicLays out the day-by-day rhythm from the traveler’s own rules: it pins reservation nights to their dates, marks depart and return days, inserts rest days on the stated cadence, distributes prioritized destinations across the explore days, and weaves in chosen activities so each day reads like a real plan.Browser
The lookupsFor every box checked, builds a targeted search link (Recreation.gov for camping, Mountain Project for climbing, RoadsideAmerica for oddities, plus fire-restriction and road-condition searches for the area).Browser
The outputA printable plan, the campground searches, and an optional one-click prompt that packages every parameter so any AI tool can write the prose version.Browser

The split is deliberate. The page does the structured scaffolding, the parts that are pure logic: ordering days, honoring reservations, respecting the drive cap, building the right searches. The open-ended reasoning, exact routing, real drive times, and picking a specific campground, is what a language model is good at, and that happens either in the traveler’s head with the links provided or by pasting the generated prompt into their own AI tool. The tool says out loud which half is which.

What the builder learns from the planner

Several rules in the planner exist because the trip taught them, and they become generator criteria rather than one-off notes.

  • Van compatibility means a minimum site length of 24 feet, drive-in access only, no hike-in or boat-in sites, and a flag on any site with a severe driveway grade. Campground-level listings often show the most restrictive site’s limit, so the check has to happen at the individual site.
  • Climate is a constraint. The trip filtered camps by elevation to keep overnight lows in a comfortable band, and that becomes a stated preference in the form rather than a manual research step.
  • Clearance is pre-checked. The traveler stores a vehicle profile once, and every tunnel, low underpass, and length-limited road on the route gets checked before departure.
  • Links are validated. Agencies retire individual trail and feature pages often, so generated trips should prefer stable hub pages over deep links, and every external link should be checked for a live response before the page ships.

5. Data, privacy, and governance

Wayfinder collects nothing. There is no account, no login, no form posted anywhere, no analytics, and no stored user data. Both the planner and the builder run entirely in the visitor’s browser. This is the same no·PII, minimum-data principle that governs my other tools, and here it is easy to hold because there is no server to hold anything.

DataWhere it livesLeaves the device?
Trip content (days, stops, camps, budget)Written into the HTML file itselfNo. It is the page.
Packing-list check stateBrowser localStorageNo
Builder form entriesBrowser memory only, discarded on closeNo
Weather lookupsOpen·Meteo, a keyless public endpointCoordinates and dates only. No identity, no account.
Confirmation numbers, names, personal detailsKept out of the shareable page entirelyNot published

The shareable page rule

The trip page is meant to be handed to a friend, so it is written to be safe to hand to a stranger. Travelers appear as roles (“Mom,” “Teen”), not names. No flight details, no reference to who will be alone and where, no work details. Confirmation numbers stay out of any version that gets shared. In the builder, anonymized labels and masked confirmation numbers are the default rather than an option a user has to remember to turn on.

No API key in a public page

A static page on public hosting cannot safely hold an API key, because any key in the source is visible to every visitor. That single fact rules out an in-app AI call for this version, which is why the builder generates a prompt for the traveler to paste into their own AI tool instead of calling a model itself. It is a constraint, and the design leans into it rather than pretending it is not there.

6. Build and portability

  • The front end is a single HTML file per surface, in vanilla JavaScript with CSS custom properties, no framework and no build step. The planner is one file, the builder is one file.
  • Hosting is GitHub Pages: static, free, nothing to operate.
  • There is no backend, no login, and no database. The only outbound network call in the planner is the weather lookup.
  • Collapsible sections use native markup (details and summary), so they are keyboard navigable by default and print correctly.
  • Maps are hand-drawn SVG, with no tile server and no map token, so the route renders with no connection.
  • Wayfinder calls no model. The builder emits a plain-text prompt that runs in any AI tool (Claude, ChatGPT, and others), so there is no model to be locked into and nothing to migrate if a better one arrives. If a hosted version ever adds a live call, the model sits behind one function and stays replaceable.
  • The page is fully readable with no signal. Weather chips degrade to a neutral placeholder rather than an error, and everything else is baked into the file.
  • The output of the builder is an editable HTML page the traveler owns, and it does not depend on Wayfinder continuing to exist.

7. Pilot and testing

The pilot is not a course rollout and it is not a beta program. It is real use.

  • The planner has been road-tested. One 15-day van trip was planned in it, driven with it, and then rewritten to the route actually taken. Every failure mode in this document came from that trip: the tunnel-clearance panic, the dead trail links, the backups that were useless because they lived at the bottom of the page, the fire rules that differ by land manager.
  • The builder is being tested by real travelers, people who ask how these trips get planned and are handed the form instead of an explanation. The question under test is whether someone who did not build the tool can get to a plan they trust without help.
  • What is being watched: whether a user finishes the form or abandons it, whether the day-by-day skeleton is close enough to be edited rather than thrown away, which lookups get clicked, and whether anyone uses the generated AI prompt or ignores it.
  • What is not being measured: nothing is instrumented, because instrumenting it would mean collecting data, and that trade is not worth making for a personal tool. Feedback is qualitative and comes from asking.

8. Definition of success

Success for the planner: a traveler opens the page in a canyon with no signal and finds the answer they need without opening a second tab. The page is still accurate on day twelve. After the trip, it reads as a record of what actually happened rather than a plan that stopped matching reality.

Success for the builder: someone lands on it, either grabs a campground search in fifteen seconds or builds a full multi-stop skeleton in about two minutes, prints it or hands the prompt to their AI tool, and walks away with a real plan, having signed up for nothing and handed over nothing.

Failure looks like this: the plan gets abandoned mid-trip because it went stale, the traveler opens a link that is dead, or the skeleton is so generic they rewrite it from scratch. Those are the three things worth designing against.

9. Rollout

Wayfinder is public in the sense that the pages are on the open web, and private in the sense that nobody is being asked to sign up for anything. Making it a real product for other people would take specific work, and it is worth being clear about what that work is rather than implying it is already done.

StepWhat it takes
TodayTwo static pages. No cost to run, nothing collected, nothing to maintain but the content.
Link validationEvery external link in a generated trip checked for a live response before the page ships, and re-checked on a schedule, falling back to the authoritative parent page when a deep link retires.
A curated place databaseCampgrounds, hot springs, trailheads, and roadside oddities with canonical names, coordinates, and tags for interest, vehicle compatibility, and accessibility. This is the real work behind a generator that produces specifics rather than searches.
Live dataCampground availability, road and pass status, and fire closures wired into the day cards. The booking platforms expose availability through endpoints, so this is possible, but it needs a scheduled service, not a static page.
AccountsSaved trips, sharing, and an in-app AI call all require a server, a database, and a key held somewhere safe. That means becoming the custodian of other people’s data, which is a deliberate decision and not a default. The static version delivers value today without that weight.

10. Open questions and risks

Question or riskWhere it stands
Link rotThe most likely way a generated trip fails a traveler. Agencies retire individual trail and feature pages routinely. Mitigation is to prefer stable hub URLs and validate before publish, but it is not built yet.
Booking platformsAvailability data is reachable, but the endpoints are undocumented and can change without notice. Anything built on them is built on sand and needs to fail quietly rather than loudly.
Generic outputA skeleton built from rules alone can read like a template. The open question is how much specificity the builder can deliver before it needs a real place database, and whether the AI prompt hand-off is a genuine solution or a way of passing the hard part along.
Who is in the vanThe builder captures what the planner likes. A trip usually carries people who want different trips. Balancing an itinerary across several travelers, so no one just rides along, is unsolved and is the most interesting thing left to build.
ScopeThe temptation is to rebuild AllTrails, Recreation.gov, and Atlas Obscura badly. The discipline is to stay the connective tissue and link out.
Whether anyone else wants thisUnknown. The planner solves a real problem for one household. That is not evidence that it solves it for anyone else, and the builder is the test of that, not the proof.

Non-goals

  • Not a flight search engine, hotel comparison tool, or restaurant review site.
  • Not a booking system. Wayfinder links to the booking platforms and the traveler books there.
  • Not for luxury travel, business trips, or international itineraries. This is for North American road trips, vehicle-based, camping and lodging mixed.
  • Not a social network, and not an app. Web first, mobile responsive, no account.
