What the study is for

The support that keeps students enrolled already exists on every campus. The problem is that students cannot find it at the moment they need it. This study is the diagnosis: a structured walk of the whole student journey, at each of the ten colleges, that records where a student hits a barrier and how severe that barrier is.

The mandate of the Student Support and Success domain is deliberately narrow. The study does not set out to fix every barrier it finds. It sets out to identify where AI can close the gaps that drive students away, above all the support and connection that already exist but go unfound. Barriers that are process or policy problems are documented and routed to the owner who can fix them. Where the fix is automation, the aim is to take routine, repetitive work off advising and financial aid staff so their hours go to the students who need a person. No one is replaced.

How the design scaled

The study began with three research personas walking the full journey at each of the ten colleges, which is thirty distinct journeys. That design describes the problem but cannot map it at the resolution needed to prioritize.

It has since scaled. The unit of the study is now one persona, at one campus, attempting one task. A library of 36 personas and a taxonomy of 46 base tasks multiply toward roughly 500 scenarios, and each scenario is attempted at each of the ten colleges, which is about 5,000 task-runs split between human and AI testers. The full method is in the methodology and requirements, the personas are in the persona library, the scenarios are in the scenario and task bank, and progress is tracked on the coverage dashboard.

Two methods, run together

  1. Expert heuristic evaluation. Evaluators inspect each college’s student-facing interfaces against Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics and record each violation with its location and a severity rating. No persona and no student is involved.
  2. Task-based usability testing. A tester adopts a persona and attempts one task on the live systems from a real felt need, narrating as they go.

Both are required. Inspection catches violations a tester silently works around and never reports. Task testing exposes failures no heuristic predicts, such as a service that exists but is named something the persona would never search for.

The protocol

Runs use a concurrent think-aloud protocol. The tester is given a goal and never a destination, and the facilitator does not prompt toward a service, name an office, or confirm that a page is the right page. Telling a tester where to go destroys the finding the run exists to make, because findability is what is under test. The facilitator intervenes only to prevent a genuine dead stop, and each intervention is recorded as an assist. The protocol is dry-run at a single college before any district-wide fieldwork.

Agent readability

Each service is scored twice: once for human usability, and once for whether a student’s own AI assistant could navigate it. The second score, the emerging practice of agent experience, matters because students increasingly send an assistant to find an answer, and a page that defeats an agent defeats them too.

What every run records

MeasureHow it is captured
Task outcomeSuccess, partial success, or failure. Partial success means the tester reached the service but could not finish the task.
Time on taskMinutes from the start of the attempt to the outcome.
AssistsThe number of times the facilitator had to intervene to prevent a dead stop.
Path takenWhere the tester looked, in order, and the local service name as the college labels it.
EaseThe Single Ease Question, a 7-point rating asked immediately after the task.
SeverityThe Nielsen 0 to 4 rating, scored independently by two raters.
Bilingual gapWhether the path exists in Spanish as well as English.

Severity is rated 0 to 4, following Nielsen: 0 not a usability problem, 1 cosmetic, 2 minor, 3 major, 4 catastrophic (a student cannot complete the task and would plausibly give up). A rating combines frequency, meaning how many students would meet the barrier; impact, meaning how hard it is to overcome once met; and persistence, meaning whether a student learns around it once or hits it again every time. Two raters score each finding independently and disagreements are adjudicated in a short review.

How a tester runs a session

A session has two parts. The run sheet supplies everything for one run: the persona, the test login created at that tester’s college, the college, the task, and what counts as done. A tester runs the campus whose systems that tester can reach, because a Maricopa login reaches only its own college. The capture form records the run: persona and college, the task attempted, the path taken, the outcome, time on task, assists, the ease score, and the severity rating. One record per task.

  • The capture form, filled in immediately after the task while it is fresh.

The journey

The route covers more than sixty touchpoints across ten stages, plus a cross-cutting population track and a swirl track. The stages mirror the service inventory, so every service identified in the crosswalk has a stop here and nothing identified goes untested.

Why this is research and not a survey

PrincipleWhat it means in the instrument
Problems, not destinationsA persona is given a felt need, never an office. The gap between self-serving and abandoning the process to call a human is the barrier data.
Swirling is built inPersonas enroll at a second college, which means a second application, residency, consortium aid, and making credits count back home. The district does not operate as a single institution from a student’s side.
Grounded in real dataThe tasks are cross-walked from the service inventory, the earlier Glendale Community College student frustrations survey, and student senate notes from another college in the district.
Barriers become prioritiesEach documented barrier maps to a proposed, human-centered fix, ranked by severity against reach. That hand-off is the recommendations work.

Analysis and tooling

Findings are grouped by affinity mapping into themes, plotted on a severity-by-reach matrix, and the worst are placed on a journey map and blueprinted against the backstage staff actions that produce them. The study is run and analyzed in a research repository for tagging and synthesis, with journey maps, service blueprints, and findability exercises (card sorting, first-click, and tree-test-style tasks) built in a collaborative whiteboard.
