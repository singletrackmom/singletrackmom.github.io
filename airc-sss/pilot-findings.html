The district study has not been run and no district findings exist. What follows is a single method pilot: one AI tester, two colleges, public pages only, twelve walks. Every severity rating below is a first-pass rating by an AI tester and has not yet been scored by the second, independent human rater the method requires. Treat each rating as a flag for fieldwork, not as a result.

What this pilot is, and what it is not

The study method is to walk the student journey the way a student walks it, from a felt need rather than a URL, and to record every barrier encountered. This page is the first live exercise of that method. It is deliberately small. One AI tester, two colleges, public pages only, no account and no login. The purpose is not to grade the two colleges. The purpose is to establish that the instrument produces concrete, decision-ready records, and to check whether the capture fields hold what the analysis will need.

What the pilot covered

  • Two colleges, Glendale and Mesa.
  • Public websites only, no login, navigated from the homepage.
  • Six tasks, each given as a problem the student feels, never as a link or an office name.
  • A preliminary Nielsen severity rating and an agent-readability note for each walk.

What the pilot did not cover

  • The authenticated journey: registration, holds, and Student Center tasks. That is where the deeper barriers usually sit, and it is unwalked here.
  • The other eight colleges.
  • Real-student think-aloud sessions to validate what the AI tester recorded.
  • The second independent severity rating and the adjudication step.
  • Governance review of the protocol, which is required before any logged-in walk.

The six tasks, as a student would feel them

  1. “I want to apply. How do I start?”
  2. “I am failing a class. Where do I get tutoring?”
  3. “When is the FAFSA deadline, and who do I ask?”
  4. “I think I need accommodations. Where do I go?”
  5. “I need food, or I cannot pay rent. Does the college help?”
  6. “Who is my advisor, and how do I get advising?”

No task named an office. The tester had to find the service the way a lost student would.

How severity and agent-readability were recorded

Severity is rated 0 to 4, following Nielsen: 0 not a usability problem, 1 cosmetic, 2 minor, 3 major, 4 catastrophic (a student cannot complete the task and would plausibly give up). A rating combines frequency, meaning how many students would meet the barrier, impact, meaning how hard it is to overcome once met, and persistence, meaning whether a student learns around it once or hits it again every time. The method requires two independent raters and an adjudication of disagreements. Only the first rating exists here.

Agent-readability asks a second question, which is whether a student’s own AI assistant could find and parse the answer, or whether the answer is trapped in a document, an unlabeled link, or behind a login. It is recorded as readable, partial, or poor.

Three moments, in the student’s voice

Severity numbers rank barriers. What makes them matter is the experience behind them. The three passages below are written by the AI tester in the voice of the persona it was carrying, and are illustrative narrations of the walks logged above. They are not quotations from real students, and no real student has yet been observed for this study.

FAFSA deadline, Mesa, preliminary severity 4

“Money is the whole reason I am scared about this. I find the financial aid page, and it says processing can take two to six weeks and that I should apply early. Early before what? There is no date. There is a phone number, but it is the weekend and I do not want to call a stranger to ask a question that should just be on the page. I close the tab.”

The most-asked aid question, with no findable answer. This is the clearest case in the pilot for a no-PII tool that simply states the deadline.

Tutoring, Glendale, preliminary severity 3

“I am failing math and I go looking for tutoring. It is not under Help, it is under Academics, which feels like it is for picking classes. I finally click Tutoring and the page is basically empty. It says to see the tiles below and there are no tiles. So now I do not know whether tutoring exists, or whether my phone is broken.”

Help that exists reads as help that is missing when the page will not render. A tool reading plain text would have found the hours.

Basic needs, Mesa, preliminary severity 3

“I cannot make rent and I am too embarrassed to say it out loud. I look for basic needs in the menu. It is not there. I end up on a large Get Help page and start clicking links that just say things like node-eight-one-five-eight. One of them turns out to be a food pantry called the Mesa Market. I would never have found that on my own.”

The pantry is real and good. It is buried behind unlabeled links a student in crisis will not dig through.

What the pilot suggests, provisionally

Two colleges is a small sample, and nothing here is generalizable to the district. What the pilot does show is that the capture fields hold the right things and that the walks produce specific, actionable records. Three shapes recur across the twelve walks, and each is a hypothesis the scaled study is built to test, not a conclusion.

The same service carries a different name at each college

Tutoring is the Center for Learning at Glendale and the Learning Enhancement Center at Mesa. Basic needs is GCC Cares at one and Student Care Services, with the Mesa Market pantry, at the other. A student who learns the word at one college cannot reuse it at the next. This is the service-crosswalk problem, observed live.

The help exists, and the label hides it

GCC Cares is a strong service sitting behind a sub-anchor under Student Life. Glendale’s FAFSA deadline is published, but not on the page literally named Important Dates. In the pilot the gap was almost never a missing service. It was that the student could not reach the service from the words they would use.

Answers are trapped where an assistant cannot reach them

Steps in a downloadable document, pages that render blank without JavaScript, and unlabeled system links are all invisible to a student’s AI assistant. As students arrive carrying their own assistants, those services become effectively unreachable for them.

The opportunities named in this pilot are factual and public: state the FAFSA deadline, surface tutoring hours, accept “I need food” in plain language and route to the pantry. None of that requires a name, a login, or a record, which keeps it inside the no-student-data line the district holds. It is also work that comes off staff desks. When the page answers the easy, repeated questions, the financial aid specialist and the advisor stop fielding them and the “we will get back to you” email shrinks, so the student with a genuinely complicated situation reaches a person sooner. Nobody is replaced. The routine work is what goes.

Honest limits

  • Two colleges of ten, one AI tester, one pass. These are illustrative patterns and not a district verdict.
  • Public pages only, by design. The authenticated journey (registration, holds, Student Center, naming an advisor) is unwalked, and it is where the deeper barriers usually live.
  • Severity is preliminary. Every rating is a single AI tester’s first pass, without the second independent rater and the adjudication the method requires, and without think-aloud validation from real students. A 4 here is a flag for fieldwork, not a final score.
  • Sites change. The pages were live when the pilot ran. A page that rendered blank or a date that was missing may already be fixed, which is itself worth re-checking.
  • Nothing named here is a built tool. The opportunities are candidate directions screened against the no-student-data line, and no pilot has been approved or built.
