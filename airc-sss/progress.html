Student Journey Gap Analysis

Case study · AI Tools & Strategy · UX Design

Overview Scope Method Synthetic students Ethics Progress Approach Build teams Roadmap

This is a formative study, not a summative one. It runs in rounds, and each round is analyzed before the next is designed, so what one round uncovers sets the question the next round asks. The log below is that record: what each round ran, what it established, and what it changed about the round after it. The findings themselves stay unpublished until the domain confirms them.

How the study runs

A study this size cannot be specified completely in advance, and pretending otherwise would be a design flaw rather than a virtue. The student journey spans ten colleges, forty-six task types, and several access tiers, and the barriers that matter are not knowable until testers start hitting them. So the study uses progressive focusing: a broad first sweep, then each subsequent round narrowed by what the previous one surfaced.

Three things are fixed and do not move between rounds. Every run returns the same findings schema, so runs stay comparable across rounds. Severity is always tester-assigned and pending human rating, never promoted by analysis. And evidence stays tiered: a page-opened run is quotable as a claim about content, a search-layer run only as a claim about retrieval. Those three constraints are what let the design flex without the corpus becoming incoherent.

Rounds are grouped into phases by access tier, because each tier needs its own authorization. Phase 1 is the public web and needs no login. Phase 2 is the signed-in journey and needs a sanctioned test account and a data-governance review. Phase 3 waits on the district platform. Phase 1 has run twice.

Round log

Round 1a · 27 July 2026 · broad sweep

Ran. A district-wide findability sweep, eleven persona runs, and three end-to-end walks of the full enrollment funnel.

Established. Thematic saturation appeared early: the same barrier types recurred across colleges within the first handful of runs, which said the instrument was working and that breadth was worth more than depth at this stage. The three funnel walks also located the access boundary precisely, all three stopping at the same wall in the same place, which is what let the study draw the line between Phase 1 and Phase 2 from evidence rather than from assumption.

Changed the next round. Saturation on the broad themes meant round 1b did not need to repeat them. It could go narrow, and test specific service paths with more scenarios per college.

Round 1b · 25 August 2026 · narrowed, and a method correction

Ran. Forty-four scenarios drawn from the bank, thirteen documented in full, plus five ten-college comparisons and two completed service-crosswalk rows.

Established. A single mechanism accounts for most of what both rounds found, and it is more useful than any individual barrier: retrieval matches vocabulary, not need. A page ranks on a student’s query when it contains the student’s word, and does not when it contains only the service’s name. The services exist. The language of need exists. They sit in different documents.

Changed the next round, twice over. First, a method correction. Four first-pass claims collapsed when the pages were actually opened, and all four had overstated the barrier. That produced the two-tier evidence rule now applied retrospectively across the whole corpus, plus two procedural rules for testers: browser verification is not optional, and click rather than retype, because transcribed URLs were producing false broken-link reports. Second, on-site search quality turned out to vary more between colleges than anything else measured, which added a new dimension to the service crosswalk that was not in the original instrument.

After 1b · the observation now shaping 1c

Both rounds so far tested one channel: the public college website. Analyzing them together surfaces an assumption underneath the whole design that has never been examined, and it is the study’s largest open threat to validity.

The study assumes a student with a problem goes to the website. That is likely true for some students and it is certainly not true for all of them. A student who needs help could reasonably start at any of these, and the corpus covers exactly one:

  • The public college website. Rounds 1a and 1b, the only channel tested.
  • The signed-in student portal. Phase 2, not started.
  • Canvas, where an enrolled student already spends their time and may reasonably expect to find everything else.
  • The chat widget on the college home page.
  • A student mobile app, if one exists at that college and if a student has any way of discovering it.
  • The phone.
  • A person. An instructor, a friend, a family member who went here.

In sampling terms, the channel is an uncontrolled variable and the sampling frame was never defined. Ranking barriers by severity on one channel, without knowing whether that channel is where students go, limits what the register can claim. It does not invalidate the findings. It bounds them: they are findings about the public web, on the public web.

The chat widget, which matters twice

Every college site carries a chat widget, already recorded as deployed infrastructure. In practice, using one means being asked for an MEID before it will answer, and that cuts in two directions.

As a barrier. A quick question stops being quick, and a prospective student who has no MEID yet cannot use it at all. That is precisely the Phase 1 population. The study has already found the MEID to be the silent gatekeeper of the enrollment funnel; a help surface that demands one puts the same wall in front of the help.

As a governance question. Requiring an MEID makes the exchange identified and attaches it to a student record. The domain’s standing constraint is that an AI fix collects no student data and no personally identifiable information. What a chat surface retains should be established before anything is plugged into it.

Round 1c, proposed scope

Proposed research question. Where do students actually go first when they hit a problem, and does the barrier they meet there differ from the one the website produces?

This is a channel-discovery round, and it is deliberately not more scenarios from the bank. More persona runs on the website would deepen a corpus that is already saturated on its main themes while leaving the assumption underneath it untested. The question is about behavior, so it needs behavioral evidence:

  • A direct question to students about where they go first, which the domain can add to work already planned.
  • What the existing chat widgets already log about the questions people bring them.
  • Call volume and question types at the offices that field them.
  • Whether a student mobile app exists at each college, and how a student would ever learn that it does.
  • What instructors are asked that has nothing to do with their course.

Naming this now, before a recommendation is funded, is considerably cheaper than discovering it afterward.
