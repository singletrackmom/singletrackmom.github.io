The inventory below is a working draft assembled from each college’s public website. It is not a verified or authoritative dataset. Each college representative confirms or corrects that college’s own column before any of it is treated as settled. No baseline numbers have been collected yet.

1. What this work produces

The work produces two linked deliverables. The first is an inventory of the landscape: which student-facing services each of the ten colleges offers, so that the domain holds one shared list of what exists. The second is a baseline: how many students use each of those services now, out of how many are enrolled. The baseline is the before-measure. It is captured now, tools are deployed, and any lift (more students reaching support, and more of them persisting) is measured against it later.

DeliverableWhat it covers
District tool inventoryThe systems every college shares: the student information system, Canvas, the financial aid system, the portal and login, and any district-licensed platforms. What everyone runs, and who owns each.
Campus tool inventoryThe platforms, practices, and vendor relationships an individual college has built that the others may not know exist. One college’s working early-alert workflow or career tool is another college’s missing piece.
The service crosswalkA grid of student-facing functions down the side and the ten colleges across the top, with each college’s own local name in the cell. At a glance, what exists where, and what is absent. The full grid is at the service crosswalk.
The usage baselineHow many unique students used each service in the last year, out of how many were enrolled. Counts and rates only, no student-level data.

2. An inventory, not a reorganization

Each of the ten Maricopa colleges is independently accredited, with its own programs, its own organizational structure, and its own names for things, and that arrangement stands. Nothing here asks any college to rename, align, or change anything. The work is to inventory which student-facing services exist at each college and how many students use each one.

The inventory comes first because the rest of the study depends on it. The personas cannot walk a journey without knowing which services a college offers. The baseline cannot be counted against a service list that does not exist. A pilot cannot be ranked by reach without knowing which colleges offer the service it would touch.

3. What the inventory surfaces

The inventory produces two kinds of finding.

  1. Hidden tools. A platform or program that one college already runs and the others do not know exists. Surfacing it allows a proven tool to spread, often at no new cost. BigInterview, the interview and resume platform, is the concrete case: Career Services at Glendale Community College has access to it, and whether the other nine colleges do is the question the inventory answers.
  2. Coverage gaps. “Not offered here” is a valid and useful answer. It surfaces both the cases where one college can share what it has and the gaps no college has solved, which are the ones that feed the recommendations.

5. The baseline

The inventory lists the services. The second half of this work counts how many students use each one, out of how many are enrolled. The measure is captured before deployment so that any change after deployment can be demonstrated. Without a baseline taken now, no lift can be shown later.

The baseline matters because current usage is likely to be low and is largely unmeasured. Only 55 percent of students say their college communicates support well, and at two-year colleges fewer than 10 percent of students with a disability receive accommodations, against 28 percent at four-year institutions. The services exist. The connection to them does not.

5.1 What is asked

At each college, one committee member sits down with the Dean of Student Life, the office that already oversees these services and holds the usage numbers. The conversation runs about fifteen minutes and covers the same questions each time.

  • How many unique students used each service last year.
  • What share of eligible students that represents, if it can be estimated.
  • How the office believes students find them, and how students actually do.
  • Where demand exceeds capacity.
  • What the office wishes students knew before they arrived.

Counts and estimates are sufficient. No student-level or identifying data is requested, and nothing is attributed to an individual. The framing is stated plainly in the conversation: this is a study of whether better student-to-service connection improves persistence, and it requires a starting number. It is not an audit and it is not a step toward replacing anyone. Automation is intended to take routine, repetitive work off staff so that human contact between students and the people who help them expands rather than contracts.

5.2 What the baseline gives the study

  • A district-comparable picture of who is reached now.
  • The denominator needed to rank pilots by student reach.
  • A before-number to measure usage and persistence gains against.
  • Early wins, where a service is strong at one college and missing at another.
  • The capacity ceilings that automation could help lift.

5.3 How the data is protected

  • Departments own their data. The study aggregates and never exposes.
  • No student PII. Counts and rates only.
  • What the district systems and institutional research already hold is confirmed before anything is duplicated.
  • Findings are shared back with departments before anything is final.

6. How the inventory gets done

  1. Assemble the first-pass service inventory from each college’s public website. Done.
  2. Recruit one representative per college.
  3. Each representative verifies and corrects only that college’s own column.
  4. Mark “not offered here” wherever it applies, as a finding rather than a blank.
  5. Lock a shared function list that the usability study and the recommendations build on.

6.1 Ground rules

  • No college is asked to rename or restructure anything.
  • Representatives own their column. The domain aggregates.
  • Local programs, including campus-specific basic-needs models, are captured as they are.
  • The map stays a living document as colleges change.

6.2 Status

  • First pass drafted from public websites, covering more than fifty functions across ten colleges. Unverified.
  • Next, confirm one representative per college.
  • Then, each representative verifies that college’s column and adds baseline numbers.

The working spreadsheet is the live version of everything above. Representatives open it, find their college, confirm or correct each service name, and add baseline numbers on the metrics tab. It is at the working spreadsheet. The evidence behind why any of this matters is at why students do not persist.
