Tester run sheet

Student journey study

Work·District barriers study·Study dashboard·Tester run sheet

The single sheet a tester reads before a session: the persona, the test login, the college, and the tasks to work in order.

No runs have been completed. This sheet is the instrument, not a record of results.

1. Three steps

  1. Read the assignment. The college representative supplies one persona, the test login created for that persona at that college, and the path. Testers run only the campus whose systems they can reach, because a login reaches only its own college.
  2. Work the tasks in order, out loud. Each task is a felt need, never a place to go. The tester starts where a student would start, usually a search on the college website, and finds their own way, narrating what they are looking for, what they expect a link to do, and why they clicked it. Insider knowledge is not used, and no one points the tester toward an office.
  3. Record each task: the outcome (success, partial success, failure), the time on task, the number of assists, the path taken, where the tester landed, the ease score, and a severity score. One record per task in the capture form.

One run is one persona at one college, attempted in character, in a single sitting of roughly fifteen minutes. The narration matters as much as the result, because it is what turns a wrong turn into an explanation of the wrong turn.

3. The persona

The tester stays in character. The persona is what meets the barriers.

Persona A, Marisol, 19

First generation, on a workforce path (Nursing or a CTE program), works about 25 hours a week. Lives with family, has no car some days, speaks Spanish at home, and navigates English jargon slowly.

Highest stakes: the application, MEID and login, financial aid (FAFSA and missing documents), placement, bilingual navigation, transportation, and food security.

Built-in barriers: she does not know the words prerequisite or bursar, her FAFSA is flagged for verification, she is placed into developmental math, and she gets a financial-aid hold.

Persona B, Darnell, 34

Returning adult, working full-time, two children, in a Business AA and transfer bound. He swirls. He has old credits, needs evening or online classes, and has no patience for systems that waste his time.

Highest stakes: transfer-credit evaluation, advising versus self-enrollment, enrolling at a second college, the aid consortium, registration conflicts, the payment plan, and career and transfer support.

Built-in barriers: old transcripts are not evaluated, he self-enrolls into the wrong courses, he hits a prerequisite block, his only free time is 9 p.m., and swirling brings a second application and a residency re-proof.

Persona C, Jax, 18

Direct from high school, undecided, on an exploratory or digital media path, uses they and them pronouns. They swirl. Digitally fluent but institutionally naive, and they assume everything works like an app.

Highest stakes: onboarding and orientation, knowing what to do first, advising while undecided, Canvas basics, belonging, disability accommodations, and mental health.

Built-in barriers: they skip orientation and miss steps, do not know who their advisor is, cannot tell counseling from advising, and need Disability Resources but do not know the term.

5. What every run records, and the severity scale

Every logged task carries the same fields: the outcome (success, partial success, or failure), the time on task in minutes, the number of assists, the barrier itself, a severity score, and an ease score. The ease score is the Single Ease Question, a 7-point rating of how easy or difficult the task felt, answered immediately after the task and before anything is written up.

Severity is rated 0 to 4, following Nielsen. A run in which nothing went wrong is a real result and is logged as 0.

ScoreMeaning
0, not a usability problemNothing went wrong. The task worked as it should.
1, cosmeticA small annoyance. The task still gets done.
2, minorNoticeable friction. Extra steps or a wrong turn, but the task gets done.
3, majorSerious difficulty. A human was needed, or the task took far longer than it should.
4, catastrophicA student cannot complete the task and would plausibly give up.

The score is not a measure of how irritating the barrier felt. It combines frequency, meaning how many students would meet this barrier, impact, meaning how hard it is to overcome once met, and persistence, meaning whether a student learns around it once or hits it again every time. Each rating is the first of two: a second rater scores the same finding independently and disagreements are adjudicated, because severity is a judgment and a single rater’s number is an opinion.

A service that does not exist at a college is also a finding. It is recorded as not offered there. Dead ends, confusing pages, and error messages are captured as screenshots.
