Test plan

Student journey study

← District barriers study overview

The tester instrument: one scenario, one campus, one sitting of about fifteen minutes, with every barrier recorded as it is met.

Nothing has been tested yet. No runs are complete. This page is the instrument, not a record of results.

1. Who runs the tests

Every district employee holds a Maricopa account, but no individual has access to the student-facing systems of all ten colleges. Access is scoped to a person’s own college. The members of the Student Support and Success domain are drawn from all ten colleges, so the domain membership constitutes the instrument: each member can test the campus whose systems that member can actually reach, which is the only practical route to coverage across all ten.

Volunteers take a single scenario in a single sitting, roughly fifteen minutes. The member opens the form, takes one assigned scenario, attempts it, and records what happened. The low burden is a deliberate response to volunteer capacity. No fixed tester count is set, and the design does not require one: coverage grows by adding personas, tasks, and colleges, not by putting more testers on the same task.

AI testers run the same scenarios in parallel to extend coverage beyond what volunteers can carry. Volunteers supply the human judgment and the authenticated, login-walled paths at the campus they can reach. AI testers carry breadth across the public paths at all ten.

The district advising tool (Salesforce) is not yet accessible to the domain. Scenarios that depend on it are deferred to a second wave, to be run during or after that tool’s pilot. This is a stated constraint on completeness, not an omission.

Student testers from the design studio are a possible addition in the fall. Nothing is arranged, and no student capacity is assumed in any plan on this site.

2. How a test works

One persona, one campus, one task, attempted from a real felt need. The tester is given a goal and never a destination, and no one points the tester toward an office, because findability is what is under test. The record captures where the tester got stuck, not only whether the task eventually succeeded.

Runs use a concurrent think-aloud protocol. The tester says what they are looking for, what they expect a link to do, and why they are clicking it, and writes that down while working. The narration is the data. It is what turns a wrong turn into an explanation of the wrong turn.

2.1 What every task records

  • The outcome: success, partial success (the service was reached but the task could not be finished), or failure.
  • Time on task, in minutes.
  • Assists: the number of times the tester needed help, or needed insider knowledge, to keep going.
  • The barrier: what went wrong, and where.
  • Severity, on the 0 to 4 scale in section 7.
  • Ease: the 7-point Single Ease Question in the form, answered immediately after the task.

The study also runs an expert heuristic evaluation of each college’s student-facing pages against Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics. That is a separate inspection and does not replace the walk described here. Inspection catches problems a tester routes around without noticing, and a walk catches failures no heuristic predicts.

3. The student account, not the work login

Every Maricopa employee also holds a student account. It signs in with the MEID and ends in maricopa.edu rather than the college work address. Testers use the student account so that they see what a student sees. A login reaches only its own college’s systems, which is why coverage has to be built campus by campus, from members who hold access at that campus.

4. Core task set

TaskWhat done looks like

Order an official transcriptThe tester reaches the transcript-ordering flow and sees the steps and the cost.
Apply for graduation and run a degree or certificate auditThe tester finds the graduation application and the audit that shows what remains.

6. Logging a run

Each task is recorded as a separate submission, immediately after the run, while it is fresh. One task equals one submission. Runs are logged in the capture form: open the capture form.

6.1 Logging rules

  • No student PII. Testers use their own student or test account, initials only, never a real student’s name or ID.
  • The record includes the barrier, the point at which the tester got stuck, the number of dead ends, whether a human was needed, the time on task, the ease score, and a severity score.
  • The local name the college uses for the service is captured as written. Names are translated, never standardized.
  • A point at which the persona would plausibly have given up is a finding. It is logged as such.

7. Severity scale

Every logged task carries a severity score. Severity is rated 0 to 4, following Nielsen. A run in which nothing went wrong is a real result and is logged as 0.

ScoreMeaning
0, not a usability problemNothing went wrong. The task worked as it should.
1, cosmeticA small annoyance. The task still gets done.
2, minorNoticeable friction. Extra steps or a wrong turn, but the task gets done.
3, majorSerious difficulty. A human was needed, or the task took far longer than it should.
4, catastrophicA student cannot complete the task and would plausibly give up.

The score is not a measure of how irritating the barrier felt. It is a judgment that combines three factors: frequency, meaning how many students would meet this barrier; impact, meaning how hard it is to overcome once met; and persistence, meaning whether a student learns around it once or hits it again every time. A small obstacle that every student meets on every visit can outrank a dramatic failure that almost no one reaches.

Each score is a first rating. A second rater scores the same finding independently, without seeing the first score, and disagreements are adjudicated in a short review, because severity is a judgment and a single rater’s number is an opinion.
