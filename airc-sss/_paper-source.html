Student Journey Gap Analysis

Student Support and Success Committee · AI Resource Center, Domain 5

Prepared by Michelle Blomberg, Co-chair, Student Support and Success domain, AI Resource Center.

Overview

This is a UX study of the student journey across all ten Maricopa colleges, which together serve roughly 140,000 students. It maps where students hit barriers as they move from first finding their Maricopa college, through applying, enrolling, taking classes, getting support, and belonging, to finishing and moving toward a job, then turns those barriers into prioritized, human-in-the-loop AI pilots. Every option is screened for responsible, minimal use of student data.

Students rarely leave because a policy was wrong. Far more often they leave for reasons of life and basic needs: they cannot get to campus, a family depends on them, they lose a job or cannot find one, or they simply cannot afford to stay, and roughly 43 percent of Maricopa students are food insecure (2023 Hope Center). Layered on top are the service barriers this study can observe within minutes of walking the journey: a student who cannot find an advisor, or cannot tell what dropping one class would do to their financial aid. Those barriers are invisible in aggregate data. So the study walks the journey. A tester, human or AI, is given a persona, a campus, and one thing that persona needs, and is never told where to go, because a student is never told where to go. What breaks, and how badly, is logged and scored, and a proposed fix is attached to each barrier: work an AI can absorb, or work that belongs to a person.

Study at a glance. 50 personas, 46 base tasks, 10 colleges, and coverage tested to saturation rather than to a fixed quota. Public, no-login Part 1 has begun and a first batch is logged and treated as preliminary; the authenticated and Salesforce runs have not started. A prior 2025 Glendale Community College Faculty Senate survey documented 83 barriers; it motivated this study and is background evidence only, not a finding of it.

Audience. The district, its students, and the administrators who decide where support goes. The work is run for the district AI Resource Center, which coordinates AI across the ten colleges.

Process. Research personas walk the live student journey one persona, one campus, one task at a time, thinking aloud, so a failure is attributable to a specific barrier. Every run records the outcome, the time on task, the assists needed, the barrier, an ease score, and a severity rating scored by at least three independent raters and reported as their mean. Barriers are affinity-mapped into themes, ranked on severity against reach, and mapped to a proposed fix. The full method is detailed under Method.

Goals

The committee works to improve student success, better grades, persistence, transfer, and jobs, by connecting students to the help they need at the moment they need it. Much of that help already exists but goes unfound. The study pursues four goals:

  1. Set the baseline. Count how many students use each student-services department now, by service and by college, to capture a starting picture of where students go today. This is a snapshot, not a scorecard. Raw office-visit counts are an imperfect measure of success: if the study succeeds and an AI tool answers routine advising, tutoring, or financial-aid questions, students can get help without ever booking a meeting or walking into an office, so office traffic could fall even as more students are helped. The baseline records current usage as a diagnostic of where students go, while help delivered through any future tool is counted entry by entry, so the study measures help reaching students across every channel, not just foot traffic. Measure: current usage by service and college, with unmeasured services flagged.
  2. Find and rank the barriers. Complete the Student Journey Gap Analysis across all ten colleges, a severity-ranked map of where students hit barriers along the sixty-plus touchpoints, from how a prospective student first finds their Maricopa college through to a job. Each barrier is rated 0 to 4 and ranked by how many students it touches. Measure: every barrier located, severity-rated, and ranked by reach.
  3. Close the highest-priority gaps. For the top-ranked barriers, deliver AI tools, and where the fix is a process rather than a tool, workflow changes, that connect students to help, enrollment, class success, and jobs faster. Workflow changes are led by staff: the ARC team and the department that owns the service consult on them. Success is whether the student’s need was met, how easily and how fast, and whether the student persisted, across whatever channel served them, with help delivered through a tool counted as help delivered. Measure: fixes delivered against the top barriers each cycle, each piloted with a defined success metric.
  4. Build with students and share. Paid multidisciplinary student teams build the tools, not the staff workflows, and what works is shared across the ten colleges. Measure: three student teams, twelve students, building tools; early-adopter practices published district-wide; and a formal pilot-to-production process in place before the first pilot.

The committee also supports related work, such as student AI training, which it co-supports with the Teaching and Learning domain and may serve as testers for, but these four goals are its central charge.

Scope

The study covers the student-services journey across all ten Maricopa colleges: the sixty-plus touchpoints a student passes through from first finding their Maricopa college, through applying, enrolling, paying, taking classes, getting support, and belonging, to finishing and moving toward a job. It maps the services that already exist and where students hit barriers reaching them. It translates what each college calls the same service and asks no college to rename anything; it is an inventory, not a reorganization.

What the study does not cover. The study follows the student-services journey, finding and reaching advising, financial aid, basic needs, career services, and the rest, up to and around the classroom. It does not go inside Canvas or the coursework itself. The in-course experience belongs to the Teaching and Learning domain, Domain 2, so that part of the student journey is deliberately not covered here.

What this is

The four tracks

The four initiatives the study is organized into.

P1

Map the landscape

What each college calls the same service, and which tools exist where. More than forty functions across ten colleges. The study translates the names and asks no college to change them.

P2

The barriers study

Expert heuristic evaluation plus task-based usability testing. One persona, one campus, one task, thinking aloud. Every barrier logged and rated for severity, 0 to 4, following Nielsen. The scenario bank is the backbone: 50 personas and 46 base tasks across 10 colleges, with each service walked by enough personas to reach saturation.

P3

Staff workflows and usage

The backstage half. How many students reach each service, how staff actually do the work, and where a practice at one college could be adopted by the others.

P4

Pilots and workflow revisions

Each barrier becomes a candidate fix, ranked by how many students it reaches. A fix may be a shared workflow rather than a tool. Anything that would collect student data is disqualified before it is considered, not after.

Students as partners from the start

This domain exists to serve students, so students help shape it from the beginning, not only as testers at the end. Partnership is built into each stage: students help surface the barriers they actually hit, co-design and test the tools, and give feedback on every pilot before it scales. The people who live the student journey are best placed to find where it breaks.

Some initiatives are student-led, with faculty and staff acting as mentors rather than directors. Findability testing, whether a student can locate a service, or find Maricopa at all, is work students are well placed to lead, as is the peer-to-peer outreach that carries the effort across campuses. Staff guide and unblock; students run the work and own the finding.

Participation is kept light and task-based, because students cannot commit to a full meeting schedule on top of the work. A student might join one think-aloud session, test a few pages, or give a round of feedback, rather than sit on a standing committee. In the near term the domain opens these opportunities through the college experiential learning committees and invites volunteers for specific, bounded tasks, which also fits the civic and volunteer hours some students actively seek. A standing Student AI Group, formed through student government, would give this domain and the other AI Resource Center domains a durable pool to draw on rather than assembling students from scratch each time.

Paid roles remain a possibility where the work is sustained or skilled, an internship, work-study, or part-time position, if funding can be found. Nothing in the plan waits on that: the study proceeds on volunteer and service participation, and treats paid roles as an addition rather than a prerequisite.

The staff side of the journey

Not every gap needs a new tool. Some practices already exist within the district, and part of the work is helping them travel so more students benefit.

A student-facing barrier often has a staff-facing cause, and staff across the colleges carry real and varied demands. The working hypothesis of this study, and it is a hypothesis rather than a judgment of any office, is that good practices already exist somewhere in the district. Leaders across the ten colleges already meet and share, and the aim here is to make that sharing more systematic, surfacing what works and helping it reach more students, with no assumption that any college is doing a worse job than another. Each college has developed its own services, names, and tools.

So the study asks two things of the ten colleges alongside the persona runs.

  1. A call to student services leaders for their AI early adopters. Any advisor, financial aid specialist, or tutor who has already automated part of their own workflow, drafted an agent, or streamlined a process is asked to describe it. The study aggregates those workflows and publishes them across the ten colleges. A workflow that already works at one college is the cheapest win available, and it requires nobody to buy anything.
  2. Direct observation, if the call turns up nothing. Domain members divide the ten colleges and sit with advisors and tutors while they work, which is contextual inquiry rather than a survey, because staff cannot reliably report the steps they have automated in their own heads. The question being answered is narrow: which parts of this work are repetitive enough that an agent could carry them, so the staff member gets more time in front of a student.

The routine reply that reads “make an appointment and we will see you in two days” is a good example of the target. It is a barrier from the student’s side, and, we would expect, a sign of how much routine demand competes for staff time. Some portion of what sits behind that reply is a factual question with a factual answer, and a student who receives that answer immediately may not need the appointment at all, which returns the appointment slot to the student whose problem actually requires a person. Nobody is replaced. The routine work is what goes.

The output of this track is therefore two kinds of recommendation, and the study treats them as equal: workflow revisions that can be shared across the district at no cost, and new tools built or adopted only where no existing practice closes the gap.

Staff participation and trust

The staff whose participation the study depends on are also those most affected by how it is conducted.

Skepticism about AI among student services staff is not an obstacle to route around. It is a rational response to a technology that has been introduced elsewhere as a substitute for people, and it is the single largest risk to this study. A domain member who believes the outcome is a smaller department will not describe their own workflow honestly, and the study depends entirely on their describing it honestly.

Three commitments hold, and they are stated to staff before any observation is scheduled.

  1. The metric the study is trying to raise is human contact. If a recommendation reduces the number of students who reach a person, it has failed on its own terms, whatever else it improves.
  2. What automation removes is the repetitive factual work: the same question answered for the four hundredth time, the appointment-booking email, the routine status check. What it returns is time in front of a student. A department that reports no reduction in routine work after a pilot is reporting a finding, and the study records it as one.
  3. Staff are the authors of the fixes, not their subjects. An early-adopter workflow is published under the office that built it, and no observation is used to evaluate an individual’s performance. The study has no access to personnel data, and does not want any.

Making participation visible

This kind of recognition already has a precedent here. The district’s accessibility initiative last year handed out a stress ball for completing the training, and the honest first reaction was resistance, because nobody wanted extra work on an already full plate. The small prize at the end still gave people a reason to start, and a way to see who else had taken part.

The same light touch can work for this, with a token of its own rather than the accessibility stress ball: a certificate or a small object that stands in for AI at Maricopa as something familiar rather than something to fear, given out at moments of participation so that taking part feels normal and visible across the ten colleges.

What matters most is the message that recognition carries, on the certificate, on the screens already running across the campuses, and from the people handing it out. AI is here to make your job easier, not to take it. That fear, that AI will be used to cut positions, is the real barrier, and it has to be met directly rather than talked around. It echoes the commitments above: the metric the study raises is human contact, and what automation removes is the repetitive work, not the person doing it. The recognition simply lowers the temperature, so staff feel invited into the work rather than measured by it.

Students build the pilots

Student teams build the tools against a real user, a real constraint, and a real deliverable.

The tools that come out of this study are built by three multidisciplinary student teams, four students each, twelve in total, working the way a design studio works: a brief, a client, a deadline, and a deliverable someone is waiting for. The teams are assembled later rather than this fall, because there is nothing to build until the gap analysis has run and the barriers are ranked. The target is to bring them together around November so they are ready to build by January 2027. Students are selected directly by the domain co-chairs, so finding students is not the constraint. Each team is assembled across disciplines because the work is multidisciplinary, and the exact mix is not set in stone. It draws on whichever programs fit the tool, for example computer information systems and information technology students for the data, the integration, and what it takes to run inside an institution, and digital media students for the interface and the language a student actually reads. Other disciplines are pulled in as a given build needs them.

They leave with something rare in a portfolio: a shipped tool with a real user, a real constraint, and an evidence base behind it.

The approach applies to students the same principle the study applies to staff: the people closest to the problem help build the answer. A student who has been lost in the financial aid pages understands the barrier directly, and a student who then builds the tool that closes it gains applied experience in design, accessibility, and consequence.

Students build the tools, not the workflows. When a fix is a change to how a department redesigns its own process around a tool, that redesign is led by the committee, working directly with the department’s own staff and other AI experts as needed.

The rules this study runs on

Four constraints govern the design of the study.

  • Problems, not destinations. A tester is given a need, never an office. A tester who cannot find advising has recorded a finding.
  • Translate, do not standardize. Every college keeps its own names and programs. The study maps them to one another and asks no college to align.
  • Minimal data. Runs are recorded against a scenario identifier and initials, not a student’s identity. Every AI option is screened for responsible, minimal use of student data, so the preferred path is building data-sovereign tools rather than buying data-collecting ones.
  • How the tests are run, in three parts by access. A synthetic student uses a web browser the way any external member of the public would, with no special access. Part 1 is public findability, which needs no login at all and runs now. Part 2 is the authenticated flows, run on a single sanctioned OIT test account that is not the author’s own student record, operated with permission and watched while it runs; enrolling and getting into classes is part of this walk-through, so the account is applied, enrolled, and registered as a real student would be. Part 3 is the Salesforce-dependent tasks, run during or after Salesforce’s own pilot. Nothing in the test requires paying a bill: the walk-through stops at any payment screen.
  • No one is replaced. Automation takes the repetitive factual questions off advising and financial aid staff so their hours go to the students with real problems. Human contact is the measure the study is trying to raise.

Synthetic students run the findability tasks in parallel, which is how coverage extends to every task at every campus, further than volunteers alone could reach.

The problem this study addresses

Findability, not availability.

Across the ten Maricopa colleges, the support that would keep students enrolled already exists on every campus. The gap is findability. Students cannot reach advising, financial aid, basic needs, accommodations, or the right person at the moment they need them.

A study with three personas walking thirty journeys can describe that problem. It cannot map it at the resolution the committee needs in order to prioritize where AI should close the gaps. The method below specifies a structured, repeatable instrument designed to produce a college-by-college, stage-by-stage map of where students hit barriers, with a severity judgment attached to each one.

Two methods, run together

Inspection and task testing surface different defects, and neither substitutes for the other.

  1. Expert heuristic evaluation. Three to five trained human evaluators, calibrated in a short norming session on a shared reference interface, inspect each college’s student-facing interfaces independently against Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics before merging their findings, recording each violation with its location and a severity rating. This is a human inspection method. No student, persona, or agent is involved.
  2. Task-based usability testing. A tester, human or AI, adopts a persona and walks a real journey on the live systems, attempting one task from a felt need and narrating while working, a concurrent think-aloud protocol.

Both are needed. A heuristic evaluation catches violations a tester will silently work around without ever reporting them, and task testing exposes failures no heuristic predicts, such as a service that exists but is named something the persona would never search for.

For public findability, the study also uses first-click testing, which needs no login and records whether the tester’s very first click lands on the right path as they navigate the live site. After each task a human tester answers the Single Ease Question, a seven-point rating of how easy the task felt; agent runs do not record it, since an ease self-report needs a person’s felt experience. The findings are placed on journey maps and, for the worst cases, service blueprints that draw the frontstage a student sees against the backstage staff actions behind it. Remote unmoderated testing is used where it fits, so testers can work across all ten colleges without travel. Nielsen Norman Group and MeasuringU are the method sources; see the references.

Synthetic testers carry the public findability tasks at scale, and the authenticated tasks run on one sanctioned test account. An AI tester works a college’s live site as a persona and reports where it breaks, which is also the agent-readability measure. The public findability tasks need no login, and the synthetic students run them across every persona, task, and college. The authenticated tasks that make up most of the journey are run on the single OIT-controlled test account, a sanctioned account that is no real person’s record and holds no real student data, operated with OIT’s permission and oversight. Because signed-in wayfinding is largely the same whichever persona is carrying it, that one staged account covers the authenticated journey without a separate login per persona. The onboarding steps, applying, first login, enrolling, and orientation, are walked on the public site and, where a signed-in step is involved, on the test account, which OIT stages and resets so the walk-through can be repeated.

Synthetic students, primary layer with a validation gate

Synthetic students are the primary layer of the findability study. Each is an AI agent carrying one whole persona, instantiated from the fixed set of 50 persona profiles rather than invented on the fly, so the demographic proportions that make the sample representative are preserved. Each agent adopts one persona’s situation, constraints, and goal, then attempts the public, unauthenticated findability tasks. They are the primary layer because they are consistent, scalable, need no login for the public findability tasks, and can walk every service at every campus with enough personas to reach saturation, a reach human testers could not cover. The public findability runs touch no logged-in system, and any authenticated run uses the single OIT test account, so no real student data is ever recorded. These are AI agents built from persona traits, not static personas: each one runs autonomously, browses the live public site, and returns a structured, inspectable finding. Appendix G, What makes each synthetic student a complete agent, spells out how the agent is constrained to the persona, the prompt scaffold it runs on, and a worked example.

The design is many specialists coordinated by one orchestrator, not a single generalist. Each of the 50 personas is its own specialist agent, a specific student held whole and never split into traits, lenses, or sub-models. A single orchestrator agent sits above them and coordinates the runs: it sequences the journey so a persona gets in the door before it tries to pay, decides which persona, task, and college to run next, and routes each run to the right specialist and records what comes back. A set of narrow specialists with a coordinator on top is more reliable than one generalist agent asked to be every student at once, because each specialist stays inside one student’s knowledge and limits while the orchestrator owns sequence and coverage. This architecture is informed by Western Governors University’s synthetic course-validation model (Western Governors University, synthetic course validation, Open edX 2026 conference presentation, in the references).

Validating AI findings against real users is standard practice, and this study builds that in through a validation gate. A small online validation sample, about one tester per campus and roughly ten in all, runs the same online findability tasks the synthetic students run, and the synthetic findings are checked against that human sample. The validation sample is deliberately much smaller than the synthetic scale; the human and synthetic runs are compared on the task-college cells they share, using percent agreement on whether a barrier is present and a kappa on the severity band; the synthetic runs scale to full coverage only if that agreement clears a pre-set threshold. Where the overlap is too thin to compute a stable statistic, the check flags gross divergence rather than certifying agreement, and synthetic output is treated only as hypotheses to test, never as results. Synthetic findings stay candidates until the human sample validates them. Nothing here overclaims what an AI tester can establish on its own.

Why synthetic-first testing holds up

The method is chosen on evidence, and the evidence cuts both ways. On the encouraging side, LLM agent frameworks now run usability testing directly on live web designs, simulating a user who browses a real interface and reports where it breaks, which is the kind of live-site task this study asks of its synthetic students (UXAgent, arxiv.org/html/2504.09407v2). In a paired study, AI personas detected roughly 73 to 77 percent of known usability issues, at times matching or beating expert panels, with about 75 percent attitudinal correlation and 92 percent agreement on themes with real users (Looma.ai study, dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3788149.3788233). Comparative work found GPT-4o reaching about 84 percent agreement with human evaluators on issue detection, a Cohen kappa around 0.50, but its severity judgments were unreliable and needed human oversight, which is precisely why humans own severity in this study (arxiv.org/pdf/2506.16345). Other studies report that LLM agents complement rather than replace human usability testing and overlap broadly on issues that are not identity-specific (dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3801978), and that LLMs uncover design issues with consistency, particularly at the early stages of a design (link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-30044-7_9).

The limits are just as clear. In education, simulated students are a valuable framework for modeling diverse learners, yet a recent review found nearly half of simulated-learner studies report no formal validation, so this study’s explicit validation step puts it ahead of the field (simulating-students review, arxiv.org/abs/2511.06078). Validating AI findings against real users is standard practice, and this study builds that in through the validation gate.

Taken together, the literature points to exactly the design this study uses: lead with synthetic students for reach, then gate on human agreement. The validation gate is where that lands. A small online validation sample, about one tester per campus and roughly ten in all, runs the same online tasks as the synthetic students, and coverage scales only if agreement holds. Humans confirm reality and set severity, since that is the judgment the evidence says AI is least reliable at. That small human sample is a spot-check that can flag gross divergence, not a statistical validation, and it is the fallback the plan keeps ready if the synthetic layer does not hold up.

Testing and keeping the agents reliable

Because the synthetic students are agents, they are held to the same testing discipline any conversational agent is held to. This is established engineering practice adapted to the synthetic students, not a one-off, and it is what keeps a finding from resting on a single lucky or unlucky run.

  • A golden set. The study keeps a small, growing set of known barriers, including the ones the real-student validation confirms, that every version of an agent is re-run against. It is the fixed check that a fix or a model change cannot silently reintroduce a miss. When an agent misses or misreads something, that case is added to the golden set and guarded against from then on, so the set grows as the study learns.
  • Simulation runs, not single prompts. Each scenario is a full task walk-through: the agent starts from a felt need and works the whole task on the live site, click by click, rather than answering one prompt. Every scenario carries a defined success condition, so a run either meets it or does not.
  • Multiple runs per scenario. Because an agent can answer differently from one run to the next, each scenario is run several times rather than once. Repetition measures consistency, surfaces edge cases a single pass would miss, and lets the study report a confidence rather than a single anecdote. Low agreement across runs of the same scenario is itself a flag worth investigating.
  • Versioning. The agent template is versioned, so a change is isolated and its effect is comparable against the version before it, and every result is tied to the template version that produced it.
  • Live and pilot evaluation. Once a fix or a tool is piloted, real interactions are evaluated against the same success criteria used in simulation, which is standard agent-testing practice carried from the lab into the pilot.

Structured output, versioning, and where the model runs

Every synthetic-student run returns its finding in a defined JSON output schema, so results are machine-readable and identical in shape from one run to the next: the barrier, its location, the path taken, the local service name, the severity flag, and the persona and campus that produced it. The output of the study is therefore not prose to be re-keyed but a structured barrier dataset the committee can sort, count, and compare across colleges.

The persona and cohort definitions are versioned, so any finding can be traced to the exact persona build and template that produced it and re-run later against the same definition. Together with the golden set and the multiple-run discipline, this gives the study pre-launch quality-assurance documentation: a record of what was tested, against which cohort, and how consistently, before any tool is put in front of a student.

The method is model-agnostic by design and built to run on a fine-tuned small language model kept inside district control, so student-facing testing never sends data to an outside service, in keeping with the district’s data-sovereignty position. The current pilot uses prompt-scaffolded agents on a sanctioned model with the human validation gate; moving to a fine-tuned small model is the production path as training data and platform support allow.

The persona-per-test model

The unit of the task-based study is a single run: one persona, at one campus, attempting one task. The tester opens the instrument and receives a brief stating who the persona is, what barriers the persona carries, the one thing the persona is trying to do, and the campus. The tester then attempts the task from a real felt need, starting where a student would start, usually a search on the college website.

The protocol

Runs use a concurrent think-aloud protocol. The tester narrates while working, saying what they are looking for, what they expect a label to mean, and why they are clicking what they click. The narration is the data. It is what turns a wrong turn into an explanation of the wrong turn.

The tester is given a goal and never a destination, and the facilitator does not prompt toward a service, name an office, or confirm that a page is the right page. Telling a tester where to go destroys the finding the run exists to make, because findability is precisely what is under test. The facilitator intervenes only to prevent a genuine dead stop, and each intervention is recorded as an assist.

Why a single task per run

A single task isolates one barrier, so a failure is attributable to a specific point in the journey. It also keeps runs short, parallelizable, and easy to assign to many testers at once, which is what coverage requires.

Why a persona library rather than a few personas

A page that works for a digitally fluent transfer student can still fail a phone-first, Spanish-dominant parent on a slow connection. Only a varied library surfaces those intersections. The 50 personas reflect who actually enrolls: ESL and non-native speakers, veterans, autistic and ADHD students, food-insecure and first-generation students, working parents, returning adults, students who need Disability Resources, swirling multi-campus students, and dual-enrollment high schoolers.

Severity

Rated 0 to 4, following Nielsen. A run that finds nothing wrong is a real result and is logged as 0.

ScoreMeaning
0, not a usability problemNothing went wrong. The task worked as it should.
1, cosmeticA small annoyance. The task still gets done.

3, majorSerious difficulty. A human was needed, or the task took far longer than it should.
4, catastrophicA student cannot complete the task and would plausibly give up.

A severity score is not a measure of how irritating a barrier felt. It is a judgment that combines three factors: frequency, meaning how many students would meet this barrier; impact, meaning how hard it is to overcome once met; and persistence, meaning whether a student learns around it once or hits it again every single time. A small irritation that every student meets on every visit can outrank a dramatic failure that almost no one encounters.

Inter-rater reliability

Severity ratings are subjective, and a single rater’s number is an opinion rather than a measurement. At least three raters therefore score each finding independently, without seeing each other’s score, and the reported severity is the mean, following Nielsen Norman Group’s guidance, shown with the median and the min-to-max spread so the coarseness of a 0 to 4 scale stays visible. Inter-rater agreement is computed with Krippendorff’s alpha for ordinal data. A short adjudication is used only to tighten the definitions, not to overwrite the mean, and the definitions above are refined wherever raters keep diverging. This is what allows the committee to compare a severity 3 at one college with a severity 3 at another and treat them as the same claim. Every AI severity rating is treated as preliminary until human raters confirm it, and a synthetic rating never stands as a final score on its own.

Sample size

The study is formative. Its purpose is to find and fix defects, not to estimate a population parameter, so the sample is sized for defect discovery. A small number of testers per persona segment surfaces the large majority of the issues that are discoverable at all, and each additional tester on the same task returns fewer new defects than the one before. Adding a fifth or a tenth tester to the same task at the same college mostly re-finds what has already been found.

Coverage is therefore scaled on the axes that return new defects, which are personas, tasks, and colleges, and not by piling testers onto the same task. Synthetic students run the findability tasks in parallel, which extends coverage along those axes rather than deepening a sample that is already saturated. No fixed tester count is promised anywhere in this study, and none is needed for the design to hold.

One caveat is stated plainly. The often-cited “five users” rule applies to qualitative insight only, finding the main usability problems in a formative study. Comparative or metric work, where the goal is to measure a rate or compare two designs with confidence, needs a larger sample, roughly 20 to 40 participants. This study is formative and uses the five-users logic for its qualitative findability findings, and it does not report metric comparisons on small samples. See Nielsen Norman Group, why you only need to test with five users, in the references.

Piloting the protocol

Before any district-wide fieldwork, the whole protocol is dry-run at a single college: the brief, the think-aloud, the capture form, the severity definitions, and the multi-rater step. The dry run is a test of the instrument, not of the college. It answers whether the tasks are legible to a tester who is not the author, whether the fields capture what the analysis needs, and whether the raters can agree. Any wording that failed is repaired before the other nine colleges are touched.

Analysis

Findings are analyzed in three passes.

  1. Affinity mapping. Every logged barrier is written up as an observation and grouped with related observations until themes emerge from the data rather than from the categories the study started with. A theme is a pattern that holds across personas and colleges. Severity is rated per finding and rolls up to the theme as the median of its member findings; reach is estimated per theme, so severity and reach are compared at the same, theme, level.
  2. Severity by reach. Each theme is placed on a matrix of severity against reach, where reach is estimated from the eligible population for that service and the observed findability-failure rate, against the district’s roughly 140,000 students. Severity and reach are plotted, not multiplied into a single score, and a reach value is flagged as an estimate wherever the underlying count is not tracked. High severity and high reach is where the first pilots go. High severity and low reach is documented and routed, not automated first.
  3. Journey mapping and service blueprinting. Themes are placed on the student journey to show where in the arc from application to completion a student is lost, and the worst are blueprinted, which means drawing the frontstage the student sees against the backstage staff actions that produce it. The blueprint is what makes the automation argument concrete, because the routine, repetitive backstage steps are exactly what an AI can absorb so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours on the students with real problems. No one is replaced.

Human and AI testers

Public findability tasks need no login, and the single OIT-controlled test account reaches all ten colleges on one login, so one account covers the authenticated journey at every campus. Synthetic students therefore carry the breadth: they walk the public, no-login paths across all ten colleges and produce a first map of every task at every campus quickly. The members of the Student Support and Success domain, drawn from several of the ten colleges, bring local knowledge, the human validation of the synthetic layer, and the people who do the in-person walk-ins.

Human volunteers take a single scenario in a single sitting, roughly fifteen minutes: a member opens the form, takes one assigned scenario, attempts it, and records what happened. The low burden is a deliberate response to volunteer capacity. Humans do the 80 in-person walk-ins, the authenticated-navigation sample on the single OIT-controlled test account, and the validation sample that checks the synthetic findings. They are the check on the synthetic layer, not the coverage engine.

Authenticated tasks are run on the single OIT-controlled test account, so testers see what a student sees without any real student data being touched. Student testers from the design studio are a possible addition in the fall. Nothing is arranged, and no plan on this site counts them as capacity.

Operations, tracking, and coverage

One scenario per tester, no duplication, every result logged, and a clear view of what remains.

  1. The bank is the single source of truth for assignment. Each scenario carries an ID. A college representative assigns one scenario to one tester by entering initials and a date on that row, so nothing is run twice and coverage is visible in the sheet itself.
  2. The capture form records a run: persona, college, task, path taken, outcome, time on task, assists, the barrier, severity, and the Single Ease Question score. It captures results well but does not assign work, so it stays as the capture instrument and its entries key back to the Scenario ID.
  3. The coverage dashboard reads the bank and shows, per college and per journey stage, how much has been walked and where the gaps are. It is how the co-chairs and the steering committee see progress without reading the sheet.

Recommendation: keep the existing capture form for recording results, and use the scenario bank as the assignment and coverage layer above it. This avoids building a new assignment system, reuses the live form, and keeps one ID linking a planned scenario to its logged result.

How we get the baseline numbers

To measure improvement we first need to know how many students use each service now. That number is not held in one place, so we gather it directly.

  1. Use the service crosswalk (Appendix B) as the list of departments to contact at each college.
  2. For each student-services department at each college, identify the head or director who owns the usage metric.
  3. The co-chair sends the standard request letter (below) to that person, asking for the past year’s unique students served, total visits or contacts, how they track it, and whether they track by individual student or only in aggregate.
  4. Record every response in a baseline sheet by service and college. Where a department does not track usage, record that; an unmeasured service is itself a finding.
  5. Follow up with non-responders after two weeks.

Expected roadblock: some departments do not track usage individually, and in some the owner of the number is unclear, so part of this step is discovering who holds the metric and whether it exists at all.

Ethics and responsible use

The study is built so that no real student is ever tested, no real student data is ever touched, and a human makes every decision the AI is not qualified to make. The safeguards are gathered here, though each also appears in the section it governs.

  • No real students, no real student data. The testers are synthetic agents built from aggregate enrollment demographics, not real people. Public findability runs on public pages with no login; the authenticated journey, including logging into the student information system to enroll, the student intranet, and the signed-in help paths, runs on a single sanctioned OIT-controlled test account that is no real person’s record and holds no real student data.
  • The model stays inside the district. The method is model-agnostic and built to run on a small language model kept in district control, so student-facing testing never sends data to an outside service. The study is led by the Student Support and Success domain (Domain 5), and its data-governance and security decisions are worked with the Strategy, Security and Governance domain (Domain 1).
  • Humans make the judgment calls. AI findings are provisional. At least three raters set each severity independently and the mean is reported, and a small human validation sample re-runs the same tasks to confirm the synthetic findings before coverage scales. The AI never sets a final severity or stands as a result on its own.
  • Transparency to staff. Before any run touches a department, its director is notified in advance, and every form or message the test account submits into a real staff inbox carries a clear line stating it is a test from a test student account for the district ARC Student Journey Barriers study, not a real student request.
  • Access is phased and gated. Part 1 touches nothing beyond public pages and runs under the tri-chairs’ approval already in hand; touching any district system is cleared by Domain 1 first; the deepest access waits on Salesforce being live and Domain 1’s sign-off. The method is audited between phases on the safest data before it escalates.
  • Research permission and human-subjects care. For the human-facing parts, the online validation sample and the in-person office walk-ins, the study will confirm with the college and the district whether the work requires Institutional Review Board (IRB) review or qualifies as exempt usability testing, and will complete that determination before any human testing begins. Human participants give informed consent, and in-person visits are conducted openly.
  • Minimal data, no PII. The study records counts and rates only, never personally identifiable information and nothing FERPA-protected. The barrier dataset lives on the district GitHub account the domain co-chair runs, with ARC access; departments own their own numbers, and the study aggregates and never exposes.
  • No one is replaced. Every fix takes routine, repetitive work off advising, financial-aid, and support staff so their time goes to the students who need a person, and the study weighs the compute cost of what it recommends, favoring the lightest option that solves the problem.

Modality and the online learner. The students balancing the most, meaning work, caregiving, and distance, disproportionately enroll online, and online and distance students are a distinct population: older, more often working, and disproportionately parents and caregivers. The typical community college student is about 28, and student-parents average about 35 (Lumina Foundation, Today’s Students). More than half of online learners have a child under 18 (BestColleges, Adult Learners). About 26 percent of community college students are parents, roughly 30 percent of all enrolled students are parents or caregivers, and about 42 percent of associate-degree seekers are parents (Institute for Women’s Policy Research). About 79 percent of part-time adult undergraduates are employed (BestColleges). Student-parents carry on average about 74 fewer discretionary hours per week, and roughly 82 percent live in households below $30,000 in income (IWPR). For many students whose work, family, or transportation rule out attending in person, distance education is what makes enrollment possible at all (The Conversation, distance learning changes lives).

The modality split built into the personas. Each of the 50 personas carries a study modality, all online, some online, or all in person, assigned to fit that student’s real situation rather than at random. Full-time working parents, adult returners juggling a job, students with transportation or childcare barriers, students in programs commonly delivered online, and swirl-to-Rio students are the online ones; recent high-school graduates living at home, dual-enrollment students, and hands-on lab and clinical programs are on campus. The mix is proportioned to a national public two-year baseline, since Maricopa does not publish its own split: roughly a quarter of students exclusively online, roughly another quarter mixing online and in person, and about 45 percent entirely in person. Across the 50 personas this yields 13 all online, 14 some online, and 23 all in person, so most personas take at least some coursework online. This proportion is a placeholder to be confirmed with Maricopa Institutional Research. A fully online persona cannot come to campus, so any service offered only in person is effectively unreachable for that student, which is itself a barrier the study is built to surface.

Testing order and prerequisites

The scenarios follow the student journey, and many of them carry a prerequisite, because a student cannot pay a bill before enrolling or reach the learning management system before registering. Each scenario is tagged with the account state it requires: none, a public task any prospective student can attempt; applied, an admitted account with a login; or enrolled, a class on the record. The bank is ordered so that testing moves through the journey in sequence. The account state each scenario requires is recorded in the scenario-bank workbook.

Authenticated testing runs on a single OIT-controlled test account, a sanctioned account that is no real person’s record and holds no real student data, not even a domain member’s. OIT creates, stages, resets, and deletes it under its own rules. The study is organized into two testing lanes.

Public findability, no login. Synthetic and human testers attempt the public college website without signing in, testing whether a student can find and follow the path to a service. The onboarding tasks, applying, first login, first-time enrollment, and orientation, are tested here as findability: whether a newcomer can locate and understand the steps. Synthetic testers carry this lane at scale, running personas against every college’s public site, which makes it the central lane for onboarding coverage.

Authenticated navigation on the OIT test account. Signed in on the single OIT-controlled test account, testers check whether, once inside, they can reach the advisor, the payment page, aid status, add and drop, transcripts, and the accommodations request. OIT stages the account into the state each task needs, applied, enrolled, or registered, so the signed-in journey can be walked without touching any real student data. The walk-through stops at any payment screen; no bill is ever paid. The record keys to the scenario, not to a person.

First-time actions, applying, first login, and first-time enrollment, are walked on the public site as findability, and the signed-in steps are run on the test account, which OIT resets so onboarding can be repeated. Where possible, a short think-aloud with a small number of real incoming students adds a human check on the onboarding experience.

The single test account reaches all ten colleges on one login, so it verifies the same signed-in path at every college, while the public findability needs no login at all. Together, that is what lets the synthetic layer reach saturation across every service at every campus.

Before the journey starts: how students find Maricopa

The study maps what happens once a student has decided a community college is the answer. For many, the harder gap comes one step earlier: they do not yet recognize their own situation as something a community college is for. A person whose job is going nowhere does not search for the nearest community college. They ask a search engine or an AI assistant how to earn more, how to change careers, whether a trade pays. Whether any Maricopa college surfaces in that moment, against private and for-profit institutions that spend heavily to be found first, is itself a barrier the study should measure.

This is the same problem the rest of the paper documents, moved to the recruitment stage: a student cannot use a service, or a whole institution, they do not know applies to them. A concrete test fits the method already in use. Take the personas’ real starting questions (“how do I make more money,” “careers that do not need a four-year degree”) and run them through common search engines and AI assistants, then record whether a Maricopa college appears, how far down, and what appears instead. It costs nothing and produces a findability result the district can act on.

The usage baseline

The before-measure. Without a baseline taken now, no lift can be shown later.

Counting how many students walk into an office measures foot traffic, and effective automation deliberately reduces foot traffic. A student who gets a correct answer from a tool in two minutes, and never waits two days for an advisor, has a better experience but registers as one fewer office visit. Measured by usage alone, that success would score as a failure. The baseline therefore measures whether the student’s need was met, how easily, how fast, and whether the student persisted, across whatever channel served them. Current usage is still recorded, as a diagnostic of where students go, not as the definition of success.

The measures the study captures at baseline and tracks after deployment:

  • Need met. Whether the student got a correct answer or reached the right place, by any channel: in person, tool, web, or staff.
  • Ease and satisfaction. Student-reported ease, using a Single Ease Question, and satisfaction with how the need was handled.
  • Speed. Time from question to resolution, including the wait to reach a person when a person is needed.
  • Outcomes. Persistence term to term, completion, and transfer, the measures the whole effort exists to move.
  • Staff time reallocated. Routine questions deflected, so staff hours shift to students with complex needs.

A drop in routine office traffic alongside steady or higher satisfaction and outcomes is success, not loss. No one is replaced; the point is to move routine, repetitive work off staff so their time goes to the students who need a person. The inventory lists the services, and the study still records how many students use each one, out of how many are enrolled, as a diagnostic. The baseline matters because current connection is likely to be low and is largely unmeasured. Only 55 percent of students say their college communicates support well, and at two-year colleges fewer than 10 percent of students with a disability receive accommodations, against 28 percent at four-year institutions. The services exist. The connection to them does not.

What is asked

At each college, one committee member sits down with the Dean of Student Life, the office that already oversees these services and holds the usage numbers. The conversation runs about fifteen minutes and covers the same questions each time.

  • How many unique students used each service last year.
  • What share of eligible students that represents, if it can be estimated.
  • How the office believes students find them, and how students actually do.
  • Where demand exceeds capacity.
  • What the office wishes students knew before they arrived.

Counts and estimates are sufficient. No student-level or identifying data is requested, and nothing is attributed to an individual. The framing is stated plainly in the conversation: this is a study of whether better student-to-service connection improves persistence, and it requires a starting number. It is not an audit and it is not a step toward replacing anyone. Automation is intended to take routine, repetitive work off staff so that human contact between students and the people who help them expands rather than contracts.

  • Early wins, where a practice at one college could be adopted by others.
  • The capacity ceilings that automation could help lift.

Barriers documented so far

The study has not run yet. The barriers below come from the instrument demonstration on public pages and the prior 2025 local survey. None is a finding of the district study.

  • At Mesa, the financial-aid landing page carries no FAFSA deadline at all. It states only that processing can take two to six weeks and advises applying early, with no date. This would be a catastrophic barrier for a Pell-dependent student, and the demonstration flagged it at a preliminary severity 4, pending independent human raters.
  • At Glendale, the page literally named Important Dates does not list the FAFSA deadline either. The date sits on a separate page called How to Apply, so a student who opens Important Dates leaves without it.
  • The same service is named differently across colleges, a findability barrier.   • Answers sit where an assistant cannot reach them: steps trapped in downloadable documents, and services like the Mesa Market food pantry reachable only through unlabeled system links.
  • How a student books advising, and what the site says to expect afterward, varies across colleges. Glendale academic advising has no online self-scheduler on its public pages: a student submits a Google form or emails and waits for a callback. Instrument demonstration, public-site walkthrough

A public-site walkthrough that opens Part 1, the no-login layer: an initial twelve walks at two colleges to prove the instrument, then broader batches on 27 July across seven more colleges and three end-to-end enrollment-funnel walks. Its records are preliminary, not yet district findings.

The study method is to walk the student journey the way a student walks it, from a felt need rather than a URL, and to record every barrier encountered. This is a demonstration of that method, deliberately kept small: one AI tester, two colleges, public pages only, no account and no login. The purpose is not to grade the two colleges. It is to establish that the instrument produces concrete, decision-ready records, and to check whether the capture fields hold what the analysis will need.

Part 1, three batches, 27 July 2026

Part 1 is the public, no-login layer, and it began on 27 July 2026. Three batches are logged so far: an initial set of single-service runs to prove the instrument and watch for saturation; a second set that widened the personas (veteran, refugee and English-learner, Native, disability, health-careers, international, dual-enrollment high schooler) and the services (veterans, apply and English, food, disability, placement, mental health, financial aid, tutoring, dual enrollment, international admissions) across seven more colleges; and a third set that walked the full front of the enrollment funnel end to end. Every finding stays a candidate until human raters confirm it, and colleges are reported as patterns, never named or ranked.

On the Nielsen 0 to 4 severity scale, twenty-one single-service runs are scored (two cosmetic, ten minor, nine major), with two search-findability runs set aside for re-testing from an Arizona location, since web results are localized and a plain search from outside the region can surface a same-named out-of-area college a local student would most likely not hit. Four defect patterns reproduce across colleges and services. First, the same support service is named differently at every college, often in branded or staff language a student would never search (a food pantry behind a mascot name, tutoring filed under a “Learning Center,” mental-health help labeled “counseling” beside academic counseling), so a plain-need search does not find it. Second, the path to a person or a next step is gated by a term a first-generation or newcomer student does not know (advisement, affiliation, MEID, SEVIS, FAFSA, placement). Third, when a need forks into several options, the site lists them all and makes an anxious newcomer self-diagnose (five high-school programs, nine placement exams, non-credit versus credit English), where one plain question would route them. Fourth, online and phone-first students are funneled into in-person framing and heavy external portals, two of which timed out mid-task on a phone. The recommended fixes follow the study’s cheapest-first ladder and hold to translate, don’t standardize: add a student-language synonym and search layer so the felt-need search finds the college’s own service, never rename it.

The enrollment funnel, walked end to end

The clearest single result came from walking the enrollment funnel in order rather than one service at a time. Three personas, a first-generation phone-first student, a veteran, and a working parent, each started from finding the college and completed the public stages: apply, residency and cost, placement, financial aid, and next steps. All three were stopped at the same point. The moment they clicked Register, the Student Center required an MEID and two-factor login they did not have. That login is the boundary between Part 1 and the signed-in tasks, now observed at three colleges rather than assumed. Two findings sit on top of it. The MEID that unlocks the entire journey is created much earlier, inside the application, disguised as a yes-or-no eligibility question and never explained, so a student meets it as an unexplained demand at four separate steps. And residency and cost, the highest-anxiety stage, was also the most broken: it leads with legal language, never states a plain price, and the residency page failed to load on two of the three walks. The study also credits what works, because fair findings require it: a “free to apply, everyone is accepted” message at the top of the funnel, a Spanish enrollment guide, a visible promise scholarship, at-home placement and online orientation for online students, and advising pages that speak in plain language to the fear of picking the wrong classes. The pattern across the three batches is convergence, new runs confirming the same defect classes rather than surfacing new ones, which is the saturation signal the method is built to reach.

The most frequently asked aid question has no findable answer, which is the clearest case in the demonstration for a factual tool that states the deadline.

Tutoring, Glendale, preliminary severity 3

“I am failing math and I go looking for tutoring. It is not under Help, it is under Academics, which feels like it is for picking classes. I finally click Tutoring and the page is basically empty. It says to see the tiles below and there are no tiles. So now I do not know whether tutoring exists, or whether my phone is broken.”

A service that exists reads as a service that does not when the page fails to render. Hours published as plain text would have been readable to the tester and to an assistant.

I end up on a large Get Help page and start clicking links that just say things like node-eight-one-five-eight. One of them turns out to be a food pantry called the Mesa Market. I would never have found that on my own.”

The pantry exists and is stocked. It sits behind unlabeled links that a student in crisis is unlikely to work through.

What the demonstration suggests, provisionally

Two colleges is a small sample and nothing here generalizes to the district. What the demonstration does establish is that the capture fields hold the right information and that the walks produce specific, actionable records. Three patterns recur across the twelve walks. Each is a hypothesis the scaled study is built to test, not a conclusion.

  1. The same service carries a different name at each college. Tutoring is the Center for Learning at Glendale and the Learning Enhancement Center at Mesa. Basic needs is GCC Cares at one and Student Care Services, with the Mesa Market pantry, at the other. A student who learns the term at one college cannot reuse it at the next. This is the service-crosswalk problem, observed in a live walk.
  2. The service exists and the label conceals it. GCC Cares is a service sitting behind a sub-anchor under Student Life, and Glendale’s FAFSA deadline is published, but not on the page named Important Dates. In this demonstration the gap was almost never a missing service. It was that the student could not reach the service from the words the student would use.
  3. Answers sit where an assistant cannot reach them. Steps in a downloadable document, pages that render blank without JavaScript, and unlabeled system links are all invisible to a student’s AI assistant. As students arrive carrying their own assistants, those services become effectively unreachable for them.

The opportunities named in this pilot are factual and public: state the FAFSA deadline, surface tutoring hours, accept “I need food” in plain language and route to the pantry. None of that requires a name, a login, or a record, which keeps it within the responsible, minimal-data stance the district holds. It also removes routine work from staff desks. When the page answers the repeated factual questions, the financial aid specialist and the advisor stop fielding them and the “we will get back to you” email shrinks, so the student with a genuinely complicated situation reaches a person sooner. No one is replaced.

Limits of this demonstration

  • Two colleges of ten, one AI tester, one pass. These are illustrative patterns and not a district finding.
  • Public pages only, by design. The authenticated journey (registration, holds, Student Center, naming an advisor) is unwalked, and that is where the deeper barriers usually sit.
  • Severity is preliminary. Every rating is a single AI tester’s first pass, without the independent human raters and the adjudication the method requires, and without think-aloud validation from real students. A 4 here is a flag for fieldwork, not a final score.
  • Sites change. The pages were live when the demonstration ran. A page that rendered blank or a date that was missing may already have been corrected, which is worth re-checking.
  • Nothing named here is a built tool. The opportunities are candidate directions screened for responsible, minimal use of student data, and no pilot has been approved or built.

How this survey shaped the study

Three properties of this data drove the design of the district instrument. Why students do not persist

The evidence base under the study. This section reports published research and one internal survey, not results from the gap analysis.

Students who feel connected to their institution and supported by its services persist to completion at higher rates. This is among the most replicated findings in higher education research. Astin’s theory of involvement and Tinto’s student departure model established it in the 1970s and 1980s, and decades of subsequent work have sharpened rather than overturned it. The premise tells the domain what to measure. If connection and support are what keep students enrolled, then the quantity worth baselining is how well students are actually reaching support right now.

Four pressures converge

Asked directly, students name pressures that are rarely academic. They are the pressures of adult life meeting an institution that assumes a student will come and find it, and the community college population carries them more heavily than any other group in higher education.

PressureWhat the research shows

The gap between stopping out and staying enrolled is frequently a gap in awareness and connection rather than in service availability.

A local signal consistent with the national pattern

The case above rests on large multi-year national studies. The evidence that follows is much smaller, a single internal survey at Glendale Community College in 2025, and it is weighted accordingly. It is a local check against the national pattern, and it is consistent with it. Faculty and staff documented 83 barriers they watch students hit, rating them 4.5 out of 5 on average for both frustration and impact. The largest categories were not classroom problems. Sorted by what the barrier is about: student access (21), communication (19), reaching a service (19), and campus technology such as printing, wireless access, login, and lab availability (14), with comfort needs, textbooks, and printing close behind. Sorted by which department owns the fix, the most-named were information technology, advisement, and basic needs.

The basic-needs pressure is documented locally as well. Glendale Community College Student Services reports that 44 percent of its students are food insecure. One survey does not describe a district of ten colleges, but it points in the same direction as the national data: the services exist, and students cannot reliably find them, reach them, or afford to use them.

Connecting the evidence to the intervention

The intervention works where the connection is made. Among caregiving students, those who persisted were considerably more likely than those who stopped out to report that their college had provided financial, basic-needs, and childcare support. Locally, the Glendale Community College Peer Success Coach program found that students matched with a coach retained at higher rates than those who were not, which is local evidence that connection to a person keeps students enrolled.

The pieces of the domain’s work connect in sequence. The inventory maps where the services are and counts how many students reach them now, which is the baseline. The barriers study locates where students fail to reach them. This evidence establishes why that failure matters. The recommendations then rank fixes by how many students each would touch. Automation answers routine factual questions and books the routine appointment so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours with the students who have a real problem. No one is replaced.

Build teams and funding

Once barriers are prioritized and the buy-or-build calls are made, the tools are built by three multidisciplinary student teams, four students each, twelve students total, drawn from across the ten colleges and led by ARC members. The teams are assembled later rather than this fall, because the gap analysis will not be complete until the study has run, and there is nothing for a team to build until the barriers are known and ranked. The target is to assemble the teams around November so they are ready to build by January 2027. Each team is mixed by discipline because the work is: computer-science and software-development students build the tool; computer-information-systems and information-technology students handle the data, the integration, and what it takes to run inside an institution; digital media students design the interface and the language a student actually reads; business and project-management students hold the schedule and the client relationship; and communication students carry the human side of the service being redesigned. How a documented barrier becomes a candidate pilot: the ranking rule, the models the design borrows from, and what a pilot has to look like before it is proposed.

No pilot has been ranked, selected, approved, or built, because the study that would rank them has not been run. This page defines the decision rule the committee will apply once findings exist, and the directions the earlier local evidence already points toward. Everything below is a method and a hypothesis, not a result.

Reach decides what gets piloted

The first question asked of every candidate is how many students it would touch. A sophisticated tool that reaches a few hundred students counts for less than a simple one that reaches tens of thousands, so the highest-reach fixes are piloted first. The rule is stated explicitly because the alternative default in an AI initiative is to build the most interesting tool.

Ranking follows the analysis described in the methodology. Logged barriers are affinity-mapped into themes first, so the committee prioritizes patterns rather than one-off incidents. Each theme is then placed on a severity-by-reach matrix, and pilots are taken from the top of that matrix downward. The result is a defensible order rather than a guess, with a baseline number to measure each pilot against.

Ranking factorSourceWhy it counts
Student reachThe service inventory and baseline (students served against students eligible)The largest filter for choosing what to build: fixes that touch the most students go first. This decides priority, not whether a pilot succeeded, which is judged by whether needs are met more easily and faster and whether students persist.
Barrier severityThe usability study, on the Nielsen 0 to 4 scaleFrequency, impact, and persistence, combined into one rating as the mean of at least three independent raters and adjudicated.

The domain does not hold that every interaction requires a human. It holds that a tool must return staff time and protect human connection where it matters, and must not displace the relationships that keep students enrolled.

Every option is screened against the no-student-data line, and none renames a college’s service.

  • Getting in the door. Program searches surface for-profit schools and aggregators above the district. Candidate fixes: high-intent program-page search optimization, paid search on those queries, a plain “fast, affordable training near you” hub, and a complete Google Business Profile for each campus.
  • Finding a service. A service is named in branded or staff language a student would never search. Candidate fixes: add a student-language synonym in search and page metadata while the college keeps its own name, and a chatbot that maps the felt need to the local name. Nothing is renamed.
  • Reaching a person. The path to a human is labeled with a term a first-generation student does not know. Candidate fixes: a plain-language entry from the felt need, a plain new-student landing page, and a triage chatbot that hands off to a person.
  • Using a service. The service is findable, but registering or booking needs a login or a callback. Candidate fixes: real online booking, a chatbot for the routine questions, and routing booking into the incoming platform (Part 3).
  • Enrolling, the account and cost wall. The whole funnel depends on an MEID the student is never told about, and residency and cost leads with legal language, shows no price, and can fail to load. Candidate fixes: one plain callout explaining the MEID and that it unlocks everything after, a real per-credit price on the cost page beside the legal detail, a performance fix so the residency page loads on a phone, and moving the reassuring advising help ahead of the account gate.

The models the design borrows from

The claim that connection-focused, AI-assisted support can move persistence is not an assumption of this study. Other institutions have demonstrated it under evaluation, and those results set the standard any pilot here is measured against. The pattern is consistent across them: technology surfaces the right student at the right moment, and a person follows through. None of the results below belong to this study.

Georgia State University, text-based outreach

An AI chatbot answered enrollment, financial aid, and deadline questions by text at any hour. Summer melt fell by 22 percent, withdrawals from unpaid balances fell by half, and persistence rose by roughly 3 percent, representing about 1,300 additional students enrolled. First-generation and Pell-eligible students used it most, which is the population community colleges most need to reach. It is the most rigorously evaluated example in the literature, tested through multiple randomized controlled trials. See source 1.

City University of New York, coordinated wraparound support

The Accelerated Study in Associate Programs model addresses the common barriers at once, advising, money, transit, tutoring, and career planning, rather than leaving students to assemble supports alone. Three-year graduation reached 53 percent against 24.6 percent for a matched comparison group, and the model has been replicated at more than 60 institutions with the same result. The lesson for this work is that students struggle under an accumulation of barriers, so coordinated connection beats optimizing any single service in isolation. See source 2.

Valencia College, connected advising

An Aspen Prize winner that links faculty, staff, courses, technology, and services into one developmental advising system following students from entry through graduation. The match between students’ education plans and their actual course-taking rose from 43 percent to 60 percent. It is the conceptual ancestor of the connected student-support model this domain is working toward. See source 3.

Caring Campus, connection as a staff practice

A behavioral model rather than a technology program, spreading specific student-connecting behaviors among faculty and staff. It is implemented at more than 120 colleges, with positive retention findings reported by the Community College Research Center. It is the human infrastructure that makes AI-assisted outreach meaningful, because a tool can surface a student who needs help but a person has to follow through. See source 4.

Glendale Community College, peer success coaches

Local evidence for the same claim. Students matched with a peer coach retained at higher rates than those who were not, and the program was a 2023 to 2024 district Innovation of the Year finalist. It raises the question this initiative exists to answer: if connection to a person keeps students enrolled, what limits the scale of that connection? Where the limit is the routine load on a coordinator’s day, automation can let one coordinator support more students. See source 5.

What a pilot has to look like

Automation takes the routine, people take the rest

A factual question (where a class meets, when the aid deadline falls, what a balance is) can be answered instantly by a tool. That removes repetitive work from advising and financial aid staff so their hours go to the students whose situations require a person. The measure of success is more staff time where it counts, not a person in every interaction. No one is replaced.

Co-designed with the people who named the barrier

Pilots are built with the staff and students who flagged the need, not handed to them. Training comes before deployment, because staff have consistently ranked training as their first AI priority.

Adopted through invitation, not mandate

A friendly, peer-to-peer adoption effort, distributed at moments where people already gather, so an AI tool arrives as something staff and students helped shape. A small, low-pressure object or character, on the model of the stress ball that eased people into accessibility work they had been resisting, lowers anxiety and signals that trying the tool is safe and optional. This is a proposed approach, not a completed campaign.

Measured against a baseline

Each pilot carries a starting picture from the baseline and a target. Success is not measured by office or service usage alone. Counting office visits measures foot traffic, and a tool that answers a student in two minutes deliberately reduces those visits while improving the experience, so usage alone would score that as a failure. A year in, the committee asks whether the student’s need was met, how easily and how fast, whether the student persisted, across whatever channel served them, and whether routine work shifted off staff toward the students who need a person. Usage is tracked as a diagnostic of where students go, not as the definition of success: a drop in routine office traffic alongside steady or higher satisfaction and outcomes is success, not loss. The question is whether students are better served, not only whether the tool shipped. No one is replaced.

Build, adopt, or configure

A pilot does not have to be a new application. For each top-ranked barrier the committee takes the lightest option that works: adopt a tool that already exists, configure something already licensed (a plug-in or a feature inside a platform already running), or build an agent or workflow into one of the audited systems. The structure is the same across all three: automation surfaces the right student or the right resource, and a person follows through. The sequence is set by reach, beginning in the systems the most students already use.

An existing vendor tool, or a configured workflow inside a licensed platform, could solve a top-ranked barrier equally well, and any option that would collect student data is screened out before it is considered.

Two directions the prior evidence already points to

The ranked list can only come from the study, and the study has not been run. Two high-reach directions are nonetheless already indicated by the earlier local survey and by the persistence research, and both are stated here as hypotheses for the committee to test rather than as decisions.

Put help where the student already is

Students rarely stop out through a failure to try. They disengage and withdraw, often while working two jobs and caring for family, and frequently without knowing what to ask for. The indicated fix is not an additional website. It is the right resource appearing where the student already is: in the learning management system, at enrollment, in the first-year experience, inside the financial aid flow, and in a chat channel. The more places the help appears, the less a student has to know the name of the office that provides it. Plain-language help of this kind answers the simple questions and routes everything else to a person.

Equip faculty as the first line of support

Faculty are among the most trusted sources of referral and are usually the first to see a student struggling. Many still drop a student after three absences, which forces non-completion at precisely the moment the student is in crisis, working a double shift, or caring for a sick parent. This is student-support work rather than curriculum work, so it sits inside this domain’s scope. The proposed pilot is a five-minute briefing for first-year faculty and adjunct faculty: who the students actually are and the basic-needs, work, and caregiving pressures they carry; why an absence is a signal to connect rather than to cut; and the few specific things a faculty member can do in thirty seconds to point a student toward help. It follows the Caring Campus model, behavioral rather than mandated, and it is considerate of faculty who already carry a heavy load.

The five-minute faculty briefing is a candidate deliverable, not an approved one. It could be produced as a short slide deck for first-year experience and adjunct onboarding once the committee agrees to it.

Other candidates the evidence suggests

Three further directions are plausible and remain hypotheses until the ranked list either confirms or drops them. A plain-language intake and routing tool, the ambient version of the routing prototype, would touch nearly every student and speaks directly to the awareness gap. Proactive, channel-flexible outreach where students already are, by text or chat rather than by email, would reach the disengaged students least likely to come find the institution. And surfacing AI tools the district already licenses but underuses is a near-zero-cost option that the tool inventory is expected to locate.

Distinctive peer and cohort models worth studying first

Several colleges run distinctive peer and cohort models worth understanding before any district-wide tool is recommended, because they show what already works and should not be flattened. Glendale’s Foster Youth Champions and Rising Scholars are peer-staffed cohort programs built on trust-based outreach. Phoenix College’s Veteran Student Center is staffed by veterans. These are human-centered supports the study should learn from, not replace.

Known sources of resistance

Three sources of resistance are documented here, each with the design decision that answers it.

Staff concern about being replaced

Willingness to learn AI is low where employees suspect the underlying goal is to eliminate their jobs. The design answers this directly. Every pilot keeps people in control and exists to remove routine work so that staff spend more time with students, not so that there are fewer staff. Training precedes any tool, pilots are co-designed with the front line, and adoption is invitational rather than mandatory.

Students who have been told AI is cheating

Many students have been told by faculty that using AI is cheating, and avoid it entirely. The tools contemplated here connect students to people and services and do not perform coursework, which places them outside the academic-integrity concern. The domain co-supports student AI training with the Teaching and Learning domain, so that students learn what responsible, permitted use involves, and it is possible this domain, Student Support and Success, serves as the testers of what those students create.

The environmental cost of the compute

The energy and water footprint of AI is substantial and it matters to this community. The design prefers tools already licensed, right-sized and efficient models over new heavy compute, and the lightest workflow that resolves the barrier. Not every fix requires a large model.

The persistence imperative

Committee: ARC, Domain 5. Status: Evidence brief, sourced. Prepared by: Domain 5 co-chairs. Updated: July 2026.

An evidence brief for faculty and academic leadership on why community college students do not persist, the support that already exists, and the practices through which faculty improve completion.

1. The strategic case

Decades of evidence, from the classic theories of student involvement and student departure through contemporary national research, converge on a well-replicated finding: students who feel connected to their institution and supported by its services persist at materially higher rates. For the populations these colleges serve, the support a student needs largely already exists. What is missing is the connection to it. Closing that gap is among the highest-return investments available in completion, and faculty, who are the first to observe disengagement and the most trusted source of referral, are central to it.

2. Understanding the students

Attrition is concentrated among the very populations these colleges were established to serve. The pressures below are not edge cases. They are the modal experience of the community college student, and they are heightened at a Hispanic-Serving Institution, which all ten Maricopa colleges are.

3. The online dimension

The students balancing the most, meaning work, caregiving, and distance, disproportionately enroll in online sections for their flexibility, and online and distance students are a distinct population: older, more often working, and disproportionately parents and caregivers, and many cannot attend on campus at all because of work schedules, childcare, or the lack of a car or a reasonable commute. The demographic detail behind this population, the ages, the parenting and employment shares, the discretionary-hours and income gaps, and the modality split built into the personas, is set out with sources under “Maricopa demographics and how the synthetic students were built.” These are students fitting a degree into the margins of a full life.

Online courses have long completed at lower rates, and the disparity is widest for the students already most at risk. Read against that population, the lower measured success in online courses reflects who enrolls online rather than the modality itself. Online sections draw a more time-constrained and higher-risk population, and a raw online-versus-in-person comparison sets that population against a less-burdened one. Such a comparison also leaves out the students who would not be enrolled at all without an online option. For many students whose work, family, transportation, or other obligations rule out attending in person, distance education is what makes enrollment possible in the first place (The Conversation, distance learning changes lives). An online-versus-in-person success gap should therefore not be read as a failure of online teaching without first accounting for the difference in who is in each group. Where isolation and the absence of structure and support do drive attrition online, those are precisely the conditions connection is designed to remedy, so intentional connection is most consequential exactly where it is hardest to establish.

7. An underutilized support infrastructure

The institution operates more than twenty student-facing services. Most students, and many faculty, cannot name a fraction of what is available to them at no cost. Awareness, not availability, is the limiting factor.

  • Basic Needs, the food pantry, emergency funds, book vouchers, childcare assistance, housing support, and campus social workers
  • Tutoring, Math Solutions, the Writing Center, and library research support
  • Disability Resources, ESL support, and health and wellness
  • Career services, veterans services, the transfer center, and Peer Success Coaches

Disability Resources serves students with ADHD, anxiety, and other non-apparent conditions, not only visible disabilities. A student who reports difficulty concentrating or completing timed assessments may qualify for accommodations. Faculty are the bridge across that gap.

8. Reconsidering punitive attrition

Administrative drops for early absence remove students at their most vulnerable point and frequently manufacture the non-completion they are intended to forestall. An early absence is a leading indicator of a student in difficulty, working an additional shift or managing a family emergency, rather than a measure of commitment. The evidence is consistent: proactive outreach and flexible pathways retain students, and removal does not. Where an attendance or drop policy affords no accommodation for a student in crisis, that policy operates against completion.

10. The faculty role

Faculty are the first to observe disengagement and the most trusted source of referral. Four practices, each a matter of seconds, materially shift outcomes.

  1. Reach out early. A brief check-in at the first sign of disengagement is frequently the single intervention that re-engages a student.
  2. Normalize help-seeking. Position services as ordinary practice rather than remediation. Stigma is a primary barrier to utilization.
  3. Refer with precision. One service, one named contact. Precision outperforms a catalogue of options.
  4. Offer a path, not an exit. An alternate means to submit or participate should precede any administrative drop.

11. The leverage of a single connection

Across more than thirty thousand graduates, the Gallup-Purdue Index found that a mentor who encouraged a student’s goals more than doubled that student’s odds of being engaged at work and thriving in life, and that a professor who cared about them as a person made them 1.9 times more likely to be engaged. Only about two in ten graduates report having had such a mentor. The role is not assigned. A professor, an advisor, a coach, a club sponsor, or a social worker can be the connection that keeps a student enrolled, and the institution’s task is to make that connection likely rather than incidental.

12. How we will measure whether it works

The study establishes a baseline first, capturing how students experience each service now: whether their need was met, how easily, how fast, and whether they persisted, with current usage recorded alongside. That baseline is the point of comparison. As we roll out any shared workflow or tool, success is judged by whether students’ needs are met more easily and faster and whether they persist, not by whether a tool was delivered and not by office-usage counts alone. Usage is a diagnostic of where students go: a tool that answers a routine question in two minutes reduces office visits by design, so a drop in routine traffic alongside steady or higher satisfaction and persistence is success, not loss. If the student experience and outcomes do not improve, the intervention did not work, and that is reported.

Appendix G, What makes each synthetic student a complete agent

This appendix spells out plainly what a synthetic student is, so a skeptical reader can see it is legitimate engineering. These are AI agents built from persona traits, not static personas. A written persona is a description. A synthetic student is a persona’s traits instantiated as an agent that actually goes and does the task.

Specialist agents and one orchestrator

The system is built as 50 specialist agents plus a single orchestrator, not one generalist agent trying to be every student at once. Each of the 50 personas is its own specialist agent, a specific student carried whole and never broken into traits, lenses, or sub-models. One orchestrator agent coordinates them. It sequences the journey so a persona gets in the door before it tries to pay, decides which persona, task, and college to run next, routes each run to the right specialist, and records what each returns. This many-specialists-plus-one-coordinator design is stronger than a single generalist agent: each specialist stays inside one student’s knowledge, device, and limits, so it models that student’s likely stopping points rather than an AI’s competence, while the orchestrator owns sequence, prerequisites, and coverage across the full task set. The approach is informed by Western Governors University’s synthetic course-validation model, in which agent-based synthetic students validate course experiences at scale (Western Governors University, synthetic course validation, Open edX 2026 conference presentation, in the references).

It runs autonomously

Given one persona and one task, the agent browses the live college website itself: it reads pages, clicks links, and navigates toward the goal, the way any member of the public using a browser would. It has no special access and no back channel. For public findability it does not log in, because a prospective student does not either; authenticated tasks are run on the single OIT-controlled test account.

It is constrained to the persona

An AI is too capable by default and would breeze through a form a real student would abandon, so the craft is constraining the agent to the persona’s likely knowledge and behavior. The agent is prompted to use only the knowledge that student would have, to flag unfamiliar terms the way that student would, and to behave with that student’s device, patience, and reading speed, stopping where a student like this plausibly would. This is a simulation of student behavior, not a reproduction of student cognition: it surfaces candidate barriers, and every one stays a hypothesis until the human sample confirms it.

It thinks aloud and returns a structured finding

The agent narrates in the first person the whole way through, and it returns an inspectable record: the outcome, the path it took, where it broke, a first-person quote, a candidate severity, and any device notes. Every finding therefore traces back to a specific page and can be compared across runs and validated by a human, which is what makes a synthetic finding usable rather than an opinion.

The persona-agent prompt scaffold

One template, filled from each of the 50 persona briefs. No model is fine-tuned and no agent is hand-built from scratch. The scaffold has three parts.

  • Who you are. Name and age, home college, language and reading (how the student reads and where they slow down), what you know and do not know (the exact terms this student would not recognize), device and tech comfort, life constraints, emotional state, and the one goal for today.
  • The hard rules. Use only this student’s knowledge and show genuine confusion at unfamiliar terms. Behave with this student’s device, patience, and reading speed. Start from the need, never from the office. Think aloud in the first person the whole time. Do not be more helpful, thorough, or clever than the persona. Never enter real personal information.
  • The findings schema. persona id and name, college, task, outcome (found, partial, not found, or gave up), path taken, where it broke, a one-line first-person quote, suspected barrier, a severity candidate from 0 to 4 for a human to confirm, a device note, effort, and any other flag. Every finding is a candidate until a human validates severity and reality.

Appendix H, Potential pilots

Candidate pilots the study points toward. None has been ranked, selected, approved, or built; the ranked list can only come from the study once it has run. Each is a hypothesis for the committee to test.

CopaMigo, bilingual student-services routing

CopaMigo is a bilingual student-services routing tool, a prototype built by Michelle Blomberg (case study: singletrackmom.github.io/copamigo). A student describes a need in plain English or Spanish, and the tool directs them to the relevant office with the contact, the hours, and what to ask for. A pilot plan for CopaMigo is prepared and is waiting to be submitted to the AI Resource Center once a formal piloting framework is in place. The planned pilot links CopaMigo from the student-support page in Canvas, which reaches every student enrolled in a Canvas course, so faculty can direct students to it.

Plain-language intake and routing

The ambient version of the routing prototype: a plain-language intake tool that would touch nearly every student and speaks directly to the awareness gap, answering the simple questions on its own and routing everything else to a person.

Proactive text or chat outreach

Channel-flexible outreach where students already are, by text or chat rather than by email, to reach the disengaged students least likely to come find the institution.

A five-minute faculty briefing

A short briefing for first-year and adjunct faculty on who the students are and the pressures they carry, why an absence is a signal to connect rather than to cut, and the few things a faculty member can do in thirty seconds to point a student toward help. It follows the Caring Campus model, behavioral rather than mandated.

Surfacing AI tools the district already licenses

A near-zero-cost option: surfacing AI tools the district already licenses but underuses, which the tool inventory is expected to locate.
