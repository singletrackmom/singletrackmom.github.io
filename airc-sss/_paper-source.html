Student Support and Success Committee · AI Resource Center, Domain 5

The plan

Goals

The committee improves student success, better grades, persistence, transfer, and jobs, by connecting students to the help they need, to enroll, to get support, to stay on track in a class, and to move toward a job, at the moment they need it. Much of that help already exists but goes unfound. These are the goals:

  1. Map the gaps. Complete a Student Journey Gap Analysis across all ten colleges, a severity-ranked map of where students hit barriers. Measure: every barrier across the sixty-plus journey touchpoints at ten colleges, rated 0 to 4 and ranked by how many students it touches.
  2. Set the baseline. Capture a starting picture of how students experience each service now, so improvement can be measured later. Success is defined by whether a student’s need was met, how easily and how fast, and whether the student persisted, across whatever channel served them, not by how many students walk into an office. Service-usage numbers are still collected from every student-services department, as a diagnostic of where students currently go rather than as the definition of success. Measure: a baseline of need met, ease, speed, and persistence by service and college, with usage tracked alongside and unmeasured services flagged as gaps.
  3. Close the highest-priority gaps with AI. For the top-ranked barriers, deliver AI tools or workflow changes that connect students to help, enrollment, class success, and jobs faster. Measure: tools and workflow fixes delivered against the top barriers each cycle, each piloted with a defined success metric.
  4. Build with students and share. Run paid multidisciplinary student build teams and share what works across the ten colleges. Measure: three student teams, twelve students, building tools; early-adopter workflows published district-wide; and a formal pilot-to-production process in place before the first pilot.

The committee also supports related work, such as student AI training, but these four goals are its central charge.

The work runs in phases:

  1. Research. The AI UX barriers study (personas walk the student journey, human and synthetic testers, every barrier rated for severity), a usage baseline (a starting measure of how well students reach and finish at each service, with current usage counts gathered from every student-services department as a diagnostic of where students go), and surfacing what already works (a call to student-services leaders for their AI early adopters, workflows shared across the ten colleges).
  2. Identify and prioritize the barriers. Group the findings, rate each barrier by severity against how many students it touches, and rank them.
  3. Decide where AI fits. For the top barriers, decide whether AI can close the gap, and whether to buy a tool or build one, every option screened for responsible, minimal use of student data.
  4. Build. Multidisciplinary student teams across the district build the tools and agents (see Build teams and funding).
  5. Pilot, safely. Each tool runs as a controlled pilot in a secure, district-hosted environment before production, following the committee’s AI pilot governance framework.
  6. Production. Tools that clear the pilot go into production, monitored, with a named owner.

Two things are weighed together: impact, how many students a fix reaches and how severe the barrier is, and feasibility, what the fix costs in money, time, and staff effort. The aim is the greatest improvement per unit of resource. A high-impact, low-cost fix, for example an in-house workflow change that streamlines a department’s own work, may rank above a higher-cost tool that would reach more students but exceeds available funding. Where the strongest solution is a purchase beyond current budget, it is documented and deferred. Every finding, its reach, its severity, and the estimated cost and breadth of each proposed fix, is analyzed with AI to rank the recommendations against the resources actually available.

Throughout, the committee refines processes and shares what works across all ten colleges.

Overview

A 10-college UX study of the student journey across Maricopa, mapping barriers and turning them into prioritized, human-in-the-loop AI pilots.

Goal

Locate the friction that pushes students out, then close it with AI where AI helps and route the rest to a person. The aim is more human contact where it matters, measured against a baseline captured before anything is deployed.

A UX study of the student journey across all 10 Maricopa colleges, which together serve roughly 140,000 students. It maps where students hit barriers as they move from application to the goal they came for, pairing expert heuristic evaluation against Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics with task-based usability testing, Nielsen 0 to 4 severity rating, journey mapping, and service blueprinting. The findings become prioritized, human-in-the-loop AI pilots, and every option is screened for responsible, minimal use of student data.

Audience

The district, its students, and the administrators who decide where support goes. The work is run for the district AI Resource Center, which coordinates AI across the 10 colleges.

Process

Research personas walk the live student journey, one persona, one campus, one task at a time, thinking aloud, so a failure is attributable to a specific barrier. Every run records the outcome, the time on task, the assists needed, the barrier, an ease score, and a severity rating scored by two independent raters. Barriers are affinity-mapped into themes and ranked on severity against reach, then mapped to a proposed AI fix. This work touches student data; there is no pretending otherwise. The gap analysis runs on dedicated test student accounts, and no finding identifies an individual student, but reaching a real service means working in the real systems. The committee’s commitment is to handle student data responsibly: collect the minimum needed, keep a person in the loop, and be transparent about what is used and why. Testers use test student accounts, never a real student’s account, and the capture form records the persona, the task, and the barrier, not any student’s identity. Because a Maricopa login reaches only its own college, each tester works only their own campus.

What this is

The purpose of the study and the method it uses.

Students rarely leave because a policy was wrong. They leave because they could not find an advisor, could not tell what dropping one class would do to their financial aid, or could not get a printer to work the day a paper was due. Those barriers are invisible in aggregate data and observable within minutes of walking the journey.

The study therefore walks the journey. A tester, human or AI, is given a persona, a campus, and one thing that persona needs, and is never told where to go, because a student is never told where to go. What breaks, and how badly, is logged and scored. The result is a map of where the district loses students, ranked by how many students each barrier touches, with a proposed fix attached to each one: work an AI can absorb, or work that belongs to a person.

The method pilot has already produced results. Twelve AI-tester walkthroughs across Glendale and Mesa found a catastrophic barrier on the first pass: at one college, the financial aid landing page carries no FAFSA deadline at all, which is the deadline most critical for a Pell-dependent student. Two colleges also showed the naming-inconsistency problem, since the same service is called Basic Needs at one and Student Care Services at the other. These findings are provisional until a second rater confirms them.

The four tracks

The four initiatives the study is organized into.

P1

What each college calls the same service, and which tools exist where. More than forty functions across ten colleges. The study translates the names and asks no college to change them.

P2

Expert heuristic evaluation plus task-based usability testing. One persona, one campus, one task, thinking aloud. Every barrier logged and rated for severity, 0 to 4, following Nielsen. The scenario bank is the backbone: 36 personas against 46 base tasks across 10 colleges.

P3

Staff workflows and usage

The backstage half. How many students reach each service, how staff actually do the work, and which offices have already solved a problem the other nine colleges are still carrying.

P4

Pilots and workflow revisions

Each barrier becomes a candidate fix, ranked by how many students it reaches. A fix may be a shared workflow rather than a tool. Anything that would collect student data is disqualified before it is considered, not after.

The staff side of the journey

Not every gap needs a new tool. Some have already been solved at one college and never travelled to the other nine.

A student-facing barrier usually has a staff-facing cause. An advisor at one college may carry a caseload of 250 students while an advisor at another carries 700, and both are expected to answer the same questions. Somebody, somewhere in the district, is already doing that work better. Every student services department has strengths and weaknesses, and at present neither travels: the strength stays local and the weakness stays uncorrected.

So the study asks two things of the ten colleges alongside the persona runs.

  1. A call to student services leaders for their AI early adopters. Any advisor, financial aid specialist, or tutor who has already automated part of their own workflow, drafted an agent, or streamlined a process is asked to describe it. The study aggregates those workflows and publishes them across the ten colleges. A workflow that already works at one college is the cheapest win available, and it requires nobody to buy anything.
  2. Direct observation, if the call turns up nothing. Domain members divide the ten colleges and sit with advisors and tutors while they work, which is contextual inquiry rather than a survey, because staff cannot reliably report the steps they have automated in their own heads. The question being answered is narrow: which parts of this work are repetitive enough that an agent could carry them, so the staff member gets more time in front of a student.

The routine reply that reads “make an appointment and we will see you in two days” is a good example of the target. It is a barrier from the student’s side, and a symptom of caseload from the staff side. Some portion of what sits behind that reply is a factual question with a factual answer, and a student who receives that answer immediately may not need the appointment at all, which returns the appointment slot to the student whose problem actually requires a person. Nobody is replaced. The routine work is what goes.

The output of this track is therefore two kinds of recommendation, and the study treats them as equal: workflow revisions that can be shared across the district at no cost, and new tools built or adopted only where no existing practice closes the gap.

Staff participation and trust

The staff whose participation the study depends on are also those most affected by how it is conducted.

Skepticism about AI among student services staff is not an obstacle to route around. It is a rational response to a technology that has been introduced elsewhere as a substitute for people, and it is the single largest risk to this study. A domain member who believes the outcome is a smaller department will not describe their own workflow honestly, and the study depends entirely on their describing it honestly.

Three commitments hold, and they are stated to staff before any observation is scheduled.

  1. The metric the study is trying to raise is human contact. If a recommendation reduces the number of students who reach a person, it has failed on its own terms, whatever else it improves.
  2. What automation removes is the repetitive factual work: the same question answered for the four hundredth time, the appointment-booking email, the routine status check. What it returns is time in front of a student. A department that reports no reduction in routine work after a pilot is reporting a finding, and the study records it as one.
  3. Staff are the authors of the fixes, not their subjects. An early-adopter workflow is published under the office that built it, and no observation is used to evaluate an individual’s performance. The study has no access to personnel data, and does not want any.

Students build the pilots

Student teams build the tools against a real user, a real constraint, and a real deliverable.

The tools that come out of this study are built by three multidisciplinary student teams, four students each, twelve in total, working the way a design studio works: a brief, a client, a deadline, and a deliverable someone is waiting for. The teams are assembled later rather than this fall, because there is nothing to build until the gap analysis has run and the barriers are ranked. The target is to bring them together around November so they are ready to build by January 2027. Students are selected directly by the domain co-chairs, so finding students is not the constraint. Each team is assembled across disciplines because the work is multidisciplinary. Computer science and software development students build the thing. Computer information systems and information technology students handle the data, the integration, and what it takes to run inside an institution. Digital media students design the interface and write the language a student actually reads. Business and project management students hold the schedule and the client relationship. Communication students carry the human side of the service being redesigned.

They leave with something rare in a portfolio: a shipped tool with a real user, a real constraint, and an evidence base behind it.

The approach applies to students the same principle the study applies to staff: the people closest to the problem help build the answer. A student who has been lost in the financial aid pages understands the barrier directly, and a student who then builds the tool that closes it gains applied experience in design, accessibility, and consequence.

Every tool remains bound by the same rules as the study: responsible, minimal use of student data, human in the loop, and a fix is only worth building if it raises the number of students who reach a person who can help them.

To student services leaders

The study is asking leaders to do two things their staff cannot do for themselves.

The rules this study runs on

Four constraints govern the design of the study.

  • Problems, not destinations. A tester is given a need, never an office. A tester who cannot find advising has recorded a finding.
  • Translate, do not standardize. Every college keeps its own names and programs. The study maps them to one another and asks no college to align.
  • Minimal data. Runs are recorded against a scenario identifier and initials, not a student’s identity. Every AI option is screened for responsible, minimal use of student data, so the preferred path is building data-sovereign tools rather than buying data-collecting ones.
  • No one is replaced. Automation takes the repetitive factual questions off advising and financial aid staff so their hours go to the students with real problems. Human contact is the measure the study is trying to raise.

Who runs the tests

The domain membership is the instrument.

Every district employee holds a Maricopa account, but access to student-facing systems is scoped to the employee’s own college. No individual can reach all ten. The Student Support and Success domain draws its members from all ten colleges, which is precisely what makes district-wide coverage possible: each member runs scenarios on the campus whose systems that member can actually reach. Students test on their own real accounts.

A volunteer takes one scenario in one sitting, roughly fifteen minutes. The low burden is deliberate, because volunteer capacity is the real constraint on a study of this size. AI testers run the same scenarios in parallel, which is how coverage extends beyond what volunteers can carry.

Method

Nothing in this section reports findings. The district study has not been run. Every quantity below is a target or a planned amount, and every result field in the instrument is empty until real runs happen. The one exception is the completed method pilot, which is reported under Findings.

The problem this study addresses

Findability, not availability.

Across the ten Maricopa colleges, the support that would keep students enrolled already exists on every campus. The failure is findability. Students cannot reach advising, financial aid, basic needs, accommodations, or the right person at the moment they need them.

A study with three personas walking thirty journeys can describe that problem. It cannot map it at the resolution the committee needs in order to prioritize where AI should close the gaps. The method below specifies a structured, repeatable instrument designed to produce a college-by-college, stage-by-stage map of where students hit barriers, with a severity judgment attached to each one.

Two methods, run together

Inspection and task testing surface different defects, and neither substitutes for the other.

  1. Expert heuristic evaluation. Evaluators inspect each college’s student-facing interfaces against Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics, working through the interface systematically and recording each violation with its location and a severity rating. This is an inspection method. No student and no persona is involved.
  2. Task-based usability testing. A tester, human or AI, adopts a persona and walks a real journey on the live systems, attempting one task from a felt need and narrating while working.

Both are needed. A heuristic evaluation catches violations a tester will silently work around without ever reporting them, and task testing exposes failures no heuristic predicts, such as a service that exists but is named something the persona would never search for.

Synthetic testers run only public, unauthenticated tasks. An AI tester browses a college’s live public website as a persona and reports where it breaks, which is also the agent-readability measure. It never logs into a real or test student account; every task behind a login is run by a human tester with a test login at their own college. That boundary keeps the study away from any real student data and any real student system. The synthetic-tester approach was inspired by a talk by Western Governors University’s CIO on synthetic students.

The persona-per-test model

The unit of the task-based study is a single run: one persona, at one campus, attempting one task. The tester opens the instrument and receives a brief stating who the persona is, what barriers the persona carries, the one thing the persona is trying to do, and the campus. The tester then attempts the task from a real felt need, starting where a student would start, usually a search on the college website.

The protocol

Runs use a concurrent think-aloud protocol. The tester narrates while working, saying what they are looking for, what they expect a label to mean, and why they are clicking what they click. The narration is the data. It is what turns a wrong turn into an explanation of the wrong turn.

The tester is given a goal and never a destination, and the facilitator does not prompt toward a service, name an office, or confirm that a page is the right page. Telling a tester where to go destroys the finding the run exists to make, because findability is precisely what is under test. The facilitator intervenes only to prevent a genuine dead stop, and each intervention is recorded as an assist.

Why a single task per run

A single task isolates one barrier, so a failure is attributable to a specific point in the journey. It also keeps runs short, parallelizable, and easy to assign to many testers at once, which is what coverage requires.

Why a persona library rather than a few personas

A page that works for a digitally fluent transfer student can still fail a phone-first, Spanish-dominant parent on a slow connection. Only a varied library surfaces those intersections. The 36 personas reflect who actually enrolls: ESL and non-native speakers, veterans, autistic and ADHD students, food-insecure and first-generation students, working parents, returning adults, students who need Disability Resources, swirling multi-campus students, and dual-enrollment high schoolers.

Severity

Rated 0 to 4, following Nielsen. A run that finds nothing wrong is a real result and is logged as 0.

ScoreMeaning
0, not a usability problemNothing went wrong. The task worked as it should.
1, cosmeticA small annoyance. The task still gets done.
2, minorNoticeable friction. Extra steps or a wrong turn, but the task gets done.
3, majorSerious difficulty. A human was needed, or the task took far longer than it should.
4, catastrophicA student cannot complete the task and would plausibly give up.

A severity score is not a measure of how irritating a barrier felt. It is a judgment that combines three factors: frequency, meaning how many students would meet this barrier; impact, meaning how hard it is to overcome once met; and persistence, meaning whether a student learns around it once or hits it again every single time. A small irritation that every student meets on every visit can outrank a dramatic failure that almost no one encounters.

Inter-rater reliability

Severity ratings are subjective, and a single rater’s number is an opinion rather than a measurement. Two raters therefore score each finding independently, without seeing each other’s score, and disagreements are adjudicated in a short review where both state their reasoning and a final score is agreed. Agreement is tracked, and the definitions above are tightened wherever raters keep diverging. This is what allows the committee to compare a severity 3 at one college with a severity 3 at another and treat them as the same claim. Every AI severity rating is treated as preliminary until a human rater confirms it, and that confirmation is the first of the two independent ratings.

Sample size

The study is formative. Its purpose is to find and fix defects, not to estimate a population parameter, so the sample is sized for defect discovery. A small number of testers per persona segment surfaces the large majority of the issues that are discoverable at all, and each additional tester on the same task returns fewer new defects than the one before. Adding a fifth or a tenth tester to the same task at the same college mostly re-finds what has already been found.

Coverage is therefore scaled on the axes that return new defects, which are personas, tasks, and colleges, and not by piling testers onto the same task. AI testers run the same scenarios in parallel, which extends coverage along those axes rather than deepening a sample that is already saturated. No fixed tester count is promised anywhere in this study, and none is needed for the design to hold.

Piloting the protocol

Before any district-wide fieldwork, the whole protocol is dry-run at a single college: the brief, the think-aloud, the capture form, the severity definitions, and the two-rater step. The dry run is a test of the instrument, not of the college. It answers whether the tasks are legible to a tester who is not the author, whether the fields capture what the analysis needs, and whether two raters can agree. Any wording that failed is repaired before the other nine colleges are touched.

Analysis

Findings are analyzed in three passes.

  1. Affinity mapping. Every logged barrier is written up as an observation and grouped with related observations until themes emerge from the data rather than from the categories the study started with. A theme is a pattern that holds across personas and colleges.
  2. Severity by reach. Each theme is placed on a matrix of severity against reach, where reach is how many of the roughly 140,000 students would plausibly meet it. High severity and high reach is where the first pilots go. High severity and low reach is documented and routed, not automated first.
  3. Journey mapping and service blueprinting. Themes are placed on the student journey to show where in the arc from application to completion a student is lost, and the worst are blueprinted, which means drawing the frontstage the student sees against the backstage staff actions that produce it. The blueprint is what makes the automation argument concrete, because the routine, repetitive backstage steps are exactly what an AI can absorb so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours on the students with real problems. No one is replaced.

Operations, tracking, and coverage

One scenario per tester, no duplication, every result logged, and a clear view of what remains.

  1. The bank is the single source of truth for assignment. Each scenario carries an ID. A college representative assigns one scenario to one tester by entering initials and a date on that row, so nothing is run twice and coverage is visible in the sheet itself.
  2. The capture form records a run: persona, college, task, path taken, outcome, time on task, assists, the barrier, severity, and the Single Ease Question score. It captures results well but does not assign work, so it stays as the capture instrument and its entries key back to the Scenario ID.
  3. The coverage dashboard reads the bank and shows, per college and per journey stage, how much has been walked and where the gaps are. It is how the co-chairs and the steering committee see progress without reading the sheet.

Recommendation: keep the existing capture form for recording results, and use the scenario bank as the assignment and coverage layer above it. This avoids building a new assignment system, reuses the live form, and keeps one ID linking a planned scenario to its logged result.

How we get the baseline numbers

To measure improvement we first need to know how many students use each service now. That number is not held in one place, so we gather it directly.

  1. Use the service crosswalk (Appendix B) as the list of departments to contact at each college.
  2. For each student-services department at each college, identify the head or director who owns the usage metric.
  3. Send the standard request letter (below) to that person, asking for the past year’s unique students served, total visits or contacts, how they track it, and whether they track by individual student or only in aggregate.
  4. Record every response in a baseline sheet by service and college. Where a department does not track usage, record that; an unmeasured service is itself a finding.
  5. Follow up with non-responders after two weeks.

Expected roadblock: some departments do not track usage individually, and in some the owner of the number is unclear, so part of this step is discovering who holds the metric and whether it exists at all.

Gathering the crosswalk and the usage baseline

The crosswalk requires two data points from each college for each service: the local name that college uses for the service, and roughly how many students it serves in a year. Guardrails

What the study holds to

  • Minimal data. Test accounts and initials only, never a real student’s data. Personas are research instruments, not real people.
  • Translate, do not standardize. Each college keeps its own service names. The study tests findability, not conformity.
  • AI-only scope. Where the fix is automation, it takes routine, repetitive work off advising and financial aid staff so their time goes to the students who need a person. No one is replaced.
  • Human contact is the metric of success.

What the study routes elsewhere

  • Process and policy barriers go to the owner who can fix them. They are documented here, not solved here.
  • Classroom, curriculum, and faculty-tool issues go to Domain 2, Teaching and Learning.
  • Data-governance and security questions, including the Salesforce data concern, go to Domain 1.
  • Retention and institutional-research data needs are coordinated with Domain 3.

An inventory, not a reorganization

Each of the ten Maricopa colleges is independently accredited, with its own programs, its own organizational structure, and its own names for things, and that arrangement stands. Nothing here asks any college to rename, align, or change anything. The work is to inventory which student-facing services exist at each college and how many students use each one.

The inventory comes first because the rest of the study depends on it. The personas cannot walk a journey without knowing which services a college offers. The baseline cannot be counted against a service list that does not exist. A pilot cannot be ranked by reach without knowing which colleges offer the service it would touch.

The inventory produces two kinds of finding. The first is hidden tools: a platform or program that one college already runs and the others do not know exists. Surfacing it allows a proven tool to spread, often at no new cost. BigInterview, the interview and resume platform, is the concrete case: Career Services at Glendale Community College has access to it, and whether the other nine colleges do is the question the inventory answers. The second is coverage gaps. “Not offered here” is a valid and useful answer. It surfaces both the cases where one college can share what it has and the gaps no college has solved, which are the ones that feed the recommendations.

The usage baseline

The before-measure. Without a baseline taken now, no lift can be shown later.

Counting how many students walk into an office measures foot traffic, and effective automation deliberately reduces foot traffic. A student who gets a correct answer from a tool in two minutes, and never waits two days for an advisor, has a better experience but registers as one fewer office visit. Measured by usage alone, that success would score as a failure. The baseline therefore measures whether the student’s need was met, how easily, how fast, and whether the student persisted, across whatever channel served them. Current usage is still recorded, as a diagnostic of where students go, not as the definition of success.

The measures the study captures at baseline and tracks after deployment:

  • Need met. Whether the student got a correct answer or reached the right place, by any channel: in person, tool, web, or staff.
  • Ease and satisfaction. Student-reported ease, using a Single Ease Question, and satisfaction with how the need was handled.
  • Speed. Time from question to resolution, including the wait to reach a person when a person is needed.
  • Outcomes. Persistence term to term, completion, and transfer, the measures the whole effort exists to move.
  • Staff time reallocated. Routine questions deflected, so staff hours shift to students with complex needs.

A drop in routine office traffic alongside steady or higher satisfaction and outcomes is success, not loss. No one is replaced; the point is to move routine, repetitive work off staff so their time goes to the students who need a person. The inventory lists the services, and the study still records how many students use each one, out of how many are enrolled, as a diagnostic. The baseline matters because current connection is likely to be low and is largely unmeasured. Only 55 percent of students say their college communicates support well, and at two-year colleges fewer than 10 percent of students with a disability receive accommodations, against 28 percent at four-year institutions. The services exist. The connection to them does not.

What is asked

At each college, one committee member sits down with the Dean of Student Life, the office that already oversees these services and holds the usage numbers. The conversation runs about fifteen minutes and covers the same questions each time.

  • How many unique students used each service last year.
  • What share of eligible students that represents, if it can be estimated.
  • How the office believes students find them, and how students actually do.
  • Where demand exceeds capacity.
  • What the office wishes students knew before they arrived.

Counts and estimates are sufficient. No student-level or identifying data is requested, and nothing is attributed to an individual. The framing is stated plainly in the conversation: this is a study of whether better student-to-service connection improves persistence, and it requires a starting number. It is not an audit and it is not a step toward replacing anyone. Automation is intended to take routine, repetitive work off staff so that human contact between students and the people who help them expands rather than contracts.

Problems found

The full study has not run yet. These are the barriers documented so far, from the method pilot and the prior local survey.

  • At Mesa, the financial-aid landing page carries no FAFSA deadline at all. It states only that processing can take two to six weeks and advises applying early, with no date. This is a catastrophic barrier for a Pell-dependent student, rated severity 4 in the pilot.
  • At Glendale, the page literally named Important Dates does not list the FAFSA deadline either. The date sits on a separate page called How to Apply, so a student who opens Important Dates leaves without it.
  • The same service is named differently across colleges, a findability barrier. Basic needs is GCC Cares at Glendale and Student Care Services at Mesa, and tutoring is the Center for Learning at Glendale and the Learning Enhancement Center at Mesa. A student who learns the term at one college cannot reuse it at the next.
  • Core pages render blank without JavaScript. Glendale’s tutoring and advising pages loaded nearly empty, so the public answer to “how do I get advising?” did not appear on a direct visit, which fails slow-connection students and AI assistants alike.
  • Answers sit where an assistant cannot reach them: steps trapped in downloadable documents, and services like the Mesa Market food pantry reachable only through unlabeled system links.

Three narrations from the walks

Severity ratings rank barriers. The narrations below record the experience behind three of them. Each was written by the AI tester in the voice of the persona it was carrying. None is a quotation from a real student, and no real student has been observed for this study.

FAFSA deadline, Mesa, preliminary severity 4

“Money is the whole reason I am scared about this. I find the financial aid page, and it says processing can take two to six weeks and that I should apply early. Early before what? There is no date. There is a phone number, but it is the weekend and I do not want to call a stranger to ask a question that should just be on the page. I close the tab.”

The most frequently asked aid question has no findable answer, which is the clearest case in the pilot for a factual tool that states the deadline.

Tutoring, Glendale, preliminary severity 3

“I am failing math and I go looking for tutoring. It is not under Help, it is under Academics, which feels like it is for picking classes. I finally click Tutoring and the page is basically empty. It says to see the tiles below and there are no tiles. So now I do not know whether tutoring exists, or whether my phone is broken.”

A service that exists reads as a service that does not when the page fails to render. Hours published as plain text would have been readable to the tester and to an assistant.

Basic needs, Mesa, preliminary severity 3

“I cannot make rent and I am too embarrassed to say it out loud. I look for basic needs in the menu. It is not there. I end up on a large Get Help page and start clicking links that just say things like node-eight-one-five-eight. One of them turns out to be a food pantry called the Mesa Market. I would never have found that on my own.”

The pantry exists and is well provisioned. It sits behind unlabeled links that a student in crisis is unlikely to work through.

What the pilot suggests, provisionally

Two colleges is a small sample and nothing here generalizes to the district. What the pilot does establish is that the capture fields hold the right information and that the walks produce specific, actionable records. Three patterns recur across the twelve walks. Each is a hypothesis the scaled study is built to test, not a conclusion.

  1. The same service carries a different name at each college. Tutoring is the Center for Learning at Glendale and the Learning Enhancement Center at Mesa. Basic needs is GCC Cares at one and Student Care Services, with the Mesa Market pantry, at the other. A student who learns the term at one college cannot reuse it at the next. This is the service-crosswalk problem, observed in a live walk.
  2. The service exists and the label conceals it. GCC Cares is a substantial service sitting behind a sub-anchor under Student Life, and Glendale’s FAFSA deadline is published, but not on the page named Important Dates. In this pilot the gap was almost never a missing service. It was that the student could not reach the service from the words the student would use.
  3. Answers sit where an assistant cannot reach them. Steps in a downloadable document, pages that render blank without JavaScript, and unlabeled system links are all invisible to a student’s AI assistant. As students arrive carrying their own assistants, those services become effectively unreachable for them.

The opportunities named in this pilot are factual and public: state the FAFSA deadline, surface tutoring hours, accept “I need food” in plain language and route to the pantry. None of that requires a name, a login, or a record, which keeps it within the responsible, minimal-data stance the district holds. It also removes routine work from staff desks. When the page answers the repeated factual questions, the financial aid specialist and the advisor stop fielding them and the “we will get back to you” email shrinks, so the student with a genuinely complicated situation reaches a person sooner. No one is replaced.

Limits of this pilot

  • Two colleges of ten, one AI tester, one pass. These are illustrative patterns and not a district finding.
  • Public pages only, by design. The authenticated journey (registration, holds, Student Center, naming an advisor) is unwalked, and that is where the deeper barriers usually sit.
  • Severity is preliminary. Every rating is a single AI tester’s first pass, without the second independent rater and the adjudication the method requires, and without think-aloud validation from real students. A 4 here is a flag for fieldwork, not a final score.
  • Sites change. The pages were live when the pilot ran. A page that rendered blank or a date that was missing may already have been corrected, which is worth re-checking.
  • Nothing named here is a built tool. The opportunities are candidate directions screened for responsible, minimal use of student data, and no pilot has been approved or built.

Three properties of this data drove the design of the district instrument. Why students do not persist

The evidence base under the study. This section reports published research and one internal survey, not results from the gap analysis.

Students who feel connected to their institution and supported by its services persist to completion at higher rates. This is among the most replicated findings in higher education research. Astin’s theory of involvement and Tinto’s student departure model established it in the 1970s and 1980s, and decades of subsequent work have sharpened rather than overturned it. The premise tells the domain what to measure. If connection and support are what keep students enrolled, then the quantity worth baselining is how well students are actually reaching support right now.

Asked directly, students name pressures that are rarely academic. They are the pressures of adult life meeting an institution that assumes a student will come and find it, and the community college population carries them more heavily than any other group in higher education.

A local signal consistent with the national pattern

The case above rests on large multi-year national studies. The evidence that follows is much smaller, a single internal survey at Glendale Community College in 2025, and it is weighted accordingly. It is a local check against the national pattern, and it is consistent with it. Faculty and staff documented 83 barriers they watch students hit, rating them 4.5 out of 5 on average for both frustration and impact. The largest categories were not classroom problems. Sorted by what the barrier is about: student access (21), communication (19), reaching a service (19), and campus technology such as printing, wireless access, login, and lab availability (14), with comfort needs, textbooks, and printing close behind. Sorted by which department owns the fix, the most-named were information technology, advisement, and basic needs.

The basic-needs pressure is documented locally as well. Glendale Community College Student Services reports that 44 percent of its students are food insecure. One survey does not describe a district of ten colleges, but it points in the same direction as the national data: the services exist, and students cannot reliably find them, reach them, or afford to use them.

Connecting the evidence to the intervention

The intervention works where the connection is made. Among caregiving students, those who persisted were considerably more likely than those who stopped out to report that their college had provided financial, basic-needs, and childcare support. Locally, the Glendale Community College Peer Success Coach program found that students matched with a coach retained at higher rates than those who were not, which is local evidence that connection to a person keeps students enrolled.

The pieces of the domain’s work connect in sequence. The inventory maps where the services are and counts how many students reach them now, which is the baseline. The barriers study locates where students fail to reach them. This evidence establishes why that failure matters. The recommendations then rank fixes by how many students each would touch. Automation answers routine factual questions and books the routine appointment so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours with the students who have a real problem. No one is replaced.

Build teams and funding

How a documented barrier becomes a candidate pilot: the ranking rule, the models the design borrows from, and what a pilot has to look like before it is proposed.

No pilot has been ranked, selected, approved, or built, because the study that would rank them has not been run. This page defines the decision rule the committee will apply once findings exist, and the directions the earlier local evidence already points toward. Everything below is a method and a hypothesis, not a result.

Reach decides what gets piloted

The first question asked of every candidate is how many students it would touch. A sophisticated tool that reaches a few hundred students counts for less than a simple one that reaches tens of thousands, so the highest-reach fixes are piloted first. The rule is stated explicitly because the alternative default in an AI initiative is to build the most interesting tool.

Ranking follows the analysis described in the methodology. Logged barriers are affinity-mapped into themes first, so the committee prioritizes patterns rather than one-off incidents. Each theme is then placed on a severity-by-reach matrix, and pilots are taken from the top of that matrix downward. The result is a defensible order rather than a guess, with a baseline number to measure each pilot against.

The domain does not hold that every interaction requires a human. It holds that a tool must return staff time and protect human connection where it matters, and must not displace the relationships that keep students enrolled.

The models the design borrows from

The claim that connection-focused, AI-assisted support can move persistence is not an assumption of this study. Other institutions have demonstrated it under evaluation, and those results set the standard any pilot here is measured against. The pattern is consistent across them: technology surfaces the right student at the right moment, and a person follows through. None of the results below belong to this study.

Georgia State University, text-based outreach

An AI chatbot answered enrollment, financial aid, and deadline questions by text at any hour. Summer melt fell by 22 percent, withdrawals from unpaid balances fell by half, and persistence rose by roughly 3 percent, representing about 1,300 additional students enrolled. First-generation and Pell-eligible students used it most, which is the population community colleges most need to reach. It is the most rigorously evaluated example in the literature, tested through multiple randomized controlled trials. See source 1.

City University of New York, coordinated wraparound support

The Accelerated Study in Associate Programs model addresses the common barriers at once, advising, money, transit, tutoring, and career planning, rather than leaving students to assemble supports alone. Three-year graduation reached 53 percent against 24.6 percent for a matched comparison group, and the model has been replicated at more than 60 institutions with the same result. The lesson for this work is that students struggle under an accumulation of barriers, so coordinated connection beats optimizing any single service in isolation. See source 2.

Valencia College, connected advising

An Aspen Prize winner that links faculty, staff, courses, technology, and services into one developmental advising system following students from entry through graduation. The match between students’ education plans and their actual course-taking rose from 43 percent to 60 percent. It is the conceptual ancestor of the connected student-support model this domain is working toward. See source 3.

Caring Campus, connection as a staff practice

A behavioral model rather than a technology program, spreading specific student-connecting behaviors among faculty and staff. It is implemented at more than 120 colleges, with positive retention findings reported by the Community College Research Center. It is the human infrastructure that makes AI-assisted outreach meaningful, because a tool can surface a student who needs help but a person has to follow through. See source 4.

Glendale Community College, peer success coaches

Local evidence for the same claim. Students matched with a peer coach retained at higher rates than those who were not, and the program was a 2023 to 2024 district Innovation of the Year finalist. It raises the question this initiative exists to answer: if connection to a person keeps students enrolled, what limits the scale of that connection? Where the limit is the routine load on a coordinator’s day, automation can let one coordinator support more students. See source 5.

What a pilot has to look like

Automation takes the routine, people take the rest

A factual question (where a class meets, when the aid deadline falls, what a balance is) can be answered instantly by a tool. That removes repetitive work from advising and financial aid staff so their hours go to the students whose situations require a person. The measure of success is more staff time where it counts, not a person in every interaction. No one is replaced.

Co-designed with the people who named the barrier

Pilots are built with the staff and students who flagged the need, not handed to them. Training comes before deployment, because staff have consistently ranked training as their first AI priority.

Adopted through invitation, not mandate

A friendly, peer-to-peer adoption effort, distributed at moments where people already gather, so an AI tool arrives as something staff and students helped shape. A small, low-pressure object or character, on the model of the stress ball that eased people into accessibility work they had been resisting, lowers anxiety and signals that trying the tool is safe and optional. This is a proposed approach, not a completed campaign.

Measured against a baseline

Each pilot carries a starting picture from the baseline and a target. Success is not measured by office or service usage alone. Counting office visits measures foot traffic, and a tool that answers a student in two minutes deliberately reduces those visits while improving the experience, so usage alone would score that as a failure. A year in, the committee asks whether the student’s need was met, how easily and how fast, whether the student persisted, across whatever channel served them, and whether routine work shifted off staff toward the students who need a person. Usage is tracked as a diagnostic of where students go, not as the definition of success: a drop in routine office traffic alongside steady or higher satisfaction and outcomes is success, not loss. The question is whether students are better served, not only whether the tool shipped. No one is replaced.

Build, adopt, or configure

A pilot does not have to be a new application. For each top-ranked barrier the committee takes the lightest option that works: adopt a tool that already exists, configure something already licensed (a plug-in or a feature inside a platform already running), or build an agent or workflow into one of the audited systems. The structure is the same across all three: automation surfaces the right student or the right resource, and a person follows through. The sequence is set by reach, beginning in the systems the most students already use.

An existing vendor tool, or a configured workflow inside a licensed platform, could solve a top-ranked barrier equally well, and any option that would collect student data is screened out before it is considered.

Two directions the prior evidence already points to

The ranked list can only come from the study, and the study has not been run. Two high-reach directions are nonetheless already indicated by the earlier local survey and by the persistence research, and both are stated here as hypotheses for the committee to test rather than as decisions.

Put help where the student already is

Students rarely stop out through a failure to try. They disengage and withdraw, often while working two jobs and caring for family, and frequently without knowing what to ask for. The indicated fix is not an additional website. It is the right resource appearing where the student already is: in the learning management system, at enrollment, in the first-year experience, inside the financial aid flow, and in a chat channel. The more places the help appears, the less a student has to know the name of the office that provides it. Plain-language help of this kind answers the simple questions and routes everything else to a person.

Equip faculty as the first line of support

Faculty are among the most trusted sources of referral and are usually the first to see a student struggling. Many still drop a student after three absences, which forces non-completion at precisely the moment the student is in crisis, working a double shift, or caring for a sick parent. This is student-support work rather than curriculum work, so it sits inside this domain’s scope. The proposed pilot is a five-minute briefing for first-year faculty and adjunct faculty: who the students actually are and the basic-needs, work, and caregiving pressures they carry; why an absence is a signal to connect rather than to cut; and the few specific things a faculty member can do in thirty seconds to point a student toward help. It follows the Caring Campus model, behavioral rather than mandated, and it is considerate of faculty who already carry a heavy load.

The five-minute faculty briefing is a candidate deliverable, not an approved one. It could be produced as a short slide deck for first-year experience and adjunct onboarding once the committee agrees to it.

Other candidates the evidence suggests

Three further directions are plausible and remain hypotheses until the ranked list either confirms or drops them. A plain-language intake and routing tool, the ambient version of the routing prototype, would touch nearly every student and speaks directly to the awareness gap. Proactive, channel-flexible outreach where students already are, by text or chat rather than by email, would reach the disengaged students least likely to come find the institution. And surfacing AI tools the district already licenses but underuses is a near-zero-cost option that the tool inventory is expected to locate.

Known sources of resistance

Three sources of resistance are documented here, each with the design decision that answers it.

Staff concern about being replaced

Willingness to learn AI is low where employees suspect the underlying goal is to eliminate their jobs. The design answers this directly. Every pilot keeps people in control and exists to remove routine work so that staff spend more time with students, not so that there are fewer staff. Training precedes any tool, pilots are co-designed with the front line, and adoption is invitational rather than mandatory.

Students who have been told AI is cheating

Many students have been told by faculty that using AI is cheating, and avoid it entirely. The tools contemplated here connect students to people and services and do not perform coursework, which places them outside the academic-integrity concern. The domain also works with the student AI group so that students learn what responsible, permitted use involves.

The environmental cost of the compute

The energy and water footprint of AI is substantial and it matters to this community. The design prefers tools already licensed, right-sized and efficient models over new heavy compute, and the lightest workflow that resolves the barrier. Not every fix requires a large model.

The persistence imperative

Committee: ARC, Domain 5. Status: Evidence brief, sourced. Prepared by: Domain 5 co-chairs. Updated: July 2026.

An evidence brief for faculty and academic leadership on why community college students do not persist, the support that already exists, and the practices through which faculty improve completion.

1. The strategic case

Decades of evidence, from the classic theories of student involvement and student departure through contemporary national research, converge on a well-replicated finding: students who feel connected to their institution and supported by its services persist at materially higher rates. For the populations these colleges serve, the support a student needs largely already exists. What is missing is the connection to it. Closing that gap is among the highest-return investments available in completion, and faculty, who are the first to observe disengagement and the most trusted source of referral, are central to it.

7. An underutilized support infrastructure

The institution operates more than twenty student-facing services. Most students, and many faculty, cannot name a fraction of what is available to them at no cost. Awareness, not availability, is the limiting factor.

  • Advising, counseling, financial aid, and scholarships
  • Basic Needs, the food pantry, emergency funds, book vouchers, childcare assistance, housing support, and campus social workers
  • Tutoring, Math Solutions, the Writing Center, and library research support
  • Disability Resources, ESL support, and health and wellness
  • Career services, veterans services, the transfer center, and Peer Success Coaches

Disability Resources serves students with ADHD, anxiety, and other non-apparent conditions, not only visible disabilities. A student who reports difficulty concentrating or completing timed assessments may qualify for accommodations. Awareness of five or more services is associated with a 13-point increase in persistence, yet only 55 percent of students report that support is communicated effectively. Faculty are the bridge across that gap.

8. Reconsidering punitive attrition

Administrative drops for early absence remove students at their most vulnerable point and frequently manufacture the non-completion they are intended to forestall. An early absence is a leading indicator of a student in difficulty, working an additional shift or managing a family emergency, rather than a measure of commitment. The evidence is consistent: proactive outreach and flexible pathways retain students, and removal does not. Where an attendance or drop policy affords no accommodation for a student in crisis, that policy operates against completion.

10. The faculty role

Faculty are the first to observe disengagement and the most trusted source of referral. Four practices, each a matter of seconds, materially shift outcomes.

  1. Reach out early. A brief check-in at the first sign of disengagement is frequently the single intervention that re-engages a student.
  2. Normalize help-seeking. Position services as ordinary practice rather than remediation. Stigma is a primary barrier to utilization.
  3. Refer with precision. One service, one named contact. Precision outperforms a catalogue of options.
  4. Offer a path, not an exit. An alternate means to submit or participate should precede any administrative drop.

11. The leverage of a single connection

Across more than thirty thousand graduates, the Gallup-Purdue Index found that a mentor who encouraged a student’s goals more than doubled that student’s odds of being engaged at work and thriving in life, and that a professor who cared about them as a person made them 1.9 times more likely to be engaged. Only about two in ten graduates report having had such a mentor. The role is not assigned. A professor, an advisor, a coach, a club sponsor, or a social worker can be the connection that keeps a student enrolled, and the institution’s task is to make that connection likely rather than incidental.

12. How we will measure whether it works

The study establishes a baseline first, capturing how students experience each service now: whether their need was met, how easily, how fast, and whether they persisted, with current usage recorded alongside. That baseline is the point of comparison. As we roll out any shared workflow or tool, success is judged by whether students’ needs are met more easily and faster and whether they persist, not by whether a tool was delivered and not by office-usage counts alone. Usage is a diagnostic of where students go: a tool that answers a routine question in two minutes reduces office visits by design, so a drop in routine traffic alongside steady or higher satisfaction and persistence is success, not loss. If the student experience and outcomes do not improve, the intervention did not work, and that is reported.
