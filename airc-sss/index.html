What this is

The purpose of the study and the method it uses.

Students rarely leave because a policy was wrong. They leave because they could not find an advisor, could not tell what dropping one class would do to their financial aid, or could not get a printer to work the day a paper was due. Those barriers are invisible in aggregate data and observable within minutes of walking the journey.

The study therefore walks the journey. A tester, human or AI, is given a persona, a campus, and one thing that persona needs, and is never told where to go, because a student is never told where to go. What breaks, and how badly, is logged and scored. The result is a map of where the district loses students, ranked by how many students each barrier touches, with a proposed fix attached to each one: work an AI can absorb, or work that belongs to a person.

The method pilot has already produced results. Twelve AI-tester walkthroughs across Glendale and Mesa found a catastrophic barrier on the first pass: at one college, the financial aid landing page carries no FAFSA deadline at all, which is the single date a Pell-dependent student cannot afford to miss. Two colleges also proved the translation problem is real, since the same service is called Basic Needs at one and Student Care Services at the other. These findings are provisional until a second rater confirms them.

The staff side of the journey

Not every gap needs a new tool. Some have already been solved at one college and never travelled to the other nine.

A student-facing barrier usually has a staff-facing cause. An advisor at one college may carry a caseload of 250 students while an advisor at another carries 700, and both are expected to answer the same questions. Somebody, somewhere in the district, is already doing that work better. Every student services department has strengths and weaknesses, and at present neither travels: the strength stays local and the weakness stays uncorrected.

So the study asks two things of the ten colleges alongside the persona runs.

  1. A call to student services leaders for their AI early adopters. Any advisor, financial aid specialist, or tutor who has already automated part of their own workflow, drafted an agent, or streamlined a process is asked to describe it. The study aggregates those workflows and publishes them across the ten colleges. A workflow that already works at one college is the cheapest win available, and it requires nobody to buy anything.
  2. Direct observation, if the call turns up nothing. Domain members divide the ten colleges and sit with advisors and tutors while they work, which is contextual inquiry rather than a survey, because staff cannot reliably report the steps they have automated in their own heads. The question being answered is narrow: which parts of this work are repetitive enough that an agent could carry them, so the staff member gets more time in front of a student.

The routine reply that reads “make an appointment and we will see you in two days” is a good example of the target. It is a barrier from the student’s side, and a symptom of caseload from the staff side. Some portion of what sits behind that reply is a factual question with a factual answer, and a student who receives that answer immediately may not need the appointment at all, which returns the appointment slot to the student whose problem actually requires a person. Nobody is replaced. The routine work is what goes.

The output of this track is therefore two kinds of recommendation, and the study treats them as equal: workflow revisions that can be shared across the district at no cost, and new tools built or adopted only where no existing practice closes the gap.

Staff buy-in is a study problem, not a communications problem

The people the study needs most are the people with the most to lose from getting this wrong.

Skepticism about AI among student services staff is not an obstacle to route around. It is a rational response to a technology that has been introduced elsewhere as a substitute for people, and it is the single largest risk to this study. A domain member who believes the outcome is a smaller department will not describe their own workflow honestly, and the study depends entirely on their describing it honestly.

Three commitments hold, and they are stated to staff before any observation is scheduled.

  1. The metric the study is trying to raise is human contact. If a recommendation reduces the number of students who reach a person, it has failed on its own terms, whatever else it improves.
  2. What automation removes is the repetitive factual work: the same question answered for the four hundredth time, the appointment-booking email, the routine status check. What it returns is time in front of a student. A department that reports no reduction in routine work after a pilot is reporting a finding, and the study records it as one.
  3. Staff are the authors of the fixes, not their subjects. An early-adopter workflow is published under the office that built it, and no observation is used to evaluate an individual’s performance. The study has no access to personnel data, and does not want any.

Students build the pilots

By students, for students, and the work is real.

The tools that come out of this study are built by student teams, working the way a design studio works: a brief, a client, a deadline, and a deliverable someone is waiting for. Several small teams, not one, each assembled across disciplines because the work is multidisciplinary. Computer science and software development students build the thing. Computer information systems and information technology students handle the data, the integration, and what it takes to run inside an institution. Digital media students design the interface and write the language a student actually reads. Business and project management students hold the schedule and the client relationship. Communication students carry the human side of the service being redesigned.

They leave with something rare in a portfolio: a shipped tool with a real user, a real constraint, and an evidence base behind it.

This is not a teaching exercise attached to a research project. It is the same argument the study makes about staff, applied to students: the people closest to the problem should be building the answer. A student who has been lost in the financial aid pages understands the barrier in a way a vendor never will, and a student who then builds the thing that closes it has learned more about design, accessibility, and consequence than any assignment could produce.

Every tool remains bound by the same rules as the study: no student data, human in the loop, and a fix is only worth building if it raises the number of students who reach a person who can help them.

The rules this study runs on

Four constraints govern the design of the study.

  • Problems, not destinations. A tester is given a need, never an office. A tester who cannot find advising has recorded a finding.
  • Translate, do not standardize. Every college keeps its own names and programs. The study maps them to one another and asks no college to align.
  • No student data. Runs are recorded against a scenario identifier and initials, never a student record. Any AI option that would collect student data is excluded before it is evaluated, so the preferred path is building data-sovereign tools rather than buying data-collecting ones.
  • No one is replaced. Automation takes the repetitive factual questions off advising and financial aid staff so their hours go to the students with real problems. Human contact is the measure the study is trying to raise.

Who runs the tests

The domain membership is the instrument.

Every district employee holds a Maricopa account, but access to student-facing systems is scoped to the employee’s own college. No individual can reach all ten. The Student Support and Success domain draws its members from all ten colleges, which is precisely what makes district-wide coverage possible: each member runs scenarios on the campus whose systems that member can actually reach. Students test on their own real accounts.

A volunteer takes one scenario in one sitting, roughly fifteen minutes. The low burden is deliberate, because volunteer capacity is the real constraint on a study of this size. AI testers run the same scenarios in parallel, which is how coverage extends beyond what volunteers can carry.

Known constraints

Conditions that bound what the study can complete, stated rather than absorbed.

  • The district advising tool is not yet accessible to the domain. Scenarios that depend on it (advising assignment and booking, aid impact at drop, consortium aid) are deferred to a second wave, to be run during or after that tool’s pilot. This is a stated limit on completeness, not an omission.
  • Persona validation with students has not been arranged. Design-studio students and the Student AI Group are possible routes in the fall, and no student capacity is assumed until it exists.
  • Coverage depends on volunteer participation across all ten colleges. One member per campus assigns scenarios and confirms coverage for that campus.

CopaMigo, the pilot that preceded the study

A tool built from the same premise, now positioned to be tested by it.

CopaMigo was built before this study was designed. The premise was the same one the study now sets out to evidence: students do not fail to use support because support is absent, they fail because they cannot name what they need or find who provides it. The persistence literature and the district’s own withdrawal data show students leaving without ever reaching a service, but no evidence existed at the level of the individual barrier. Rather than wait for it, the tool was built as a working hypothesis: a bilingual triage layer that takes a student’s plain-language problem and routes it to the correct human service, collecting no student data.

The study supplies what the tool has lacked. Barriers logged and severity-rated across ten colleges give CopaMigo an evidence base it was designed on faith, and the tool in turn gives the study a live candidate to evaluate against its own severity-by-reach ranking rather than a hypothetical one. Whether CopaMigo, a purchased alternative, or no tool at all is the right answer is a question the fieldwork should decide, and the same no-student-data line applies to every option.

Study background

Nothing in this section reports findings. The district study has not been run. Every quantity below is a target or a planned amount, and every result field in the instrument is empty until real runs happen. The one exception is the completed method pilot, which is reported under Findings.

The problem this study addresses

Findability, not availability.

Across the ten Maricopa colleges, the support that would keep students enrolled already exists on every campus. The failure is findability. Students cannot reach advising, financial aid, basic needs, accommodations, or the right person at the moment they need them.

A study with three personas walking thirty journeys can describe that problem. It cannot map it at the resolution the committee needs in order to prioritize where AI should close the gaps. The method below specifies a structured, repeatable instrument designed to produce a college-by-college, stage-by-stage map of where students hit barriers, with a severity judgment attached to each one.

Two methods, run together

Inspection and task testing surface different defects, and neither substitutes for the other.

  1. Expert heuristic evaluation. Evaluators inspect each college’s student-facing interfaces against Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics, working through the interface systematically and recording each violation with its location and a severity rating. This is an inspection method. No student and no persona is involved.
  2. Task-based usability testing. A tester, human or AI, adopts a persona and walks a real journey on the live systems, attempting one task from a felt need and narrating while working.

Both are needed. A heuristic evaluation catches violations a tester will silently work around without ever reporting them, and task testing exposes failures no heuristic predicts, such as a service that exists but is named something the persona would never search for.

The persona-per-test model

The unit of the task-based study is a single run: one persona, at one campus, attempting one task. The tester opens the instrument and receives a brief stating who the persona is, what barriers the persona carries, the one thing the persona is trying to do, and the campus. The tester then attempts the task from a real felt need, starting where a student would start, usually a search on the college website.

The protocol

Runs use a concurrent think-aloud protocol. The tester narrates while working, saying what they are looking for, what they expect a label to mean, and why they are clicking what they click. The narration is the data. It is what turns a wrong turn into an explanation of the wrong turn.

The tester is given a goal and never a destination, and the facilitator does not prompt toward a service, name an office, or confirm that a page is the right page. Telling a tester where to go destroys the finding the run exists to make, because findability is precisely what is under test. The facilitator intervenes only to prevent a genuine dead stop, and each intervention is recorded as an assist.

Why a single task per run

A single task isolates one barrier, so a failure is attributable to a specific point in the journey. It also keeps runs short, parallelizable, and easy to assign to many testers at once, which is what coverage requires.

Why a persona library rather than a few personas

A page that works for a digitally fluent transfer student can still fail a phone-first, Spanish-dominant parent on a slow connection. Only a varied library surfaces those intersections. The 36 personas reflect who actually enrolls: ESL and non-native speakers, veterans, autistic and ADHD students, food-insecure and first-generation students, working parents, returning adults, students who need Disability Resources, swirling multi-campus students, and dual-enrollment high schoolers.

Severity

Rated 0 to 4, following Nielsen. A run that finds nothing wrong is a real result and is logged as 0.

A severity score is not a measure of how irritating a barrier felt. It is a judgment that combines three factors: frequency, meaning how many students would meet this barrier; impact, meaning how hard it is to overcome once met; and persistence, meaning whether a student learns around it once or hits it again every single time. A small irritation that every student meets on every visit can outrank a dramatic failure that almost no one encounters.

Inter-rater reliability

Severity ratings are subjective, and a single rater’s number is an opinion rather than a measurement. Two raters therefore score each finding independently, without seeing each other’s score, and disagreements are adjudicated in a short review where both state their reasoning and a final score is agreed. Agreement is tracked, and the definitions above are tightened wherever raters keep diverging. This is what allows the committee to compare a severity 3 at one college with a severity 3 at another and treat them as the same claim. Every AI severity rating is treated as preliminary until a human rater confirms it, and that confirmation is the first of the two independent ratings.

Sample size

The study is formative. Its purpose is to find and fix defects, not to estimate a population parameter, so the sample is sized for defect discovery. A small number of testers per persona segment surfaces the large majority of the issues that are discoverable at all, and each additional tester on the same task returns fewer new defects than the one before. Adding a fifth or a tenth tester to the same task at the same college mostly re-finds what has already been found.

Coverage is therefore scaled on the axes that return new defects, which are personas, tasks, and colleges, and not by piling testers onto the same task. AI testers run the same scenarios in parallel, which extends coverage along those axes rather than deepening a sample that is already saturated. No fixed tester count is promised anywhere in this study, and none is needed for the design to hold.

Piloting the protocol

Before any district-wide fieldwork, the whole protocol is dry-run at a single college: the brief, the think-aloud, the capture form, the severity definitions, and the two-rater step. The dry run is a test of the instrument, not of the college. It answers whether the tasks are legible to a tester who is not the author, whether the fields capture what the analysis needs, and whether two raters can agree. Any wording that failed is repaired before the other nine colleges are touched.

Analysis

Findings are analyzed in three passes.

  1. Affinity mapping. Every logged barrier is written up as an observation and grouped with related observations until themes emerge from the data rather than from the categories the study started with. A theme is a pattern that holds across personas and colleges.
  2. Severity by reach. Each theme is placed on a matrix of severity against reach, where reach is how many of the roughly 140,000 students would plausibly meet it. High severity and high reach is where the first pilots go. High severity and low reach is documented and routed, not automated first.
  3. Journey mapping and service blueprinting. Themes are placed on the student journey to show where in the arc from application to completion a student is lost, and the worst are blueprinted, which means drawing the frontstage the student sees against the backstage staff actions that produce it. The blueprint is what makes the automation argument concrete, because the routine, repetitive backstage steps are exactly what an AI can absorb so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours on the students with real problems. No one is replaced.

Operations, tracking, and coverage

One scenario per tester, no duplication, every result logged, and a clear view of what remains.

  1. The bank is the single source of truth for assignment. Each scenario carries an ID. A college representative assigns one scenario to one tester by entering initials and a date on that row, so nothing is run twice and coverage is visible in the sheet itself.
  2. The capture form records a run: persona, college, task, path taken, outcome, time on task, assists, the barrier, severity, and the Single Ease Question score. It captures results well but does not assign work, so it stays as the capture instrument and its entries key back to the Scenario ID.
  3. The coverage dashboard reads the bank and shows, per college and per journey stage, how much has been walked and where the gaps are. It is how the co-chairs and the steering committee see progress without reading the sheet.

Recommendation: keep the existing capture form for recording results, and use the scenario bank as the assignment and coverage layer above it. This avoids building a new assignment system, reuses the live form, and keeps one ID linking a planned scenario to its logged result.

Fieldwork and delegation

How the fieldwork is organized, what is asked of each college, and who owns which piece.

Gathering the crosswalk and the usage baseline

The crosswalk requires two data points from each college for each service: the local name that college uses for the service, and roughly how many students it serves in a year. One contact, the service owner, answers those two questions for their own service. There is no long survey, no student-level reporting, and no audit.

The AI-agent walkthrough reads each college’s public site and drafts a first-pass crosswalk row for every service, so an owner who opens the intake finds the row mostly filled in. The owner is not building a grid from scratch. The owner is checking one row, correcting the local name where the draft is wrong, and adding an approximate annual figure. That reduces the task from about an hour to a few minutes, which is what makes it feasible for a busy advising or financial aid office.

Generating a fresh persona on demand

For large unmoderated runs, a tester does not have to reuse a fixed persona. A fresh persona is generated by combining attribute pools, so each run probes a different intersection of barriers. The method is repeatable and it keeps the no-PII rule intact, because nothing in it is a real person. A short generator prompt produces these briefs in batches for AI testers, drawing one value from each pool. The fixed library above is the core to be validated, and the generator extends it for breadth.

Draw the attributes

  • Age band: 17 to 19, 20 to 24, 25 to 34, 35 to 49, 50 and over.
  • Status: first generation, returning adult, veteran, international on an F-1 visa, DACA or Dreamer, dual enrollment, foster-care alumna, reentry.
  • Home language: English, Spanish, Arabic, Somali, Mandarin, Vietnamese, Korean, Japanese, other.
  • Access need: Disability Resources, autistic, ADHD, low bandwidth, phone only, after hours only.
  • Life pressure: food insecure, housing insecure, working parent, caregiver, full-time job.

Set the goal and write the brief

  1. Pick one goal that fits the draw: enroll, pay, plan, set up, fix, find support, transfer, graduate, or swirl.
  2. Pick one base task from the taxonomy that serves that goal, and assign one campus from the ten.
  3. Write one paragraph stating who the persona is, the barriers the persona carries, the one thing the persona wants, and the campus. No destination is named. The tester has to find the path.
  4. Record the run in the capture form against a new Scenario ID.

What the map produces

Two linked deliverables: an inventory of what exists, and a baseline of who reaches it.

The first deliverable is an inventory of the landscape: which student-facing services each of the ten colleges offers, so that the domain holds one shared list of what exists. The second is a baseline: how many students use each of those services now, out of how many are enrolled. The baseline is the before-measure. It is captured now, tools are deployed, and any lift (more students reaching support, and more of them persisting) is measured against it later.

DeliverableWhat it covers
District tool inventoryThe systems every college shares: the student information system, Canvas, the financial aid system, the portal and login, and any district-licensed platforms. What everyone runs, and who owns each.
Campus tool inventoryThe platforms, practices, and vendor relationships an individual college has built that the others may not know exist. One college’s working early-alert workflow or career tool is another college’s missing piece.
The service crosswalkA grid of student-facing functions down the side and the ten colleges across the top, with each college’s own local name in the cell. At a glance, what exists where, and what is absent.
The usage baselineHow many unique students used each service in the last year, out of how many were enrolled. Counts and rates only, no student-level data.

An inventory, not a reorganization

Each of the ten Maricopa colleges is independently accredited, with its own programs, its own organizational structure, and its own names for things, and that arrangement stands. Nothing here asks any college to rename, align, or change anything. The work is to inventory which student-facing services exist at each college and how many students use each one.

The inventory comes first because the rest of the study depends on it. The personas cannot walk a journey without knowing which services a college offers. The baseline cannot be counted against a service list that does not exist. A pilot cannot be ranked by reach without knowing which colleges offer the service it would touch.

The inventory produces two kinds of finding. The first is hidden tools: a platform or program that one college already runs and the others do not know exists. Surfacing it allows a proven tool to spread, often at no new cost. BigInterview, the interview and resume platform, is the concrete case: Career Services at Glendale Community College has access to it, and whether the other nine colleges do is the question the inventory answers. The second is coverage gaps. “Not offered here” is a valid and useful answer. It surfaces both the cases where one college can share what it has and the gaps no college has solved, which are the ones that feed the recommendations.

Reading the crosswalk

Each student-facing function is a row, and each of the ten colleges supplies its own local name. It is a translation layer, not a push to standardize. The colleges appear in the entries below in a fixed order, in short form: Chandler-Gilbert (CGCC), Estrella Mountain (EMCC), GateWay, Glendale (GCC), Mesa (MCC), Paradise Valley (PVCC), Phoenix, Rio Salado, Scottsdale (SCC), and South Mountain (SMCC). Where a function is identical district-wide, the entry says so rather than repeating the same name ten times.

Two markers carry meaning. “Verify” means a representative should confirm the entry with their own office. “Not found” means no public-facing service could be located, which is a finding to confirm, not an empty cell.

The usage baseline

The before-measure. Without a baseline taken now, no lift can be shown later.

The inventory lists the services. The second half of this work counts how many students use each one, out of how many are enrolled. The measure is captured before deployment so that any change after deployment can be demonstrated. The baseline matters because current usage is likely to be low and is largely unmeasured. Only 55 percent of students say their college communicates support well, and at two-year colleges fewer than 10 percent of students with a disability receive accommodations, against 28 percent at four-year institutions. The services exist. The connection to them does not.

What is asked

At each college, one committee member sits down with the Dean of Student Life, the office that already oversees these services and holds the usage numbers. The conversation runs about fifteen minutes and covers the same questions each time.

  • How many unique students used each service last year.
  • What share of eligible students that represents, if it can be estimated.
  • How the office believes students find them, and how students actually do.
  • Where demand exceeds capacity.
  • What the office wishes students knew before they arrived.

Counts and estimates are sufficient. No student-level or identifying data is requested, and nothing is attributed to an individual. The framing is stated plainly in the conversation: this is a study of whether better student-to-service connection improves persistence, and it requires a starting number. It is not an audit and it is not a step toward replacing anyone. Automation is intended to take routine, repetitive work off staff so that human contact between students and the people who help them expands rather than contracts.

What the baseline gives the study

  • A district-comparable picture of who is reached now.
  • The denominator needed to rank pilots by student reach.
  • A before-number to measure usage and persistence gains against.
  • Early wins, where a service is strong at one college and missing at another.
  • The capacity ceilings that automation could help lift.

How the data is protected

  • Departments own their data. The study aggregates and never exposes.
  • No student PII. Counts and rates only.
  • What the district systems and institutional research already hold is confirmed before anything is duplicated.
  • Findings are shared back with departments before anything is final.

How the inventory gets done

  1. Assemble the first-pass service inventory from each college’s public website. Done, covering more than fifty functions across ten colleges, and unverified.
  2. Recruit one representative per college.
  3. Each representative verifies and corrects only that college’s own column, and adds baseline numbers on the metrics tab.
  4. Mark “not offered here” wherever it applies, as a finding rather than a blank.
  5. Lock a shared function list that the barriers study and the recommendations build on.

No college is asked to rename or restructure anything. Representatives own their column and the domain aggregates. Local programs, including campus-specific basic-needs models, are captured as they are, and the map stays a living document as colleges change. Working data was assembled from each college’s public website in June 2026, contains no student data, and is for committee use. The live version is the working spreadsheet.

Method pilot, public-site walkthrough

A twelve-walk test of the instrument at two colleges, run to show that the method produces usable records. It is not a district finding.

The study method is to walk the student journey the way a student walks it, from a felt need rather than a URL, and to record every barrier encountered. This is the first live exercise of that method, deliberately kept small: one AI tester, two colleges, public pages only, no account and no login. The purpose is not to grade the two colleges. It is to establish that the instrument produces concrete, decision-ready records, and to check whether the capture fields hold what the analysis will need.

FAFSA deadline, Mesa, preliminary severity 4

What the pilot suggests, provisionally

Two colleges is a small sample and nothing here generalizes to the district. What the pilot does establish is that the capture fields hold the right information and that the walks produce specific, actionable records. Three patterns recur across the twelve walks. Each is a hypothesis the scaled study is built to test, not a conclusion.

  1. The same service carries a different name at each college. Tutoring is the Center for Learning at Glendale and the Learning Enhancement Center at Mesa. Basic needs is GCC Cares at one and Student Care Services, with the Mesa Market pantry, at the other. A student who learns the term at one college cannot reuse it at the next. This is the service-crosswalk problem, observed in a live walk.
  2. The service exists and the label conceals it. GCC Cares is a substantial service sitting behind a sub-anchor under Student Life, and Glendale’s FAFSA deadline is published, but not on the page named Important Dates. In this pilot the gap was almost never a missing service. It was that the student could not reach the service from the words the student would use.
  3. Answers sit where an assistant cannot reach them. Steps in a downloadable document, pages that render blank without JavaScript, and unlabeled system links are all invisible to a student’s AI assistant. As students arrive carrying their own assistants, those services become effectively unreachable for them.

The opportunities named in this pilot are factual and public: state the FAFSA deadline, surface tutoring hours, accept “I need food” in plain language and route to the pantry. None of that requires a name, a login, or a record, which keeps it inside the no-student-data line the district holds. It also removes routine work from staff desks. When the page answers the repeated factual questions, the financial aid specialist and the advisor stop fielding them and the “we will get back to you” email shrinks, so the student with a genuinely complicated situation reaches a person sooner. No one is replaced.

Limits of this pilot

  • Two colleges of ten, one AI tester, one pass. These are illustrative patterns and not a district finding.
  • Public pages only, by design. The authenticated journey (registration, holds, Student Center, naming an advisor) is unwalked, and that is where the deeper barriers usually sit.
  • Severity is preliminary. Every rating is a single AI tester’s first pass, without the second independent rater and the adjudication the method requires, and without think-aloud validation from real students. A 4 here is a flag for fieldwork, not a final score.
  • Sites change. The pages were live when the pilot ran. A page that rendered blank or a date that was missing may already have been corrected, which is worth re-checking.
  • Nothing named here is a built tool. The opportunities are candidate directions screened against the no-student-data line, and no pilot has been approved or built.

How this survey shaped the study

Three properties of this data drove the design of the district instrument. First, the reported barriers cluster in findability, hours, and hand-offs rather than in the absence of a service: the help usually exists and the student cannot reach it. Second, the highest-rated items are concentrated in advising, enrollment, financial aid, and basic needs, which is where the study puts its first scenarios. Third, the ratings here are single-rater and unadjudicated, which is exactly the weakness the district study corrects by defining severity on the Nielsen 0 to 4 scale, judging it on frequency, impact, and persistence, and having two independent raters score each finding.

The routine, repeatable questions in this list, hours, deadlines, where to go, who to ask, are the ones automation can absorb so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours on the students whose situations require a person. No one is replaced.

Why students do not persist

The evidence base under the study. This section reports published research and one internal survey, not results from the district barriers study.

Students who feel connected to their institution and supported by its services persist to completion at higher rates. This is among the most replicated findings in higher education research. Astin’s theory of involvement and Tinto’s student departure model established it in the 1970s and 1980s, and decades of subsequent work have sharpened rather than overturned it. The premise tells the domain what to measure. If connection and support are what keep students enrolled, then the quantity worth baselining is how well students are actually reaching support right now.

Four pressures converge

Asked directly, students name pressures that are rarely academic. They are the pressures of adult life meeting an institution that assumes a student will come and find it, and the community college population carries them more heavily than any other group in higher education.

PressureWhat the research shows
Wellbeing and stressIn the 2024 Lumina and Gallup study, 35 percent of students had considered leaving in the previous six months. Emotional stress led the reasons at 54 percent, mental health followed at 43 percent, and cost at 31 percent. The reasons students give describe wellbeing rather than academic performance.
Basic needsThe Hope Center survey of 74,350 students in 2023 to 2024 found 59 percent facing food or housing insecurity. Among students who stopped out or were at risk of stopping out, 79 percent cited basic needs and financial challenges. This is the majority experience, not an edge case.
WorkAbout 80 percent of community college students work and 39 percent work full time. Nearly two-thirds of students who start at a two-year college work twenty or more hours a week. Enrollment is fitted into the margins of a working life.
CaregivingMore than one in five community college students are parents. In the 2024 Community College Survey of Student Engagement, 71 percent of caregiving students said caregiving could cause them to drop out. Student parents complete at 18 percent within six years, against 54 percent for traditional students.

The services that would relieve these pressures exist on every campus. A student working two jobs and caring for a parent does not have time to work out which office to call, and often does not recognize a personal situation as the thing a given service was built for. Only 55 percent of students say their institution communicates support well, and 18 percent actively disagree. The gap between stopping out and staying enrolled is frequently a gap in awareness and connection rather than in service availability.

A local signal consistent with the national pattern

The case above rests on large multi-year national studies. The evidence that follows is much smaller, a single internal survey at Glendale Community College in 2025, and it is weighted accordingly. It is a local check against the national pattern, and it is consistent with it. Faculty and staff documented 83 barriers they watch students hit, rating them 4.5 out of 5 on average for both frustration and impact. The largest categories were not classroom problems. Sorted by what the barrier is about: student access (21), communication (19), reaching a service (19), and campus technology such as printing, wireless access, login, and lab availability (14), with comfort needs, textbooks, and printing close behind. Sorted by which department owns the fix, the most-named were information technology, advisement, and basic needs.

The basic-needs pressure is documented locally as well. Glendale Community College Student Services reports that 44 percent of its students are food insecure. One survey does not describe a district of ten colleges, but it points in the same direction as the national data: the services exist, and students cannot reliably find them, reach them, or afford to use them.

Connecting the evidence to the intervention

The intervention works where the connection is made. Among caregiving students, those who persisted were considerably more likely than those who stopped out to report that their college had provided financial, basic-needs, and childcare support. Locally, the Glendale Community College Peer Success Coach program found that students matched with a coach retained at higher rates than those who were not, which is local evidence that connection to a person keeps students enrolled.

The pieces of the domain’s work connect in sequence. The inventory maps where the services are and counts how many students reach them now, which is the baseline. The barriers study locates where students fail to reach them. This evidence establishes why that failure matters. The recommendations then rank fixes by how many students each would touch. Automation answers routine factual questions and books the routine appointment so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours with the students who have a real problem. No one is replaced.

What a volunteer opens

The single sheet a tester reads before a session: the persona, the test login, the college, and the tasks to work in order.

  1. Read the assignment. The college representative supplies one persona, the test login created for that persona at that college, and the path. Testers run only the campus whose systems they can reach, because a login reaches only its own college.
  2. Work the tasks in order, out loud. Each task is a felt need, never a place to go. The tester starts where a student would start, usually a search on the college website, and finds their own way, narrating what they are looking for, what they expect a link to do, and why they clicked it. Insider knowledge is not used, and no one points the tester toward an office.
  3. Record each task: the outcome (success, partial success, failure), the time on task, the number of assists, the path taken, where the tester landed, the ease score, and a severity score. One record per task in the capture form.

One run is one persona at one college, attempted in character, in a single sitting of roughly fifteen minutes. The narration matters as much as the result, because it is what turns a wrong turn into an explanation of the wrong turn.

Logging a run

Each task is recorded as a separate submission, immediately after the run, while it is fresh. One task equals one submission, logged in the capture form: open the capture form.

  • No student PII. Testers use their own student or test account, initials only, never a real student’s name or identifier.
  • The record includes the barrier, the point at which the tester got stuck, the number of dead ends, whether a human was needed, the time on task, the ease score, and a severity score.
  • The local name the college uses for the service is captured as written. Names are translated, never standardized.
  • A point at which the persona would plausibly have given up is a finding. It is logged as such.
  • A service that does not exist at a college is recorded as not offered there. Dead ends, confusing pages, and error messages are captured as screenshots.
