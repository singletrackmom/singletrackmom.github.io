Student Journey Gap Analysis

AI Resource Center · Student Support and Success

By Michelle Blomberg, Co-chair, Student Support and Success domain, AI Resource Center.

A ten-college study across the Maricopa district that builds fifty demographic-grounded personas, sends synthetic students to walk the real student journey one task at a time, and finds where students hit barriers, so support staff can lift routine work off their desks without anyone being replaced.

Status: planning and instrument-building. Fieldwork has not started and no runs have been logged, so nothing here reports results. The design is described, the counts are the plan, and nothing is in production.

What this is

A structured way to find where students lose their way, ranked by how many students each gap touches.

Across the Maricopa district, ten colleges each run advising, financial aid, basic needs, and other support in their own way. A student rarely knows which office to ask, and the path from a felt need to the right person is full of dead ends. This study walks that path on purpose, at scale, so the domain can see the gaps clearly and decide where an AI tool, a shared staff workflow, or a person is the right answer.

Goal

Map where students in the district hit barriers reaching support, rank each gap by how many students it touches, and hand staff located, severity-rated evidence they can act on.

Audience

The Student Support and Success domain of the district AI Resource Center, made up of advisors, financial-aid, and other support staff across all ten colleges, plus the college leaders and faculty who help run the study and recruit the student build teams.

Process

Build fifty demographic-grounded personas, send synthetic students to run each one against a real task at a real college, log what breaks and how badly with more than one rater, prioritize the highest-reach gaps, decide where AI fits first, then have paid student teams build the fixes as human-in-the-loop tools that collect no student data. Humans decide what to fix. No one is replaced.

The core concept

Fifty whole personas mirror the district’s real enrollment demographics. Each one is carried intact by a synthetic student that runs a single task at a single college on public pages only, so the study reaches a scale across all ten colleges no team of people could cover, while never touching student data.

The full committee plan is maintained as a separate working document. These tabs are the readable version.

The students

Fifty whole personas, each a full student, built to mirror the district’s real demographics, carried by synthetic students.

The study builds a fixed set of 50 personas. Each persona is a whole student, carried through a test intact: race and ethnicity, first-generation status, Pell eligibility, food security, veteran status, parenting, disability, language, and enrollment pattern all live together in one person, in roughly the proportions the district actually enrolls. Nothing is split apart. The set is a proportioned cross-section of who really shows up, so the barriers it surfaces are the barriers real students hit, weighted by how common each kind of student is.

A synthetic student then carries one whole persona at a time: one persona, one college, one task that starts from a felt need, never from an office, because a student is never told where to go. Every finding is severity-rated on the Nielsen 0 to 4 scale by more than one rater and traces to a specific barrier on a specific page at a specific college, so results are inspectable and comparable across the ten colleges. The synthetic students touch only public pages, so there is no student data and no privacy exposure, and they run at a scale across all ten colleges that no team of people could reach. Humans still decide what any finding means. No one is replaced.

Research and testing

Expert heuristic evaluation plus task-based usability testing, synthetic-first and honestly validated.

The method pairs expert heuristic evaluation with task-based usability testing. Findings are scored on the Nielsen 0 to 4 severity scale by more than one rater, mapped onto a journey map of the student path, and lined up against a service crosswalk so the same function can be compared across colleges even when each college names it differently.

Synthetic-first, then validated

Synthetic students run the full findability matrix, every task at every college, on public pages. Because those pages need no login, full campus coverage becomes possible, and one Maricopa MEID reaches all ten colleges anyway. Being honest: AI synthetic users are unproven and can be confidently wrong. They are validated against human testers on a sample before the study trusts them, and if they do not prove reliable, the study leans on human sampling instead. Humans do the in-person walk-ins, an authenticated sample on district-wide accounts, and the validation sample.

A finding it catches

The study captures obvious frictions like appointment-booking delay. At one advising office, for example, you submit a form or email and then wait for a callback rather than booking a time online. Stated factually, that is the kind of routine wait an in-office agent could remove, so staff spend their time on the students who need a person.

Prioritize and decide

Rank the gaps, then decide where AI fits first and whether to buy or build.

Once the runs surface the barriers, the domain ranks each gap by how many students it touches and how feasible the fix is. A gap that blocks thousands of first-generation students on a common task outranks a rare edge case, so the effort goes where it changes the most journeys.

Where AI fits first

Not every gap wants an AI answer. Some are a broken link or a confusing label a person fixes in an afternoon. AI is aimed at the routine, repetitive work that eats staff time, the same factual question answered for the four hundredth time and the “make an appointment and we will get back to you” reply. Those are the first candidates for automation.

Buy versus build

Every option is screened against the no-student-data line. The district declined a vendor AI add-on specifically because it would collect student data, so the preferred path is building data-sovereign tools the district controls over buying data-collecting ones. Where a bought tool genuinely fits and clears the data line, it stays on the table, but the default leans to build.

In-office agents that lift routine work

The fixes are designed as in-office agents that take routine, repetitive work off staff desks, not as replacements. An agent that answers the same factual question or books the appointment that used to trigger a callback frees an advisor for the student sitting in front of them with a real problem. The staff in this domain are advisors and financial-aid and support staff, so the whole design reads as making their jobs easier. No one is replaced.

Build with students

Paid, multidisciplinary student teams ship the fixes in one semester.

Each chosen fix becomes a project for a paid, multidisciplinary student team that builds over one semester, January to May 2027. Teams are selected by the domain co-chairs and funded through grant writers, so students are paid for real work, not asked to volunteer. They leave with a shipped tool built for a real user, not a class exercise, which is the whole point: tools that outlast the semester.

For a support-staff domain, the first fix is often the cheapest one, a workflow a staff member at one college already streamlined, shared to the other nine, asking nobody to buy anything. The bigger builds go to the student teams under the same no-student-data rule that governs every option.

Pilots and production

Prove each fix in staged pilot rounds before anything reaches production.

A built tool does not go straight to every student. Each fix runs in staged pilots, is measured against the success measures, revised, and only then moved toward production. Nothing here is in production today.

  • Through August 31, 2026 · Findability testing across the ten colleges. Validate the synthetic students against human testers on a sample.
  • September to December 2026 · Prioritize the gaps, decide where AI fits first, and settle buy versus build.
  • January to May 2027 · Paid student teams build the chosen fixes over one semester.
  • Spring 2027 · Pilot round 1.
  • Fall 2027 · Pilot round 2.
  • January 2028 · Pilot round 3 and production start.

Every pilot collects no student data and keeps a human in the loop. A fix that does not clear the success measures is revised or dropped, never shipped on schedule alone.

Every fix is judged by what happened for the student, measured four ways:

  • Need met. Did the student actually reach the right support and resolve what they came for.
  • Ease and satisfaction. How easy and satisfying it felt, captured with the Single Ease Question right after the task.
  • Time to resolution. How long it took to get from the felt need to a resolved outcome.
  • Persistence. Whether the student persisted, completed, and transferred over time.

Office usage is not the measure. A busy office is not a working journey, and a quiet office is not a failing one. Automation is aimed at routine, repetitive work, so staff are freed for the students who need a person. No one is replaced.
