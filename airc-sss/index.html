Student Journey Gap Analysis

Case study · AI for learning · Agentic builds · UX research

A ten-college study of the Maricopa student journey that walks the real path from finding Maricopa to work, so support staff can lift routine work off their desks without anyone being replaced.

What the study does

A structured way to find where students lose their way, ranked by how many students each gap touches.

Across the Maricopa district, ten colleges each run advising, financial aid, basic needs, and other support in their own way. Before a student can even apply, they have to find Maricopa, and from there the path from a felt need to the right person is full of dead ends. This study walks that whole path on purpose, at scale, so the domain can see the gaps clearly and decide where an AI tool, a shared staff workflow, or a person is the right answer. It covers the student-services journey up to and around the classroom, not inside Canvas or the coursework itself, since the in-course experience belongs to the Teaching and Learning domain, Domain 2.

Goal

Set a baseline of how many students use each service now, then find and rank where students hit barriers reaching support by how many students each gap touches, and hand staff located, severity-rated evidence they can act on. A successful AI tool answers routine questions without a booking or an office visit, so help is counted through the tool even as office traffic falls. Paid student teams build the tools, not the staff workflows, which the ARC team and the department that owns the service lead.

Audience

The Student Support and Success domain of the district AI Resource Center, made up of advisors, financial-aid, and other support staff across all ten colleges, plus the college leaders and faculty who help run the study and recruit the student build teams.

Process

Build fifty demographic-grounded personas as specialist agents, add one orchestrator agent that sequences the journey and routes the runs, and walk the path from finding Maricopa through to work. The full synthetic matrix runs every persona against every task at every college, 50 personas times 50 tasks times 10 colleges, 25,000 synthetic runs. Log what breaks and how badly with more than one rater, prioritize the highest-reach gaps, decide where AI fits first, then have paid student teams build the fixes as human-in-the-loop tools that collect no student data. Humans decide what to fix. No one is replaced.

The core concept

Each persona is its own specialist agent, built to mirror the district’s real enrollment demographics, and one orchestrator agent sequences the journey and routes every run. The persona agents run the public findability matrix with no login, and the authenticated tasks on a single OIT-controlled test account that holds no real student data, so the full matrix, 25,000 synthetic runs across all ten colleges, reaches a scale no team of people could cover while never touching a real student’s data.

The students

Fifty whole personas, each a full student and its own specialist agent, built to mirror the district’s real demographics and coordinated by one orchestrator agent.

The study builds a fixed set of 50 personas. Each persona is a whole student, carried through a test intact: race and ethnicity, first-generation status, Pell eligibility, food security, veteran status, parenting, disability, language, and enrollment pattern all live together in one person, in roughly the proportions the district actually enrolls. Nothing is split apart. The set is a proportioned cross-section of who really shows up, so the barriers it surfaces are the barriers real students hit, weighted by how common each kind of student is.

Each persona is its own specialist agent, not just a written profile. It is an AI agent instantiated with one persona’s traits, then set loose to autonomously browse the live college website the way any member of the public would in a browser: no special access, no login, since findability is tested on public pages. The agent stays constrained to that student’s knowledge and behavior, so it hits dead ends and gives up the way a real student would rather than using insider shortcuts. It thinks aloud as it goes and returns a structured, inspectable finding at the end. A written persona alone cannot do that; the agent actually walks the path.

Above the persona agents sits one orchestrator agent. It sequences the journey, from finding Maricopa through to work, and routes each run to the right persona agent at the right college, so the whole matrix stays organized and comparable. The personas stay whole; the orchestrator coordinates them, it does not flatten them.

Each persona agent carries one whole persona at a time: one persona, one college, one task that starts from a felt need, never from an office, because a student is never told where to go. The full synthetic matrix runs every persona through every task at every college, 50 personas times 50 tasks times 10 colleges, 25,000 synthetic runs, a scale only synthetic agents make possible. Every finding is severity-rated on the Nielsen 0 to 4 scale by more than one rater and traces to a specific barrier on a specific page at a specific college, so results are inspectable and comparable across the ten colleges. The persona agents touch only public pages, so there is no student data and no privacy exposure. Humans still decide what any finding means, run the 80 in-person walk-ins, and run a small online validation sample, about one tester per campus and roughly ten in all, to check the synthetic findings. No one is replaced.

Modality and the online learner

Each of the 50 personas carries a study modality, all online, some online, or all in person, assigned to fit that student’s real situation rather than at random. Full-time working parents, adult returners juggling a job, students with transportation or childcare barriers, and students in programs commonly delivered online are the online ones; recent high-school graduates living at home, dual-enrollment students, and hands-on lab and clinical programs are on campus. The mix is proportioned to a national public two-year baseline, since Maricopa does not publish its own split: roughly a quarter of students exclusively online, roughly another quarter mixing online and in person, and about 45 percent entirely in person. Across the 50 personas that yields 13 all online, 14 some online, and 23 all in person, so most personas take at least some coursework online. The proportion is a placeholder to be confirmed with Maricopa Institutional Research.

Online and distance students are a distinct, time-constrained population, older, more often working, and disproportionately parents and caregivers, who enroll online precisely because work, family, or transportation rule out coming to campus. That makes modality a barrier the study is built to surface. A fully online persona cannot come to campus, so any service offered only in person is effectively unreachable for that student, and an online-only persona that hits an in-person-only service is a finding, not an error. Lower success in online courses reflects who enrolls, a busier, more time-constrained student, not the modality itself.

Research and testing

Expert heuristic evaluation plus task-based usability testing, synthetic-first and honestly validated.

The method pairs expert heuristic evaluation with task-based usability testing. Findings are scored on the Nielsen 0 to 4 severity scale by more than one rater, mapped onto a journey map of the student path, and lined up against a service crosswalk so the same function can be compared across colleges even when each college names it differently.

The method, in detail

Two techniques work together. In expert heuristic evaluation, evaluators inspect each college’s student-facing pages against Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics and record every violation with its location and a severity rating, an inspection method that needs no student. In task-based usability testing, a tester, synthetic or human, adopts a persona and walks a real journey on the live site, attempting one task from a felt need and narrating throughout in a concurrent think-aloud, so the narration is the data. Heuristic evaluation catches violations a tester silently works around, and task testing exposes failures no heuristic predicts, like a service that exists but is named something the persona would never search for.

Findability gets its own instruments. First-click testing checks whether the very first click lands toward the right place, since a service named in staff language is invisible to the student looking for it. Right after each task the tester answers the Single Ease Question, a 7-point rating of how easy the task felt. Findings are severity-rated on the Nielsen 0 to 4 scale by more than one rater, then mapped onto a journey map of the student path and a service blueprint that shows the staff-side steps behind each student-facing moment, and lined up against a service crosswalk so the same function compares across colleges even when each names it differently.

Two testing lanes

Authenticated testing runs on a single OIT-controlled test account, a sanctioned account with no real student data, not even a member’s. The work is organized into two lanes:

  • Public findability, no login. Synthetic and human testers attempt the public college site without signing in, testing whether a student can find and follow the path to a service. The persona agents carry this lane at scale, which makes it the central lane for coverage.
  • Authenticated navigation on the OIT test account. Signed in on the single OIT-controlled test account, testers check whether they can reach the advisor, the payment page, aid status, add and drop, transcripts, and the accommodations request. OIT stages the account into the state each task needs, so no real student data is touched; the walk-through stops at any payment screen, and the log keys to the scenario, not the person.

First-time actions, applying, first login, and first-time enrollment, are walked on the public site as findability, and the signed-in steps run on the single test account, which OIT resets so onboarding can be repeated. Where possible, a short think-aloud with a few real incoming students adds a human check.

Synthetic-first, then validated

The synthetic students carry the online testing at scale: the persona agents run the full synthetic matrix, every persona through every task at every college, starting with how a prospective student even chooses a college, before anyone applies. The public findability tasks need no login, and the authenticated tasks run on the single OIT-controlled test account, which reaches all ten colleges on one login. AI synthetic users are unproven and can be confidently wrong, so synthetic findings stay candidates until humans validate them. Humans do two things the synthetic students do not. First, the 80 in-person walk-ins at each physical office, which cannot be done synthetically and are their own human task, not the part the synthetics test. Second, a small online validation sample, about one tester per campus and roughly ten in all, who run the same online tasks the synthetic students run so the results can be checked against the synthetic data. That online human validation is what confirms the synthetic findings are real, and it is deliberately much smaller than the synthetic scale. If the synthetic layer does not hold up against it, the study leans on human sampling instead.

Accounts

Public findability needs no login. The authenticated sample runs on a single OIT-owned, OIT-controlled test account, a sanctioned account with no real student data that reaches every college on one login. The domain has requested it, and it has not yet been granted.

Coverage is the full matrix: every one of the 50 personas runs every one of the 50 tasks at all 10 colleges (CGCC, EMCC, GWCC, GCC, MCC, PVCC, PC, RIO, SCC, SMCC), which is 50 × 50 × 10 = 25,000 synthetic runs, a scale only synthetic agents make possible. Humans run 80 in-person walk-ins (8 offices × 10 colleges) and a small online validation sample, about one tester per campus and roughly ten in all, that repeats the same online tasks to check the synthetic findings. No runs are logged yet.

A finding it catches

The study captures obvious frictions like appointment-booking delay. At one advising office, for example, you submit a form or email and then wait for a callback rather than booking a time online. Stated factually, that is the kind of routine wait an in-office agent could remove, so staff spend their time on the students who need a person.

Prioritize and decide

Rank the gaps, then decide where AI fits first and whether to buy or build.

Once the runs surface the barriers, the domain groups the findings into themes and weighs two things together. Impact is how many of the roughly 140,000 students a fix reaches and how severe the barrier is on the Nielsen 0 to 4 scale. Feasibility is what the fix costs in money, time, and staff effort. The aim is the greatest improvement per unit of resource, so a gap that blocks thousands of first-generation students on a common task outranks a rare edge case, and a high-impact, low-cost fix can rank above a costlier tool that would reach more students but exceeds available funding. Where the strongest solution is a purchase beyond current budget, it is documented and deferred rather than dropped, so the record stays honest about what money, not merit, is holding back.

Where AI fits first

Not every gap wants an AI answer. Some are a broken link or a confusing label a person fixes in an afternoon. AI is aimed at the routine, repetitive work that eats staff time, the same factual question answered for the four hundredth time and the “make an appointment and we will get back to you” reply. Those are the first candidates for automation.

Buy versus build

Every option is screened against the no-student-data line before it is considered, not after. The preferred path is building data-sovereign tools the district controls over buying data-collecting ones. Where a bought tool genuinely fits and clears the data line, it stays on the table, but the default leans to build.

In-office agents that lift routine work

The fixes are designed as in-office agents that take routine, repetitive work off staff desks, not as replacements. An agent that answers the same factual question or books the appointment that used to trigger a callback frees an advisor for the student sitting in front of them with a real problem. The staff in this domain are advisors and financial-aid and support staff, so the whole design reads as making their jobs easier. No one is replaced.

Who changes the workflow

Deciding what to fix and how a service should work is staff-led. The domain committee and the AI experts on the AI Resource Center, together with the department that owns the service, design any workflow change, because they understand the service and carry the accountability for it. The paid student teams build the tools that support those decisions, they do not set staff workflow. Students build, the committee and the service owners decide.

Build with students

Paid, multidisciplinary student teams ship the fixes in one semester, the way a design studio works: a brief, a client, a deadline, and a deliverable someone is waiting for.

Once the gaps are ranked and the domain has decided where AI fits first, each chosen fix becomes a real project for a paid student team. The teams are assembled after the analysis has run, not before, because there is nothing to build until the barriers are known and prioritized. For a support-staff domain the first fix is often the cheapest one, a workflow a staff member at one college already streamlined, shared to the other nine, asking nobody to buy anything. The bigger builds, the in-office agents and student-facing tools, go to the student teams under the same no-student-data rule that governs every option.

Who builds, and from which programs

The work is multidisciplinary, so the teams are recruited across disciplines, each program carrying the part of the build it is trained for. The domain draws students from five areas across the district:

  • Computer science and software development, who build the tools themselves, the agents and the code behind them.
  • Computer information systems and information technology, who handle the data, the integration, and what it takes to run a tool inside the institution.
  • Digital media, who design the interface and write the language a student actually reads, so the tool is findable and clear.
  • Business and project management, who hold the schedule and the client relationship and keep the build on track.
  • Communication, who carry the human side of the service being redesigned.

The team model

The fixes are built by three multidisciplinary teams of four students each, twelve students in all, drawn from across the ten colleges and led by AI Resource Center members. Each team works one semester, January to May 2027, against a real user, a real constraint, and a real deliverable. The domain co-chairs select the students directly, so finding students is never the constraint. A student who has been lost in the financial-aid pages understands the barrier firsthand, and a student who then builds the tool that closes it gains applied experience in design, accessibility, and consequence. Every tool the teams build stays bound by the same rules as the study: responsible, minimal use of student data, a human in the loop, and a fix that raises the number of students who reach a person who can help them.

Paying the students

The binding constraint is money to pay the students, and the plan treats it as the thing to solve first. This is paid work, not volunteer or class labor, so the domain will pursue grants and other outside funding to pay the teams. AmeriCorps and grants tied to AI, technology, and workforce development are the candidate sources, and the domain will write toward them so the funding is in hand before a team is stood up. No fix goes to a student team until the students building it are paid.

Why it is worth it for students

This is a paid, resume-building, experiential-learning opportunity, not unpaid class work. A student on one of these teams builds a real tool for a real user with a real deliverable, and leaves with something rare in a portfolio: a shipped tool, a real user, a real constraint, and an evidence base behind it. It is the same through-line the study holds for students everywhere, tools and experience that outlast the semester.

Pilots and rollout

Prove each fix in staged pilot rounds before any wider rollout.

A built tool does not go straight to every student. Each fix runs in staged pilots, is measured against the success measures, revised, and only then rolled out more widely. The domain recommends the district put a formal piloting process in place first, a defined intake, review, secure sandbox, and path to production, so no tool is ever piloted with students without one. The domain has drafted such a framework as a starting point, and the pilot rounds are gated on it being in place.

Every pilot has the same shape before it starts: a defined intake, a review, a secure sandbox separate from live student systems, a named success measure, and a path to production if it clears. Pilots are co-designed with the front-line staff whose work the fix touches, not run at them, and a fix can be something the district builds, adopts from another college, or configures inside a platform it already licenses. Whichever it is, it collects no student data and keeps a person in the loop.

The rounds are staged so each one tests more than the last. Round 1 in spring 2027 puts the first built tools in front of a limited group and measures them against need met, ease, time to resolution, and persistence. Round 2 in fall 2027 widens the group and folds in what round 1 revised. Round 3 in January 2028 runs alongside the start of production, so tools that have cleared two rounds move into monitored production with a named owner while newer fixes keep piloting.

Every fix is judged by what happened for the student, measured four ways:

  • Need met. Did the student actually reach the right support and resolve what they came for.
  • Ease and satisfaction. How easy and satisfying it felt, captured with the Single Ease Question right after the task.
  • Time to resolution. How long it took to get from the felt need to a resolved outcome.
  • Persistence. Whether the student persisted, completed, and transferred over time.

These four are read against a baseline taken before any tool ships. The study first counts how many students reach each service now, by service and by college, then measures the lift later: more students reaching support, and more of them persisting. Help is counted entry by entry across every channel, so a question answered immediately by a tool counts as help delivered even when it never becomes an office visit. A service that gets easier to reach can show fewer visits and more students helped at the same time.

Office usage is not the measure. A busy office is not a working journey, and a quiet office is not a failing one. Automation is aimed at routine, repetitive work, so staff are freed for the students who need a person. No one is replaced.

Barriers roll up two ways so the district can act on them. Each is severity-rated 0 to 4 on the Nielsen scale by more than one rater and reported as their mean, then ranked by reach, how many students it touches, so the biggest and most common barriers rise to the top. Findings are placed on a journey map by stage, from choosing a college through to a job, so a dean can see exactly where students are lost and whether a fix moved the number.

Counts are the plan: the full synthetic matrix is 50 personas times 50 tasks times 10 colleges, 25,000 synthetic runs, plus 80 in-person walk-ins across 8 offices at 10 colleges and a validation sample against real students. Zero are logged and fieldwork has not started.
