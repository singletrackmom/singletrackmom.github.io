Student Journey Gap Analysis

AI Resource Center · Student Support and Success

A ten-college study across the Maricopa district that builds demographic-grounded synthetic students, walks the real student journey one task at a time, and finds where students hit barriers, so support staff can lift routine work off their desks without anyone being replaced.

Status: planning and instrument-building. Fieldwork has not started and no runs have been logged, so nothing here reports results. The design is described, the counts are the plan, and nothing is in production.

Goal

Map where students in the Maricopa district lose their way reaching advising, financial aid, basic needs, and other support, and rank each gap by how many students it touches, so the domain can decide where an AI tool, a shared staff workflow, or a person is the right answer.

Audience

The Student Support and Success domain of the district AI Resource Center, a group made up of advisors, financial-aid, and other support staff across all ten colleges, plus the college leaders and faculty who help run the study and recruit the student build teams.

Process

Build a fixed set of demographic-grounded synthetic students, write scenarios that put each one against a real task at a real college, log what breaks and how badly, and turn the highest-reach gaps into human-in-the-loop pilots that collect no student data. Success is measured by whether the student’s need was met, how easy and satisfying it felt, how fast it happened, and whether the student persisted, never by how much an office gets used.

The synthetic students

A synthetic student mirrors who actually enrolls, and it is built the way a reliable test instrument has to be built.

The study builds AI synthetic students from a fixed set of 36 personas, and those personas are proportioned to the real demographics of the Maricopa district and Glendale Community College student body. Race and ethnicity, first-generation status, Pell eligibility, food insecurity, veteran status, parenting, disability, Spanish-first speakers, and international students all appear in roughly the proportions the district actually enrolls. Because the mix mirrors the real student body, the barriers the study surfaces are the barriers real students hit, weighted by how common each kind of student is, rather than a handful of convenient stand-ins.

One model told to be a whole student at once, Spanish-first and first-generation and phone-only and in a hard week, does all of it poorly and answers differently every run, which makes it useless as a test. So each persona is worked as a set of single-purpose lenses, and each lens does one job well. A language lens reads a page as a Spanish-first student. A first-generation lens flags where a process assumes college knowledge no one explained. A device lens checks whether the task even works on a phone with no laptop. A load lens reads for the weight a student in a hard moment is carrying. A coordinator reads each page or task and runs only the lenses that page needs, so a financial-aid form draws the language and first-generation lenses while a club page draws the belonging lens. Every flag traces back to one lens, one page, one college, with a severity score, so the findings are inspectable and comparable across colleges and across two versions of the same site.

Some failures never show up on a single page. They live in the order of the journey. A student cannot pay a bill before enrolling, and a service is useless if it sits where a new student would never look. So the synthetic students also test the journey itself against the service map, checking that the sequence and placement of services match the path a real student actually takes, not just whether each page reads well on its own.

The method is built to be defensible. The synthetic students touch only public pages, never a logged-in system, so there is no student data and no privacy exposure. They cover all ten colleges at a scale no team of people could reach. And they decide nothing: they surface located problems and hand them to staff, who choose what to fix. The point is to move the feedback loop earlier, to find the barrier before a student is lost to it.

This is early, and stated plainly. The 36 personas and the 530-scenario bank are built and the service map is drafted. The single-lens decomposition and the coordinator are the method being built and validated now. The honest hard problem is reliability: getting a synthetic student to fail the way a real student would, consistently, run after run, and tuning the flags so staff get the few that matter instead of noise.

How the study works

A structured, repeatable walk of the journey on both sides of the desk.

  • Give a synthetic student a persona, a college, and one felt need, never an office, because a student is never told where to go.
  • Walk the live systems from that need and log every barrier, scored for severity, so gaps can be compared across the ten colleges.
  • Run online findability scenarios and in-person walk-in scenarios in parallel, and map what each college calls the same service so no college is asked to rename anything.
  • Rank the gaps by severity against how many students each one reaches, then decide per gap whether a shared workflow, a built or adopted tool, or a person is the fix.

Alongside the persona runs, the study asks student services staff to surface the workflows they have already streamlined. A practice that works at one college is the cheapest win available, and it asks nobody to buy anything. This is a support-staff domain, so automation is aimed at the routine, repetitive work: the same factual question answered for the four hundredth time, the “make an appointment and we will get back to you” reply, freeing staff for the students who need a person. No one is replaced.

What is next

Finish the instruments, run the fieldwork, then decide.

  • Finish the run-log form and verify the service crosswalk, then dry-run the protocol at one college.
  • Run the scenarios across the ten colleges and gather the workflows staff have already streamlined.
  • Assemble paid, multidisciplinary student teams around November to build the resulting tools by January 2027, so students leave with a shipped tool for a real user.
  • Defer flows that depend on the district Salesforce advising platform to a later wave, run during or after that platform’s own rollout.
  • Screen every option against the no-student-data line, favoring data-sovereign tools we build over data-collecting ones we buy.
