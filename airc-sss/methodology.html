1. The problem this study addresses

Across the ten Maricopa colleges, the support that would keep students enrolled already exists on every campus. The failure is findability. Students cannot reach advising, financial aid, basic needs, accommodations, or the right person at the moment they need them.

A study with three personas walking thirty journeys can describe that problem. It cannot map it at the resolution the committee needs in order to prioritize where AI should close the gaps. This document specifies a structured, repeatable instrument designed to produce a college-by-college, stage-by-stage map of where students hit barriers, with a severity judgment attached to each one.

3. Two methods, run together

The study runs two distinct evaluations, because they surface different defects and neither substitutes for the other.

  1. Expert heuristic evaluation. Evaluators inspect each college’s student-facing interfaces against Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics, working through the interface systematically and recording each violation with its location and a severity rating. This is an inspection method. No student and no persona is involved.
  2. Task-based usability testing. A tester, human or AI, adopts a persona and walks a real journey on the live systems, attempting one task from a felt need and narrating as they go.

Both are needed because a heuristic evaluation catches violations a tester will silently work around without ever reporting them, while task testing exposes failures no heuristic predicts, such as a service that exists but is named something the persona would never search for.

4. The persona-per-test model

The unit of the task-based study is a single run: one persona, at one campus, attempting one task. The tester opens the instrument and receives a brief stating who the persona is, what barriers the persona carries, the one thing the persona is trying to do, and the campus. The tester then attempts the task from a real felt need, starting where a student would start, usually a search on the college website.

4.1 The protocol

Runs use a concurrent think-aloud protocol. The tester narrates while working, saying what they are looking for, what they expect a label to mean, and why they are clicking what they click. The narration is the data. It is what turns a wrong turn into an explanation of the wrong turn.

The tester is given a goal and never a destination, and the facilitator does not prompt toward a service, name an office, or confirm that a page is the right page. Telling a tester where to go destroys the finding the run exists to make, because findability is precisely what is under test. The facilitator intervenes only to prevent a genuine dead stop, and each intervention is recorded as an assist.

4.2 Why a single task per run

A single task isolates one barrier, so a failure is attributable to a specific point in the journey. It also keeps runs short, parallelizable, and easy to assign to many testers at once, which is what coverage requires.

4.3 Why a persona library rather than a few personas

A page that works for a digitally fluent transfer student can still fail a phone-first, Spanish-dominant parent on a slow connection. Only a varied library surfaces those intersections. The 36 personas reflect who actually enrolls: ESL and non-native speakers, veterans, autistic and ADHD students, food-insecure and first-generation students, working parents, returning adults, students who need Disability Resources, swirling multi-campus students, and dual-enrollment high schoolers.

5. What every run records

Each task run produces one record, logged immediately after the task while it is fresh. The minimum record is the same for a human tester and for an AI tester, so the two are comparable.

MeasureHow it is captured
Task outcomeSuccess, partial success, or failure. Partial success means the tester reached the service but could not finish the task.
Time on taskMinutes from the start of the attempt to the outcome.
AssistsThe number of times the facilitator had to intervene to prevent a dead stop.
BarrierWhat went wrong, where, and in the tester’s own words.
SeverityThe 0 to 4 rating defined in section 6.
EaseThe Single Ease Question, a 7-point rating of how easy or difficult the task felt, asked immediately after the task.

The path taken, the local service name as the college labels it, whether a human was needed, and any bilingual gap are also recorded, since they are what turn a rating into a fix.

6. Severity

Severity is rated 0 to 4, following Nielsen: 0 not a usability problem, 1 cosmetic, 2 minor, 3 major, 4 catastrophic (a student cannot complete the task and would plausibly give up). A run that finds nothing wrong is a real result and is logged as 0.

ScoreMeaning
0, not a usability problemNothing went wrong. The task worked as it should.
1, cosmeticA small annoyance. The task still gets done.
2, minorNoticeable friction. Extra steps or a wrong turn, but the task gets done.
3, majorSerious difficulty. A human was needed, or the task took far longer than it should.
4, catastrophicA student cannot complete the task and would plausibly give up.

A severity score is not a measure of how irritating a barrier felt. It is a judgment that combines three factors: frequency, meaning how many students would meet this barrier; impact, meaning how hard it is to overcome once met; and persistence, meaning whether a student learns around it once or hits it again every single time. A small irritation that every student meets on every visit can outrank a dramatic failure that almost no one encounters.

6.1 Inter-rater reliability

Severity ratings are subjective, and a single rater’s number is an opinion rather than a measurement. Two raters therefore score each finding independently, without seeing each other’s score, and disagreements are adjudicated in a short review where both state their reasoning and a final score is agreed. Agreement is tracked, and the definitions above are tightened wherever raters keep diverging. This is what allows the committee to compare a severity 3 at one college with a severity 3 at another and treat them as the same claim.

7. Sample size

The study is formative. Its purpose is to find and fix defects, not to estimate a population parameter, so the sample is sized for defect discovery. A small number of testers per persona segment surfaces the large majority of the issues that are discoverable at all, and each additional tester on the same task returns fewer new defects than the one before. Adding a fifth or a tenth tester to the same task at the same college mostly re-finds what has already been found.

Coverage is therefore scaled on the axes that return new defects, which are personas, tasks, and colleges, and not by piling testers onto the same task. AI testers run the same scenarios in parallel, which extends coverage along those axes rather than deepening a sample that is already saturated. No fixed tester count is promised anywhere in this study, and none is needed for the design to hold.

8. Piloting the protocol

Before any district-wide fieldwork, the whole protocol is dry-run at a single college: the brief, the think-aloud, the capture form, the severity definitions, and the two-rater step. The dry run is a test of the instrument, not of the college. It answers whether the tasks are legible to a tester who is not the author, whether the fields capture what the analysis needs, and whether two raters can agree. Any wording that failed is repaired before the other nine colleges are touched.

9. Analysis

Findings are analyzed in three passes.

  1. Affinity mapping. Every logged barrier is written up as an observation and grouped with related observations until themes emerge from the data rather than from the categories the study started with. A theme is a pattern that holds across personas and colleges.
  2. Severity by reach. Each theme is placed on a matrix of severity against reach, where reach is how many of the roughly 140,000 students would plausibly meet it. High severity and high reach is where the first pilots go. High severity and low reach is documented and routed, not automated first.
  3. Journey mapping and service blueprinting. Themes are placed on the student journey to show where in the arc from application to completion a student is lost, and the worst are blueprinted, which means drawing the frontstage the student sees against the backstage staff actions that produce it. The blueprint is what makes the automation argument concrete, because the routine, repetitive backstage steps are exactly what an AI can absorb so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours on the students with real problems. No one is replaced.

10. The scenario bank and the path to 5,000 runs

A taxonomy of 46 base tasks covers the whole journey: apply and admissions, MEID, residency, orientation, self-enrollment, and the student ID, then financial aid, scholarships, the bookstore, adding and dropping classes and the aid impact, transcripts, tutoring, Disability Resources, counseling, basic needs, campus tech, veterans benefits, transfer, swirling, and graduation offboarding.

Each base task is run by several personas, because different barriers reveal different failures. That multiplication lifts the bank toward roughly 500 distinct persona and task scenarios. Each scenario is then attempted at each of the ten colleges, where the local service name and the page layout differ. Five hundred scenarios across ten colleges is about 5,000 task-runs.

The bank is the data backbone. It is delivered as a spreadsheet with one row per scenario and fields for status, tester, date, result, and severity. Those fields are empty until runs begin.

10.1 Human and AI testers

Every district employee holds a Maricopa account, but no individual has access to the student-facing systems of all ten colleges. Access is scoped to a person’s own college. The members of the Student Support and Success domain are drawn from all ten colleges, so the domain membership constitutes the instrument: each member can test the campus whose systems that member can actually reach, which is the only practical route to coverage across all ten.

Volunteers take a single scenario in a single sitting, roughly fifteen minutes: a member opens the form, takes one assigned scenario, attempts it, and records what happened. The low burden is a deliberate response to volunteer capacity. No fixed tester count is set, and the design does not depend on one.

AI testers run the same scenarios in parallel to extend coverage beyond what volunteers can carry. AI testers walk the public, no-login paths at breadth across the ten colleges and produce a first map quickly. Volunteers cover the login-walled flows that require an authenticated account at their own college, and they validate what the AI surfaced. Every AI severity rating is treated as preliminary until a human rater confirms it, which is also the first of the two independent ratings described in section 6.1.

Student testers from the design studio are a possible addition in the fall. Nothing is arranged, and no plan on this site counts them as capacity.

10.2 Scenarios that cannot run yet

The district advising tool (Salesforce) is not yet accessible to the domain. Scenarios that depend on it (find or get assigned an advisor, book an advising appointment, see the aid impact at the moment of dropping a class, and confirm consortium aid at a second college) are deferred to a second wave, to be run during or after that tool’s pilot. They are flagged in the bank and excluded from the runs that can start now. This is a stated constraint on completeness, not an omission.

11. Operations, tracking, and coverage

Coordination has to deliver one scenario per tester, no duplication, every result logged, and a clear view of what remains. The approach layers three pieces that already mostly exist.

  1. The bank is the single source of truth for assignment. Each scenario carries an ID. A college representative assigns one scenario to one tester by entering initials and a date on that row, so nothing is run twice and coverage is visible in the sheet itself.
  2. The existing Jotform capture form records a run: persona, college, task, path taken, outcome, time on task, assists, the barrier, severity, and the Single Ease Question score. It captures results well but does not assign work, so it stays as the capture instrument and its entries key back to the Scenario ID.
  3. The coverage dashboard reads the bank and shows, per college and per journey stage, how much has been walked and where the gaps are. It is how the co-chairs and the steering committee see progress without reading the sheet.

Recommendation: keep the Jotform capture form for recording results, and use the scenario bank as the assignment and coverage layer above it. This avoids building a new assignment system, reuses the live form, and keeps one ID linking a planned scenario to its logged result.

The live Jotform capture form already follows this model.

13. Guardrails

13.1 What the study holds to

  • No PII. Test accounts and initials only. Personas are research instruments, not real people.
  • Translate, do not standardize. Each college keeps its own service names. The study tests findability, not conformity.
  • AI-only scope. Where the fix is automation, it takes routine, repetitive work off advising and financial aid staff so their time goes to the students who need a person. No one is replaced.
  • Human contact is the metric of success.

13.2 What the study routes elsewhere

  • Process and policy barriers go to the owner who can fix them. They are documented here, not solved here.
  • Classroom, curriculum, and faculty-tool issues go to Domain 2, Teaching and Learning.
  • Data-governance and security questions, including the vendor advising-tool data concern, go to Domain 1.
  • Retention and institutional-research data needs are coordinated with Domain 3.

14. What is needed to proceed

14.1 Coverage across ten colleges

Access to student-facing systems is scoped to the employee’s own college, so no individual can reach all ten. The Student Support and Success domain draws its members from all ten colleges, and that membership is the instrument: each member runs scenarios on the campus whose systems that member can already reach, using the Maricopa account that member already holds. Student testers use their own real accounts. Coverage is therefore a function of volunteer participation across the ten colleges, not of provisioning.

14.2 Real-student persona validation

The 36 personas are designed from known demographics and the GCC frustrations data. They should be validated against real students before fieldwork. Two possible routes are the design-studio students and the Student AI Group. Neither is arranged, so persona validation is an open item and not scheduled work.

14.3 The advising-tool wave

Advising assignment and booking, the aid impact shown at the moment of dropping a class, and consortium-aid confirmation cannot be tested until the district advising tool is accessible to the domain. These scenarios are flagged in the bank and held for a second wave, to be run during or after that tool’s pilot. The data-governance concern around that tool belongs to Domain 1.

14.4 Tester coverage

Coverage rests on the domain membership itself. Because access is scoped to a person’s own college and the domain draws its members from all ten, each member is the route to the one campus that member can reach. Volunteers take one scenario in one sitting, and coverage is built from however many of those arrive. The design scales by adding personas, tasks, and colleges rather than by stacking testers on one task, so a small volunteer pool does not invalidate it. AI testers run the same scenarios in parallel and carry breadth. One college representative per campus, to assign scenarios and confirm coverage, is the lightest workable structure.
