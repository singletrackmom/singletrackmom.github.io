1. The strategic case

Decades of evidence, from the classic theories of student involvement and student departure through contemporary national research, converge on a well-replicated finding: students who feel connected to their institution and supported by its services persist at materially higher rates. For the populations these colleges serve, the support a student needs largely already exists. What is missing is the connection to it. Closing that gap is among the highest-return investments available in completion, and faculty, who are the first to observe disengagement and the most trusted source of referral, are central to it.

2. Understanding our students

Attrition is concentrated among the very populations these colleges were established to serve. The pressures below are not edge cases. They are the modal experience of the community college student, and they are heightened at a Hispanic-Serving Institution, which nine of the ten Maricopa colleges are.

PressureThe external load students carry into our courses
Basic needs59 percent of students face food or housing insecurity. Among students who stop out or are at risk, 79 percent cite basic needs and financial pressure rather than academics.
EmploymentRoughly 80 percent of community college students are employed and 39 percent work full time. Most who begin at a two-year college work 20 or more hours weekly. Enrollment is fitted into the margins of a working life.
CaregivingMore than one in five students are parents, and many more support siblings, aging parents, or ill relatives. In the 2024 national community college survey, 71 percent of caregiving students identified caregiving as a withdrawal risk. Extended-family obligation is especially common across Maricopa.
Wellbeing35 percent of students have considered leaving in the past six months. The leading reasons are emotional stress (54 percent) and mental health (43 percent), ahead of cost (31 percent). Disengagement more often signals exceeded capacity than low commitment.

3. The online dimension

The students balancing the most, meaning work, caregiving, and distance, disproportionately enroll in online sections for their flexibility. Yet online courses have long completed at lower rates, and the disparity is widest for the students already most at risk. The driver is not the modality itself. It is isolation and the absence of structure and support, precisely the conditions connection is designed to remedy. Intentional connection is therefore most consequential exactly where it is hardest to establish.

4. Institutional evidence

The national pattern is confirmed by local data. Faculty and staff documented the barriers they observe students encounter, and the largest categories are not instructional. They are access, communication, and navigation to the appropriate service.

The conclusion is unambiguous. The support exists, and students cannot reliably locate, reach, or afford to use it. The binding constraint is connection, not capacity.

5. The drivers of attrition

When students leave, the reasons are seldom academic.

Material insecurity, 79 percentOf students who stop out, 79 percent cite basic needs and finances rather than the difficulty of the material.
The awareness gapOnly 55 percent of students say their institution communicates support effectively. The gap between leaving and staying is frequently a gap in awareness, not availability.
IsolationAbsent deliberate connection, students disengage quietly. The most at-risk students are the least likely to initiate contact with the institution.

6. Mapping barriers to existing support

A student in difficulty frequently does not know what to request, or hesitates to ask. A single, specific referral closes that gap.

BarrierThe service that already answers it
Cost of course materialsBasic Needs book vouchers, library course reserves, and open educational resources address this directly. No student should fail for inability to purchase a text.
Childcare and dependent careChildcare assistance and family resources exist. For the substantial share of students who are parents or caregivers, this is often determinative of persistence.
Food and housing insecurityThe food pantry, emergency funds, and campus social workers are designed for precisely this. Basic Needs programs are a significant and significantly underrecognized institutional asset.
Inability to attend in personAn alternate means of submission or participation should precede any administrative drop. A make-up, a flexible deadline, or a remote option preserves enrollment.

7. An underutilized support infrastructure

The institution operates more than twenty student-facing services. Most students, and many faculty, cannot name a fraction of what is available to them at no cost. Awareness, not availability, is the limiting factor.

  • Advising, counseling, financial aid, and scholarships
  • Basic Needs, the food pantry, emergency funds, book vouchers, childcare assistance, housing support, and campus social workers
  • Tutoring, Math Solutions, the Writing Center, and library research support
  • Disability Resources, ESL support, and health and wellness
  • Career services, veterans services, the transfer center, and Peer Success Coaches

A point worth underscoring: Disability Resources serves students with ADHD, anxiety, and other non-apparent conditions, not only visible disabilities. A student who reports difficulty concentrating or completing timed assessments may qualify for accommodations. Awareness of five or more services is associated with a 13-point increase in persistence, yet only 55 percent of students report that support is communicated effectively. Faculty are the bridge across that gap.

8. Reconsidering punitive attrition

Administrative drops for early absence remove students at their most vulnerable point and frequently manufacture the non-completion they are intended to forestall. An early absence is a leading indicator of a student in difficulty, working an additional shift or managing a family emergency, rather than a measure of commitment. The evidence is consistent: proactive outreach and flexible pathways retain students, and removal does not. Where an attendance or drop policy affords no accommodation for a student in crisis, that policy operates against completion.

ResponseConsequence
RemovalEnds the relationship at the point of greatest need and forecloses recovery. The student departs before anyone has established why.
ReconnectionA brief outreach and an alternate pathway, whether a make-up, a flexible deadline, or a referral, maintains enrollment and preserves the opportunity to recover.

10. The faculty role

Faculty are the first to observe disengagement and the most trusted source of referral. Four practices, each a matter of seconds, materially shift outcomes.

  1. Reach out early. A brief check-in at the first sign of disengagement is frequently the single intervention that re-engages a student.
  2. Normalize help-seeking. Position services as ordinary practice rather than remediation. Stigma is a primary barrier to utilization.
  3. Refer with precision. One service, one named contact. Precision outperforms a catalogue of options.
  4. Offer a path, not an exit. An alternate means to submit or participate should precede any administrative drop.

11. The leverage of a single connection

Across more than thirty thousand graduates, the Gallup-Purdue Index found that a mentor who encouraged a student’s goals more than doubled their odds of being engaged at work and thriving in life, and that a professor who cared about them as a person made them 1.9 times more likely to be engaged. Yet only about two in ten graduates report having had such a mentor. That person is not assigned. A professor, an advisor, a coach, a club sponsor, a social worker, anyone who believes in a student can be the connection that keeps them enrolled. No student should leave for want of one.
