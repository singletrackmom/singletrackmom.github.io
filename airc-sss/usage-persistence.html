This page reports published research and one internal survey. It reports no results from the student journey study, which has not been run.

1. The premise

Students who feel connected to their institution and supported by its services persist to completion at higher rates. This is among the most replicated findings in higher education research. Astin’s theory of involvement and Tinto’s student departure model established it in the 1970s and 1980s, and decades of subsequent work have sharpened rather than overturned it.

The premise tells the domain what to measure. If connection and support are what keep students enrolled, then the quantity worth baselining is how well students are actually reaching support right now.

2. Why students do not persist

Asked directly, students name pressures that are rarely academic. They are the pressures of adult life meeting an institution that assumes a student will come and find it, and the community college population carries them more heavily than any other group in higher education. Four pressures converge.

2.1 Wellbeing and stress

In the 2024 Lumina and Gallup study, 35 percent of students had considered leaving in the previous six months. Emotional stress led the reasons at 54 percent, mental health followed at 43 percent, and cost at 31 percent. The reasons students give describe wellbeing rather than academic performance.

2.2 Basic needs

The Hope Center survey of 74,350 students in 2023 to 2024 found 59 percent facing food or housing insecurity. Among students who stopped out or were at risk of stopping out, 79 percent cited basic needs and financial challenges. This is the majority experience, not an edge case.

2.3 Work

About 80 percent of community college students work and 39 percent work full time. Nearly two-thirds of students who start at a two-year college work twenty or more hours a week. Enrollment is fitted into the margins of a working life.

2.4 Caregiving

More than one in five community college students are parents. In the 2024 Community College Survey of Student Engagement, 71 percent of caregiving students said caregiving could cause them to drop out. Student parents complete at 18 percent within six years, against 54 percent for traditional students.

2.5 Why the help goes unused

The services that would relieve these pressures exist on every campus. A student working two jobs and caring for a parent does not have time to work out which office to call, and often does not recognize a personal situation as the thing a given service was built for. Only 55 percent of students say their institution communicates support well, and 18 percent actively disagree. The gap between stopping out and staying enrolled is frequently a gap in awareness and connection rather than in service availability.

3. A local signal consistent with the national pattern

The case above rests on large multi-year national studies. The evidence that follows is much smaller, a single internal survey at Glendale Community College in 2025, and it is weighted accordingly. It is not the basis of the work. It is a local check against the national pattern, and it is consistent with it.

Faculty and staff documented 83 barriers they watch students hit, rating them 4.5 out of 5 on average for both frustration and impact. The largest categories were not classroom problems. Sorted by what the barrier is about: student access (21), communication (19), reaching a service (19), and campus technology such as printing, wireless access, login, and lab availability (14), with comfort needs, textbooks, and printing close behind. Sorted by which department owns the fix, the most-named were information technology, advisement, and basic needs.

The basic-needs pressure is documented locally as well. Glendale Community College Student Services reports that 44 percent of its students are food insecure. One survey does not describe a district of ten colleges, but it points in the same direction as the national data: the services exist, and students cannot reliably find them, reach them, or afford to use them. That is the gap the usability study is built to locate stop by stop. The full survey, with all 83 barriers, is at the survey page.

4. Connecting the evidence to the intervention

The intervention works where the connection is made. Among caregiving students, those who persisted were considerably more likely than those who stopped out to report that their college had provided financial, basic-needs, and childcare support. Locally, the Glendale Community College Peer Success Coach program found that students matched with a coach retained at higher rates than those who were not, which is local evidence that connection to a person keeps students enrolled.

The pieces of the domain’s work connect in sequence. The inventory maps where the services are and counts how many students reach them now, which is the baseline. The usability study locates where students fail to reach them. This evidence page establishes why that failure matters. The recommendations then rank fixes by how many students each would touch. Automation answers routine factual questions and books the routine appointment so that advising and financial aid staff spend their hours with the students who have a real problem. No one is replaced.
