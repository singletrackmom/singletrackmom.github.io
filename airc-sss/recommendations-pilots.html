How a documented barrier becomes a candidate pilot: the ranking rule, the models the design borrows from, and what a pilot has to look like before it is proposed.

No pilot has been ranked, selected, approved, or built, because the study that would rank them has not been run. This page defines the decision rule the committee will apply once findings exist, and the directions the earlier local evidence already points toward. Everything below is a method and a hypothesis, not a result.

Reach decides what gets piloted

The first question asked of every candidate is how many students it would touch. A sophisticated tool that reaches a few hundred students counts for less than a simple one that reaches tens of thousands, so the highest-reach fixes are piloted first. The rule is stated explicitly because the alternative default in an AI initiative is to build the most interesting tool.

Ranking follows the analysis described in the methodology. Logged barriers are affinity-mapped into themes first, so the committee prioritizes patterns rather than one-off incidents. Each theme is then placed on a severity-by-reach matrix, and pilots are taken from the top of that matrix downward. The result is a defensible order rather than a guess, with a baseline number to measure each pilot against.

Ranking factorSourceWhy it counts
Student reachThe service inventory and baseline (students served against students eligible)The largest filter. Fixes that touch the most students go first.
Barrier severityThe usability study, on the Nielsen 0 to 4 scaleFrequency, impact, and persistence, combined into one rating by two independent raters and adjudicated.
Equity weightThe persistence evidenceAdditional weight where a barrier falls hardest on the students most at risk of leaving.

The domain does not hold that every interaction requires a human. It holds that a tool must return staff time and protect human connection where it matters, and must not displace the relationships that keep students enrolled.

The models the design borrows from

The claim that connection-focused, AI-assisted support can move persistence is not an assumption of this study. Other institutions have demonstrated it under evaluation, and those results set the standard any pilot here is measured against. The pattern is consistent across them: technology surfaces the right student at the right moment, and a person follows through. None of the results below belong to this study.

Georgia State University, text-based outreach

An AI chatbot answered enrollment, financial aid, and deadline questions by text at any hour. Summer melt fell by 22 percent, withdrawals from unpaid balances fell by half, and persistence rose by roughly 3 percent, representing about 1,300 additional students enrolled. First-generation and Pell-eligible students used it most, which is the population community colleges most need to reach. It is the most rigorously evaluated example in the literature, tested through multiple randomized controlled trials. See source 1.

City University of New York, coordinated wraparound support

The Accelerated Study in Associate Programs model addresses the common barriers at once, advising, money, transit, tutoring, and career planning, rather than leaving students to assemble supports alone. Three-year graduation reached 53 percent against 24.6 percent for a matched comparison group, and the model has been replicated at more than 60 institutions with the same result. The lesson for this work is that students struggle under an accumulation of barriers, so coordinated connection beats optimizing any single service in isolation. See source 2.

Valencia College, connected advising

An Aspen Prize winner that links faculty, staff, courses, technology, and services into one developmental advising system following students from entry through graduation. The match between students’ education plans and their actual course-taking rose from 43 percent to 60 percent. It is the conceptual ancestor of the connected student-support model this domain is working toward. See source 3.

Caring Campus, connection as a staff practice

A behavioral model rather than a technology program, spreading specific student-connecting behaviors among faculty and staff. It is implemented at more than 120 colleges, with positive retention findings reported by the Community College Research Center. It is the human infrastructure that makes AI-assisted outreach meaningful, because a tool can surface a student who needs help but a person has to follow through. See source 4.

Glendale Community College, peer success coaches

Local evidence for the same claim. Students matched with a peer coach retained at higher rates than those who were not, and the program was a 2023 to 2024 district Innovation of the Year finalist. It raises the question this initiative exists to answer: if connection to a person keeps students enrolled, what limits the scale of that connection? Where the limit is the routine load on a coordinator’s day, automation can let one coordinator support more students. See source 5.

What a pilot has to look like

Automation takes the routine, people take the rest

A factual question (where a class meets, when the aid deadline falls, what a balance is) can be answered instantly by a tool. That removes repetitive work from advising and financial aid staff so their hours go to the students whose situations require a person. The measure of success is more staff time where it counts, not a person in every interaction. No one is replaced.

Co-designed with the people who named the barrier

Pilots are built with the staff and students who flagged the need, not handed to them. Training comes before deployment, because staff have consistently ranked training as their first AI priority.

Adopted through invitation, not mandate

A friendly, peer-to-peer adoption effort, distributed at moments where people already gather, so an AI tool arrives as something staff and students helped shape. A small, low-pressure object or character, on the model of the stress ball that eased people into accessibility work they had been resisting, lowers anxiety and signals that trying the tool is safe and optional. This is a proposed approach, not a completed campaign.

Measured against a baseline

Each pilot carries a starting number from the service inventory and a target. A year in, the committee can say whether more students reached the service and whether they persisted, rather than only whether the tool shipped.

Build, adopt, or configure

A pilot does not have to be a new application. For each top-ranked barrier the committee takes the lightest option that works: adopt a tool that already exists, configure something already licensed (a plug-in or a feature inside a platform already running), or build an agent or workflow into one of the audited systems. The structure is the same across all three: automation surfaces the right student or the right resource, and a person follows through. The sequence is set by reach, beginning in the systems the most students already use.

CopaMigo, a bilingual routing prototype built at Glendale Community College, is one illustration of the build option and is an early prototype in testing, not a district pilot. An existing vendor tool or a configured workflow inside a licensed platform could solve the same barrier equally well, and any option that would collect student data is screened out before it is considered.

See the prototype at CopaMigo.

Two directions the prior evidence already points to

The ranked list can only come from the study, and the study has not been run. Two high-reach directions are nonetheless already indicated by the earlier local survey and by the persistence research, and both are stated here as hypotheses for the committee to test rather than as decisions.

Put help where the student already is

Students rarely stop out through a failure to try. They disengage and withdraw, often while working two jobs and caring for family, and frequently without knowing what to ask for. The indicated fix is not an additional website. It is the right resource appearing where the student already is: in the learning management system, at enrollment, in the first-year experience, inside the financial aid flow, and in a chat channel. The more places the help appears, the less a student has to know the name of the office that provides it. Plain-language help of this kind answers the simple questions and routes everything else to a person. CopaMigo is one small instance of the approach, not the approach itself.

Equip faculty as the first line of support

Faculty are among the most trusted sources of referral and are usually the first to see a student struggling. Many still drop a student after three absences, which forces non-completion at precisely the moment the student is in crisis, working a double shift, or caring for a sick parent. This is student-support work rather than curriculum work, so it sits inside this domain’s scope. The proposed pilot is a five-minute briefing for first-year faculty and adjunct faculty: who the students actually are and the basic-needs, work, and caregiving pressures they carry; why an absence is a signal to connect rather than to cut; and the few specific things a faculty member can do in thirty seconds to point a student toward help. It follows the Caring Campus model, behavioral rather than mandated, and it is considerate of faculty who already carry a heavy load.

The five-minute faculty briefing is a candidate deliverable, not an approved one. It could be produced as a short slide deck for first-year experience and adjunct onboarding once the committee agrees to it.

Other candidates the evidence suggests

Three further directions are plausible and remain hypotheses until the ranked list either confirms or drops them. A plain-language intake and routing tool, the ambient version of the routing prototype, would touch nearly every student and speaks directly to the awareness gap. Proactive, channel-flexible outreach where students already are, by text or chat rather than by email, would reach the disengaged students least likely to come find the institution. And surfacing AI tools the district already licenses but underuses is a near-zero-cost option that the tool inventory is expected to locate.

Known sources of resistance

Three sources of resistance are documented here, each with the design decision that answers it.

Staff concern about being replaced

Willingness to learn AI is low where employees suspect the underlying goal is to eliminate their jobs. The design answers this directly. Every pilot keeps people in control and exists to remove routine work so that staff spend more time with students, not so that there are fewer staff. Training precedes any tool, pilots are co-designed with the front line, and adoption is invitational rather than mandatory.

Students who have been told AI is cheating

Many students have been told by faculty that using AI is cheating, and avoid it entirely. The tools contemplated here connect students to people and services and do not perform coursework, which places them outside the academic-integrity concern. The domain also works with the student AI group so that students learn what responsible, permitted use involves.

The environmental cost of the compute

The energy and water footprint of AI is substantial and it matters to this community. The design prefers tools already licensed, right-sized and efficient models over new heavy compute, and the lightest workflow that resolves the barrier. Not every fix requires a large model.
