Recommendations & pilots
Turn barriers into help, ranked by reach

This is the deliverable the first three initiatives build toward. Every documented barrier becomes a candidate AI fix, and before we pilot anything, we rank candidates by one question first: how many students would this actually reach? A brilliant tool that touches 200 students matters less than a plain one that reaches 20,000. Every pilot keeps a human at the other end, and is co-designed with the staff and students who flagged the need.

Reach comes first How we decide what to pilot

The first question for every pilot: how many students will this touch?
Reach is the top-ranked criterion, ahead of everything else. The highest-reach fixes get piloted first.

The temptation in any AI initiative is to build the most interesting tool. We resist it on purpose. The ranking starts with reach: how many students each fix would actually touch, pulled from the Initiative 3 baseline. Then each barrier from Initiative 2 carries a severity, and we weight toward the populations the data says are most at risk of leaving. We pilot from the top of that list down. The result is a defensible order, not a hunch, and a clear before-number to measure each pilot against.

Why we are confident it works Proven models, not speculation

We are not guessing that connection-focused, AI-assisted support moves persistence. Institutions have already shown it, with rigorous evaluation. These models set the bar Initiative 4’s pilots aim at, and the pattern across all of them is the same: technology that surfaces the right student at the right time, paired with a human who follows through.

Georgia State University · Pounce (AI text outreach)

Summer melt down 22%. Withdrawals from unpaid balances down 50%. Persistence up ~3%, about 1,300 more students enrolled.

An AI chatbot answered enrollment, financial-aid, and deadline questions by text, any hour, without judgment. First-generation and Pell-eligible students used it most, exactly the populations community colleges most want to reach. The most rigorously evaluated example in the literature, through multiple randomized controlled trials. [1]

CUNY ASAP · coordinated wraparound support

Three-year graduation 53% vs 24.6% for a matched comparison group, more than double.

ASAP addresses every common barrier at once, advising, money, transit, tutoring, career planning, rather than leaving students to navigate supports alone. Replicated at 60+ institutions with the same result. The lesson for Initiative 4: students struggle with an accumulation of barriers, so coordinated connection beats optimizing any single service in isolation. [2]

Valencia College · LifeMap connected advising

Match between students’ education plans and actual course-taking rose from 43% to 60%.

An Aspen Prize winner that links faculty, staff, courses, technology, and services into one developmental advising system following students from entry to graduation. The conceptual ancestor of the connected student-support vision Domain 5 is working toward. [3]

Caring Campus · connection as staff practice

120+ colleges implementing; positive retention findings from Columbia’s CCRC.

Not a technology program at all, a behavioral model that spreads specific student-connecting behaviors among faculty and staff. It is the human infrastructure that makes AI-driven outreach meaningful: a tool can surface a student who needs help, but a person has to follow through. Initiative 4 pairs AI identification with this kind of human response. [4]

GCC · Peer Success Coaches (our own evidence)

Students matched with a coach retained at higher rates than those who were not.

We do not have to look outside Maricopa. GCC’s own program, a 2023–24 Maricopa Innovation of the Year finalist, proved locally what the national research shows: connection to a person keeps students enrolled. The open question it raises is the Initiative 4 question, if it works, what limits its scale? If the answer is staff capacity, that is precisely where AI can help a coordinator support more students. [5]

What a pilot looks like Human-in-the-loop, co-designed, measured

Principle

AI connects, humans decide

Every pilot routes a student toward a person or a service, then hands off. The metric is more human contact, not less. If a tool would replace the relationship instead of opening it, we drop it.

Build

Co-designed with the front line

Pilots are built with the staff and students who flagged the need, not handed to them. Training comes before deployment, because staff ranked training as their top AI priority.

Adopt

The “I am AI” campaign

A friendly, peer-to-peer adoption effort distributed at participation moments, so AI tools arrive as something students and staff helped shape, not something imposed on them.

Measure

Against the Initiative 3 baseline

Each pilot has a before-number from Initiative 3 and a target. A year in, we can say whether more students reached the service and whether they persisted, not just whether the tool shipped.

Two directions we already know we want Where the data points hardest

The final ranked list comes from Initiative 2 and Initiative 3, but two high-reach directions are already clear, because the barrier data and the persistence research both point straight at them.

1 · Put the resources everywhere the student already is

Stop making overwhelmed students come find help. Surface it at every touchpoint.

Students do not persist by failing to try; they drown. They are working two jobs, caring for family, and they genuinely do not know what to ask for. The fix is not one more website to visit, it is the right resource showing up where they already are: in Canvas, at enrollment, in First Year Experience, in the financial-aid flow, in a Discord channel. The more places the help appears, the less a student has to know the name of the office that provides it. CopaMigo, my bilingual routing prototype, is a first attempt at this, a student describes their situation in plain language and gets routed to the right human service. It is honestly about 1% of the work. The direction is to make plain-language, route-me-to-a-human help ambient across the whole journey, not a single tool a student has to discover.

See CopaMigo, the 1% prototype →

2 · Equip faculty as the first line of support

Faculty notice first. Many still drop students after three absences. That forces non-completion.

Faculty are one of the most trusted sources of referral, and they are usually the first to see a student slipping. But a student missing class after three absences is often a student in crisis, working a double, or caring for a sick parent, and dropping them is the opposite of what the data says they need. This is student-support work, not curriculum work, so it sits squarely in our lane. The pilot is a five-minute briefing built for first-year faculty and adjuncts: here is who your students actually are, the basic-needs and work and caregiving pressures they carry; here is why an absence is a signal to connect, not to cut; and here are the few specific things you can do in thirty seconds to point a student to help. It is modeled on Caring Campus, a behavioral approach, gentle with faculty who already carry a lot, that gives them ways to help rather than one more mandate.

The five-minute faculty briefing can be produced as a short slide deck for First Year Experience and adjunct onboarding. It is a candidate deliverable for this initiative.

Other high-reach candidates Directional, pending the ranked list

Beyond those two, the data points at a few more places the biggest-reach fixes will likely sit. A plain-language needs-intake-and-routing tool, the ambient version of CopaMigo, would touch nearly every student and directly attacks the awareness gap. Proactive, channel-flexible outreach where students already are, text and Discord rather than email, reaches the disengaged students least likely to come find the institution. And surfacing AI tools the district already licenses but underuses, like BigInterview, is a near-zero-cost win Initiative 1 will help locate. Each is a hypothesis until the ranked list confirms it.
