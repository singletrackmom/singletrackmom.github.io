Leanna Archambault, Ph.D.

Professor. LDT Program Coordinator. Editor of Computers & Education, which ASU’s own profile calls a top-ranked journal in the field. Ph.D. in Instructional and Curricular Studies, UNLV. 9,631 citations, h-index 37, i10-index 79. She made first contact and is likely to lead.

ASU profile · Google Scholar · Her own publication list

Her profile organises her work around three questions: what challenges teachers face online and how universities can better prepare them, how institutions help educators become critical consumers and discerning producers of online instructional resources, and how emerging technologies can improve teaching and learning.

The publications

  • Pillars of Online Pedagogy: A framework for teaching in online learning environments (2022), with Leary and Rice, Educational Psychologist 57(3). 541 citations, her most-cited recent work. Argues effective online teaching rests on a small set of transferable pillars, building relationships and community, active learning, timely and constructive feedback, and designing for learner agency, rather than replicating classroom practice through a screen. The framework most likely to come up, and the program’s common vocabulary.
    Open access, Boise State ScholarWorks · DOI
  • Examining TPACK among K-12 online distance educators in the United States (2009), with Crippen, CITE Journal 9(1). 1,358 citations, her most-cited paper. A national survey found online teachers rate themselves strongest on pedagogy and content and weakest where technology intersects the two, so the technology-facing domains are exactly where online teacher preparation is thinnest.
    Open access
  • Revisiting technological pedagogical content knowledge: Exploring the TPACK framework (2010), with Barnett, Computers & Education 55(4). 1,257 citations. A factor analysis found the seven proposed domains do not separate empirically. Worth knowing: this is critical of a framework she is associated with. She is not a TPACK cheerleader.
    DOI
  • Offering preservice teachers field experiences in K-12 online learning (2012), Kennedy and Archambault, Journal of Teacher Education 63(3). 560 citations. A national census found only about 1.3 percent of teacher-education programs offered any field experience in an online or blended K-12 setting, so teachers were being credentialed for a modality they had never practised in.
    DOI
  • Academic Communities of Engagement (2020), Borup, Graham, West, Archambault and Spring, ETR&D 68(2). 476 citations. A learner’s success online depends on the gap between their independent engagement capacity and what the course demands, and that gap is closed by two support communities, the course community and the learner’s personal community. Student support is a design problem, not only an instructional one.
    DOI, open access
  • K-12 distance educators at work: Who’s teaching online across the United States (2009), with Crippen, JRTE 41(4). The demographic companion to the TPACK study.
    Free full text, ERIC

  • A systematic reexamination of field experiences in PK-12 online environments (2025), Woo, Archambault, Borup, Buss and Henriksen, Computers & Education. The follow-up to the 2012 survey. She has been tracking the same gap for thirteen years. DOI
  • “The artificial and the real: Strategies for establishing social presence through the combination of AI-generated text with instructor and student-centered video recordings” (2025), Open Praxis 17(1). Open access

The angle. Her thirteen-year through-line is that we keep certifying people to teach online without giving them supervised practice online. Pillars is her constructive answer. AI is her current front. Mentioning the Computers & Education editorship is a low-risk compliment that is also substantively true.

Do not use the word “parachute.” It is not her term and it cannot be attributed to any of her publications. Describe the 1.3 percent finding instead and you are on solid ground.

AI mentors, in depth

His definitive statement is not the 2025 news piece. It is the abstract for his February 2026 seminar, “AI Mentors as Instructional Infrastructure: Supporting Teaching and Learning at Scale,” delivered to engineering faculty, which means he is now pitching this beyond his own college. Seminar page · Recording, about an hour

What an AI mentor is, in his words

From that abstract: AI mentors are “intentionally designed, role-based instructional supports that extend teaching presence without replacing faculty judgment or increasing workload.” He asks the audience to consider where they might function as “effective instructional infrastructure rather than standalone tools.”

The seminar takeaways quote him again: “AI tools should be viewed as a secondary layer of instructional presence.”

On what it does for students, from ASU News, 18 March 2026: “It casts the students into an internship role,” with the goal of practising professional communication skills. ASU News

The phrase to echo back to him is “instructional infrastructure rather than standalone tools.” It is his, it is current, and it is not in the 2025 article most people would have read.

How it actually works

  • Built in ASU’s CreateAI Builder, ASU’s in-house toolkit launched 2025, giving faculty access to more than 50 large language models. Students reach faculty-built experiences through faculty sponsorship.
  • Chatbots with personas, embedded in his course. ASU’s college news calls them digital partners “developed to reflect the knowledge base of instructional experts.” Not a Canvas-native feature.
  • Role-based and course-aligned, so per-course rather than per-student. Nothing public describes persistent individual learner profiles.
  • Programmatic, not a one-off. His CV describes integrating AI mentor models into program-level practices including prompts, templates and student-facing AI guidance, aligned with Principled Innovation.
  • Students both use them and study them. His FL-IDN webinar was titled “AI mentors as design partners.” The students are meant to understand the tool as a design object.

The problem he says it solves

  • Scale with heterogeneity: growing enrolments, learners with “diverse prior knowledge, pacing needs, confidence levels, and executive function limitations.”
  • Instructor presence in asynchronous online courses, including supporting students outside faculty working hours.
  • Reducing instructional friction and supporting “the work of learning beyond content,” which is executive-function scaffolding, not content delivery.
  • Without increasing workload. He states that as a constraint, which is notable.
  • Separately he uses AI for course maintenance: reviewing Canvas courses for alignment, clarity, accessibility compliance and consistency.

Guardrails, and the honest read on your data line

His guardrails are pedagogical, not about data. Everything he says himself is about role boundaries: extend teaching presence “without replacing faculty judgment,” a “secondary layer,” supplement instruction rather than replace it, aligned with Principled Innovation.

The data position is ASU’s, not his. CreateAI’s stated policy is that all data stays within ASU’s governed environment, is FERPA-compliant, and is not used to train external AI models.

So: FERPA-compliant containment, not data minimisation. That is meaningfully stronger than consumer ChatGPT, but it is not your line of “no student data and no PII.” A conversational mentor inherently processes student-authored input. Nothing public states his position on PII minimisation, retention or opt-out. That is an open question, not a documented conflict, and it is a fair thing to ask as a design question.

What he has found, including the parts that did not go well

  • Timeline. Programmatic rollout across LDT began Spring 2023, framed as leveraging AI as a design partner. He now says they are integral to how the program teaches learning design.
  • “The Blandening,” 2 February 2026. His most interesting finding and it is a negative one. Grading student work he found: “Collectively, their writing is better. But here’s the problem: it all sounds the same. Not similar; the same.” He argues voice is diagnostic rather than decorative, “a window into how they’re thinking,” and that “a student who outsources their reasoning to AI can produce acceptable work right up until the moment the problem requires actual judgment, and in learning design, that moment comes early and often.” He refuses the ban response: “The laggards will never win by banning the technology, but the innovators move us forward by redesigning around what the technology can do.” Read it
  • The apprenticeship layer is collapsing. Starter deliverables that used to train novices, objectives, storyboards, quiz items, LMS cleanup, are now AI first passes. His conclusion: the differentiator becomes “a learning designer’s ability to supervise AI-enabled production with quality gates, rather than the ability to draft from scratch.” Read it
  • No student outcome data, satisfaction metrics or failure post-mortems on the mentors themselves are public.

The Accordion feedback workflow

Nothing public describes it. It appears in exactly one place, his CV: he supervises and mentors co-instructors and adjunct faculty “including training in the LDT Accordion feedback workflow and evidence-based online teaching practices.” He supervises 10 to 12 co-instructors.

Do not guess at what it is. It is his own coinage, it is undocumented, and it is the thing you would be trained in if hired. Ask him.

Three questions to ask him

  • “Your seminar abstract frames AI mentors as instructional infrastructure rather than standalone tools, and as a secondary layer of instructional presence. What changes in how you design one when you treat it as infrastructure? Is the mentor scoped to the course, or does the role persona travel across the program?” Shows you read the 2026 seminar, not just the 2025 news piece, and it gets at mechanics that are not public.
  • “You built these in CreateAI, where ASU’s line is that data stays in the governed environment and is not used to train external models. Where do you personally draw the line on what a mentor should retain about a student? Is there anything you deliberately designed it not to see or remember?” Your hard line, asked as a design question rather than an ultimatum.
  • “In The Blandening you said you wanted to go next to how to design around the flattening of student voice rather than how to stop it. Do the AI mentors do any of that work? And separately, what is the Accordion feedback workflow? It is the one piece of your practice I could not find anything about.” Proves you read the Substack closely enough to catch an unfulfilled promise, and asks about the thing you would actually be trained in.
