## 5. OER: where the good stuff is, and RLOs (reference)

I should know this cold if I am selling myself as a learning designer. The canonical, reputable OER sources:

- **OER Commons** (oercommons.org). Largest cross-discipline repository, and its Open Author tool lets me build and host my own RLOs.
- **MERLOT** (merlot.org). Peer-reviewed learning objects, strong for STEM and interactive materials. This is literally a "learning objects" repository, the natural home for my sims.
- **OpenStax** (openstax.org). Free, peer-reviewed open textbooks, STEM heavy.
- **LibreTexts** (libretexts.org). Remixable open textbook libraries, STEM heavy.
- **Open Textbook Library**, U of Minnesota (open.umn.edu/opentextbooks). Vetted open textbooks with faculty reviews.
- **MIT OpenCourseWare** (ocw.mit.edu). Full course materials.
- **PhET Interactive Simulations**, CU Boulder (phet.colorado.edu). Free interactive STEM sims. This is my model for publishing sims as RLOs, and it is local (CU Boulder).
- **OASIS** (oasis.geneseo.edu) and the **Mason OER Metafinder** (mason.deming.gmu.edu). Federated search across many OER sources at once, the fastest way to find something for a specific topic.
- **Pressbooks** (pressbooks.com). Author and remix open textbooks.
- **SkillsCommons** (skillscommons.org). Workforce and CTE OER.
- Community-college focused: **CCCOER** and **Open Oregon**.

**Reusable Learning Object (RLO), yes it is a real, established concept.** A self-contained chunk of learning that is (1) built around explicit learning objectives, (2) openly licensed, (3) tagged with metadata, and (4) reusable across contexts. My sims qualify the moment I add objectives + a CC license + metadata and deposit them in OER Commons or MERLOT. Learn the Creative Commons license types (CC BY, CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC, and so on) so I can both use others' OER correctly and license my own.

Note: the `oer-course-finder` skill can pull real, license-verified OER for a specific course when I am ready to build one. Use it per course, not in the abstract.

What draws me to LDT is that it’s the applied version of that question: how do you design a digital experience that a working adult can actually use. I’d like to teach in a program that takes that seriously and does it at scale.

### 2. What experience do you have teaching adult learners asynchronously online?
I teach community college students online and in hybrid, and my population skews older than people assume: working adults, parents, career changers, students taking one course at a time. So the design constraints are the same ones your students live with, which is that the course has to be legible on a phone at 9pm. What I’ve learned is that asynchronous doesn’t mean absent. I put a lot of structure into predictable due dates, short recorded feedback instead of long written comments when the point is nuance, and clear weekly entry points so nobody wastes their first twenty minutes figuring out where to start. I’ll be honest that most of my formal graduate-level teaching has been through course design and faculty development rather than as instructor of record in a graduate program, so co-teaching under a lead instructor is a good fit for how I’d want to enter.

### 3. Walk us through your experience with Canvas.
I teach in Canvas now and I build in it, not just deliver in it. I’ve built full courses as IMSCC packages for import, which means I work at the level of modules, outcomes, and the export structure, not just the front-end pages. I also led the campus LMS team back in the Blackboard era and co-chaired the district eLearning Advisory Group, and I contributed to the RFP that led Maricopa to adopt Canvas. I want to be precise about that, I didn’t run the migration and I wasn’t on the migration team, I was on the evaluation and advisory side. Practically, for this role, nobody would need to teach me SpeedGrader, rubrics, sections, or how to keep a gradebook clean across a 7.5-week session.

### 4. This role is co-teaching inside someone else’s course design. How do you handle that?
That’s the part I actually like. I’ve been the program director who wrote the shared syllabus, so I know what it costs when one section drifts and students compare notes. My rule is that consistency is a student equity issue, not a control issue, so I teach the design that’s handed to me and I don’t quietly rewrite assignments mid-session. Where I add value is inside the lane: better feedback, faster turnaround, catching the student who went quiet in week three. If I think something in the design isn’t working, I document what I saw with examples and bring it to the lead instructor at the end of the session, when it can actually be used.

### 5. How do you use AI in your own teaching and design work?
Three ways, and all of them are scaffolding rather than substitution. In my design courses, students write their own client-discovery questions, which I require them to generate themselves, and then AI plays the fictitious client that answers those questions so their branding decisions get steered the way a real brief would. The thinking stays theirs and the AI plays a role. Second, I built a course-design tool called Dial Your Course that’s grounded in a vetted reference corpus, authentic assessment, UDL 3.0, regular and substantive interaction, Bloom’s, backward design, and a QM and OSCQR crosswalk, so it argues from sources instead of from vibes. Third, on the research side, I run persona agents to surface usability problems before I put them in front of real people. It’s a prototype in every case, not production, and I say so.

### 6. Where is the line between AI helping a graduate design student and doing the work for them?
The line I use is that AI can carry the labor but not the judgment. A learning designer’s job is deciding what the problem is, who it’s for, and what evidence would count as success, and if AI makes those calls the student hasn’t learned the profession. So generating a first pass at a storyboard, or rewriting an objective five ways to compare them, that’s fine and it’s what practitioners actually do. Choosing the objective, defending it against the needs assessment, and explaining why the alternative was worse, that has to be the student. In grading I look for the reasoning trail, not for detection. If I can’t see a decision being made, the artifact doesn’t earn the grade no matter how polished it is.

### 7. Tell us about a time you evaluated an existing program or system.
I’m leading a student-journey usability study across all ten Maricopa colleges right now, covering roughly 140,000 students. I built 50 demographic-grounded synthetic persona agents run through an orchestrator to walk the same service journeys, and then a human validates the severity ratings, because a model’s sense of “how bad is this” is not trustworthy on its own. The central finding is that the same service is named differently at every college, so a student who gets routed once often starts over from scratch at the next door. That’s the kind of finding you only get by looking across a system instead of auditing one college at a time, and it’s exactly the evaluation-of-learning-systems question your LDT 506 students are asked to take on.

### 8. How do you give feedback that students actually use?
Kind but improvement-focused, and specific enough that they can act on it before the next deadline. In a 7.5-week session that means turnaround matters more than length, so I’d rather return something useful in 48 hours than something exhaustive in a week. I use the rubric language deliberately so the feedback and the score are obviously the same conversation, and when the issue is a judgment call rather than a rule I record 90 seconds of audio or screen capture instead of writing three paragraphs, because tone survives voice better than text. And I name one thing that worked, not as padding, but because designers need to know which of their instincts to keep.

### 9. Have you taught large-enrollment online courses?
Not at the scale you run here, and I’d rather say that plainly. My sections are community college sized. What I do have is the system-level version of the same problem, which is designing something that has to work for 140,000 students across ten institutions without me being in the room, and running program-level consistency across multiple sections and instructors as a program director. The habits transfer: rubric-driven grading so scoring holds up across a big pile, templated feedback stems that I then make specific, triage so the students at risk of disappearing get contacted first, and a clean gradebook so the coordinator never has to reconstruct anything. If a section here is much larger than I’m used to, I’d want to know the norms for turnaround and workload up front rather than guess.

### 10. Our students come from all over the world. How do you support them?
My honest answer is that my direct experience is with a very diverse domestic population rather than a globally distributed one, so I’d be learning some of this. What I’ve built toward is the underlying problem, which is that people can’t use a service they can’t parse. CopaMigo, a student services routing prototype I built, detects the language a student writes or speaks in and answers in that same language, because at my college the barrier often isn’t motivation, it’s that the information exists only in English and only in institutional vocabulary. In an asynchronous course that translates into practical things: never make a deadline depend on knowing a U.S. holiday, keep due times explicit about time zone, avoid idiom in assignment prompts, and treat a confused question as a design defect in my instructions rather than a deficit in the student.

### 11. What’s your approach to accessibility?
Accessible from the first draft, never as a cleanup pass, because retrofitting costs hours and usually still ships something worse. Everything I build goes out to WCAG 2.1 AA: real alt text, one main landmark, no skipped heading levels, visible focus states, labeled form controls, 4.5:1 contrast on all text, and layouts that collapse to one column on a phone. I serve as a lead reviewer for Quality Matters and OSCQR, so I also review other people’s courses against those standards, and Dial Your Course carries UDL 3.0 and the QM/OSCQR crosswalk in its reference corpus so the recommendations come from the standard rather than from a guess. The part I care most about is the one that isn’t a checklist, which is whether a student with limited executive bandwidth can find the next thing to do in under a minute.

### 12. How do you build relationships and presence in an asynchronous course?
By being predictable and by being a person. Predictable means the same rhythm every week, announcements that go out when I said they would, and feedback that arrives on schedule, because in asynchronous space reliability is what trust is made of. Being a person means short video where video does something text can’t, replying in a voice that sounds like a human read the work, and reaching out privately the first week someone goes quiet instead of the fourth. I also design so students have a reason to see each other’s work, since critique is the core of every design course I teach and it does more for community than any icebreaker. My north star is that students should leave with something they keep using, and that only happens if they felt like someone was on the other end.

### 13. Why teach part-time for ASU when you already have a full-time faculty job?
Two reasons and neither of them is money. First, I want to teach the graduate version of what I’ve been doing for 25 years, which is designing learning with technology, and this program is the best one in the country at it. Second, my own work is heading toward learning design and AI systems, and I’d rather do that alongside people who publish and evaluate in the field than in isolation at my own campus. I teach in Arizona and I live in Colorado, so I’m already working remotely with distributed colleagues and I know how to hold that. I’m also clear-eyed that this is a pool, that the load is capped, and that a semester may or may not materialize.

## The Engineering Learning framework (verified July 8, learn to speak all four)

Source: trefnycenter.mines.edu/engineering-learning. Four phases, presented as a cycle but explicitly **non-linear and iterative**, and collaborative (they stress working with colleagues throughout). This is the shared language of both Mines Online and Trefny, so I should use these words.

1. **Design.** Articulate the course vision and build three foundational deliverables: measurable, specific learning outcomes, summative assessments aligned to those outcomes, and a motivating course description (purpose, value, relevance). Together these form the **course guide**. (This is the "outcomes drive the design" heart of their philosophy.)
2. **Build.** Use the course guide to create the actual components: lessons, activities, policies, assessments. In online contexts this phase produces a "polished course build."
3. **Enact.** Teach the course, interact with students, and monitor feedback (engagement, assignment performance, early-course surveys, end-of-course evals), adjusting while teaching.
4. **Reflect.** Review what worked and what did not, discuss with other instructors or the Trefny Center or Mines Online colleagues, and refine for next time.

**My hook:** this maps almost one-to-one onto how I redesigned AVC 100 (outcomes first, aligned low-stakes assessments, data-informed revision from success-rate and Canvas analytics) and onto Render and CopaMigo (built backward from the outcome the learner needs). I can narrate my own work in their four words without forcing it.
