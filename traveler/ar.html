The 2027 edition of The Traveler adds augmented reality. Point a phone at a printed artwork and the piece stands up in 3D, in its real color, so a reader can turn a sculpture around that a flat page could only show from one side.

Why AR

Ceramics, sculpture, jewelry, and other 3D work lose their form when they are flattened into a photograph. AR gives each piece back its depth without changing the printed magazine: the page stays the page, and the phone adds the third dimension.

Who Does What

  • 3D modeling students (AVC185) assist with the scanning, using the 3D scanner in STEAMworks, and help build the models.
  • The Traveler production team photographs each piece, builds the color models, and tests every printed page with a phone.
  • The artists give written permission before their work is scanned or published in 3D.

Three Ways to Capture

Color is the point. A grey model of a glazed pot is not the artwork, so whatever method is used, every model has to carry the piece’s real color and surface.

MethodStrengthWeakness
Photo method (photogrammetry)True color and surface, because the model is built from the photographs; needs only a phone, a light box, and RealityScanStruggles with glass, shiny glazes, and plain surfaces with no texture to track
STEAMworks 3D scannerPrecise geometry and fine detail, and real modeling practice for AVC185 studentsColor is often weak or missing, so a scan alone can come out grey
Hybrid: scan for shape, photos for colorThe best of both: the scanner’s accurate form wrapped in the photographs’ true colorTakes an extra step in Blender to transfer the photo color onto the scanned shape; worth it for a few key pieces, likely too slow for all forty

Open decision. Photo only, scanner only for the pieces photos cannot handle, or the hybrid for a few key pieces. Test captures of the same practice piece with each method will decide.

Scan Plus Photos

Laying photographs over a scanned model is an established technique, usually called texture projection or photo texturing. The scanner captures the exact shape, the photos capture the true color, and software aligns the two. RealityScan on a desktop computer can combine scanner data with photos in one project, and Blender can transfer photo color onto a scanned mesh. It adds steps, so it fits a few key pieces best; the test captures will show whether it is worth doing for more.

How to Capture a Piece

Before any piece is captured, the artist signs a permission form and the piece is logged on a tracking sheet. The full student production guide walks the team through practice lessons before the real pieces arrive.

1. Build the light box

A cardboard box with paper windows spreads lamp light so the art has no shiny hot spots. It takes about 30 minutes, or borrow a light tent.

  1. Stand a large box (at least 2 feet on every side) with the open side facing you.
  2. On the left, right, and top, cut out a window, leaving a 2-inch border.
  3. Tape white tissue or parchment paper over each window, smooth and flat; use two layers if the bulb shows through.
  4. Tape a sheet of white poster board high on the back wall and let it curve down onto the floor, with no fold, so no line shows behind the art.
  5. Place a daylight (5000K) LED lamp outside the left and right windows, about a foot away; add one on top if the top looks dark.
  6. Check with your hand: the shadow should be soft and fuzzy, not sharp. Tape the lamp positions so they never move.

2. Set up the photo station

The phone stays still on a tripod and the art turns on a lazy Susan, photographed from three heights.

  1. Center a lazy Susan in the box, with patterned paper on top so the app can track the turning.
  2. Set the tripod about 2 feet in front of the art; clip a circular polarizer on the phone lens and twist it until shiny spots are smallest.
  3. Turn off overhead lights that cause glare.
  4. Put matching tape marks on the lazy Susan and the box floor, and tape the tripod leg positions, so every session starts the same.

3. Photograph the piece

  1. Start a new scan in RealityScan and name it for the entry.
  2. From the low position, take a photo, turn the lazy Susan about 10° (two fingers at the edge), and repeat until the tape marks line up: about 36 photos.
  3. Repeat at eye level, then from above, tipped down at the piece: about 100 photos in all.
  4. Build the model, check every side for holes and true color, and export it as a GLB file.
  5. If the app loses track of the turning piece, leave it still and walk a slow circle around it at the same three heights.

4. Scan or spin

Each piece gets one capture attempt, capped at 20 minutes. A clean model goes on to AR; a piece that will not model, such as glass, jewelry, or a high-gloss glaze, becomes a 360° spin instead.

  1. For a spin, lock every camera setting in Blackmagic Camera (white balance 5000K, ISO, shutter, focus).
  2. Shoot exactly 36 photos at eye level, one every 10°.
  3. In Lightroom, correct the first photo, then copy its settings to all 36 so the color never jumps.

5. Put it on the page

  1. Shrink each model so it loads fast on a phone, then publish it on a web page with a “View in AR” button.
  2. Train the printed photo of the piece as an image target, so pointing a phone at the page brings up the model.
  3. Add a small QR code beside each photo as the backup, and test both on the real Traveler paper, in bright and dim light, on iPhone and Android.

Technology

  • The STEAMworks 3D scanner (AVC185) and RealityScan for capture, with Polycam as the backup
  • Blender to transfer photo color onto scanned shapes, for hybrid pieces
  • Blackmagic Camera to lock exposure and focus on the phone, and Lightroom for color
  • GLB models shown with Google’s model-viewer for “View in AR”
  • MindAR image targets so the printed page triggers the model, plus QR codes
  • A 360° image viewer for pieces that cannot be modeled
  • InDesign and Photoshop for the printed issue, as always
