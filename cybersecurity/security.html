Program flags for the program director (not for learners).

  • Prerequisite gap. Learners in this track take Introduction to Networking, not a Linux course, before Information Security Fundamentals, and no corequisite course (a Windows or hardware support course) appears in the 7-credit plan. Enrolling them requires Program Director permission for the prerequisite and a decision on the corequisite. Record both as written program policy, not case by case. (The program’s Arizona research raised the same issue for the 12-credit version.) Week 10 Lab Part C (“Help desk identity tasks in Windows and Microsoft 365”) is outlined for the day-one account tasks: unlocks, password resets, group membership, MFA re-registration as a documented click path, and tickets written to a standard. It is not yet built, timed or graded; it will be built and timed in the pilot. It is not a substitute for the corequisite and does not teach Active Directory domains, Group Policy, device imaging or hardware support.
  • No command-line foundation. A Linux course is where learners would normally gain Linux command-line skill. The labs below use a Linux shell. Week 8 therefore opens with a one-hour command-line basics mini-lesson and a guided environment setup, and every lab gives every command. Plan an optional tutoring hour in Week 8 and publish its day and time in the syllabus.
  • Lab platform decision (launch blocker). The design assumes a hosted, browser-based Linux lab as the primary environment, with local Docker as the alternative for learners whose computers pass the minimum-spec check (see Lab environment and setup). Which hosted platform the college uses (a college virtual desktop, a licensed lab service or another option) is a program decision that must be made, priced and tested before enrollment opens. Until it is made, the course cannot launch.
  • Combined weekly load. Information Security Fundamentals is about 19.5 hours a week (18 in Week 14). Career Launch runs alongside it. After the 25 November 2026 rebalancing, Career Launch is about 3.5 hours in Week 10, about 5.25 hours in each of Weeks 11 and 12 (practice exam 1 and its review moved to Week 11; targeted practice and mock interview 1 moved to Week 12), about 3 hours in Week 13 and about 4.5 hours in Week 14. The combined load is therefore about 23 hours in Week 10, about 24.75 in Weeks 11 and 12, and about 22.5 in Weeks 13 and 14 (security course drops to 18 hours in Week 14). Practice exams are counted in Career Launch only, never in Information Security Fundamentals. Both syllabi publish these same figures. Weeks 11 and 12 remain above the 22.5-hour program average; the remaining fix is to move about 4 hours of Career Launch work into Weeks 1 to 9, where it runs at about 2.6 hours a week.
  • Security+ alignment is a program choice. The official Information Security Fundamentals outline does not name Security+. The SY0-801 mapping below is added value, not an official requirement.

Workload and Hours

The brief asked for “about 9.5 hours of learner work per week … 3 credits x 45 hours / 7 weeks.” That arithmetic gives a different number:

  • 3 credits x 45 hours = 135 hours of total learner work. The 45-hours-per-credit figure follows the federal credit-hour definition: at least one hour of direct instruction plus two hours of out-of-class work per week for about 15 weeks, or an equivalent amount of work over a different period (34 CFR 600.2, “Credit hour”).
  • 135 hours / 7 weeks = about 19.3 hours a week, not 9.5. (9.5 hours a week is what 135 hours spread over 14 weeks would give.)

Design decision. This build plans 19.5 hours a week in Weeks 8 to 13 and 18 hours in Week 14, for 135 hours total. Building to 9.5 hours a week would deliver about half of the work a 3-credit course must represent, which puts the credit award and Workforce Pell eligibility at risk. If the program cannot ask working adults for 19 to 20 hours a week on this course alone, the fix is a longer calendar, not a lighter course. The learning designer and program director must confirm this choice (see Claims and links the course designer must confirm).

How the hours were estimated (25 November 2026 re-estimate). The first draft gave every week the same template and overstated video time while understating reading and assessments. This version re-estimates each activity at a novice pace with a stated method:

  • Reading: about 10 pages an hour for NIST and other dense technical text, about 12 pages an hour for Tolboom and plain-language government pages. Only readings marked Core count toward the hours. Readings marked Reference (not required) support the labs and the exam but are not counted and are never graded.
  • Video: 1.5 times the runtime, to allow for pausing, rewatching and embedded questions.
  • Labs: the time a novice with no command-line background is likely to need, about 1.5 to 2 times what an experienced user would take, including one round of troubleshooting.
  • Writing: about 250 to 300 finished words an hour for analytical writing with citations; recordings at about three times their length to plan, record and check.
  • Live session: 1.0 hour. The weekly 90-minute session is shared: this course uses and counts 60 minutes, and Career Launch uses and counts its 30-minute career segment, so no minute is counted twice.

Where a week ran over 19.5 hours under this method, readings were moved to Reference rather than raising hours. These are still estimates. A pilot with two or three real novice testers who keep time logs will set the final figures before the first full cohort, and the weekly totals may change after the pilot.

Lab Environment and Setup

Primary option: hosted lab. The course is designed for a hosted, browser-based Linux lab provided by the college, so that any learner with a current web browser, including on a Chromebook or a shared computer, can do every lab. The hosted images include Docker, the Tolboom lab files and a weekly seed file used for the lab token (below). Which platform the college uses is a program decision.

Alternative: local Docker. Learners whose own computer passes the minimum-spec check may install Docker Desktop (Windows or macOS) or Docker Engine (Linux) instead. Docker Desktop is free for personal and educational use under Docker’s subscription terms.

Minimum-spec check (complete before Week 8 starts, ideally in program Week 7). Run this check in the last week of Introduction to Networking so problems are solved before Information Security Fundamentals opens.

  1. Operating system. Windows 10 or 11 (64-bit), macOS on an Intel or Apple Silicon Mac, or a current 64-bit Linux. Chromebooks and tablets use the hosted lab.
  2. Memory and disk. At least 8 GB of RAM and at least 20 GB of free disk space.
  3. Administrator rights. You can install software on this computer. Work-owned computers often block this; use the hosted lab instead.
  4. Virtualization (Windows). Press Ctrl+Shift+Esc to open Task Manager, select Performance, then CPU. Find “Virtualization.” If it says “Enabled,” you are ready. If it says “Disabled,” turning it on requires a setting in your computer’s firmware (BIOS or UEFI); ask for help in the Week 8 tutoring hour or use the hosted lab.
  5. Result. Submit the one-question “Computer check” survey in Canvas: hosted lab or local Docker. Anyone who cannot pass the check uses the hosted lab, with no penalty and no explanation needed.

Step-by-step local setup on Windows (about 1 to 2 hours the first time). Windows users always use Ubuntu (WSL), the Linux terminal that WSL 2 installs, for every lab command, so the commands in this course work exactly as written. Do not use PowerShell or Command Prompt for lab commands.

  1. Open the Start menu, type PowerShell, right-click Windows PowerShell and choose Run as administrator.
  2. Type wsl --install and press Enter. This installs WSL 2 and Ubuntu. Restart when asked.
  3. After the restart, an Ubuntu window opens and asks you to make a Linux username and password. Write them down. (The password does not show as you type. That is normal.)
  4. Download Docker Desktop for Windows from Docker’s install page and run the installer. Keep the option Use WSL 2 instead of Hyper-V selected.
  5. Start Docker Desktop and wait until it says the engine is running. In Docker Desktop settings, open Resources, then WSL integration, and turn on integration for Ubuntu.
  6. Open Ubuntu from the Start menu. Type docker run hello-world. If you see “Hello from Docker,” setup is done.
  7. To open your Downloads folder from Ubuntu, type cd /mnt/c/Users/ then press Tab to fill in your Windows user name, then type /Downloads. (A screenshot of a terminal opened in a lab folder on Windows and on macOS is in the Canvas setup page.)

Step-by-step local setup on macOS (about 30 to 60 minutes).

  1. Check your chip: Apple menu, About This Mac. Note “Apple” (M1, M2 and later) or “Intel.”
  2. Download the matching Docker Desktop for Mac from Docker’s install page, open it and drag Docker to Applications.
  3. Start Docker from Applications and accept the prompts.
  4. Open Terminal (press Command+Space, type Terminal). Type docker run hello-world.
  5. On Apple Silicon, some lab images are built for Intel chips only. The lab steps add --platform linux/amd64 where this matters. A “platform does not match” warning is expected and safe.

Antivirus and security software. Some lab files (the Week 13 log4j lab and the Week 11 password list) contain text that antivirus tools flag on purpose, because they demonstrate known attacks inside containers. If Windows Security or another tool removes or quarantines a lab file, do not turn off your antivirus. Post in Lab Help, and use the hosted lab for that week, where the files are already in place.

“Docker will not start” troubleshooting page. Canvas has a troubleshooting page with the ten most common setup errors and their fixes (virtualization off, WSL not installed, not enough disk space, Docker Desktop not running, permission denied, Apple Silicon platform warning, port already in use, antivirus quarantine, docker-compose not found, and a lab folder opened in the wrong place). If setup fails through no fault of your own, you get an extension and move to the hosted lab. You never receive a zero for a setup problem.

Lost container work. A container started with docker run -it keeps your work only while that container exists. If you type exit or close the window, the container stops, and the next docker run starts a fresh, empty one. To get back into the same container, type docker ps -a to list your containers, find the name in the last column, then type docker start -ai <name>. Labs that need more than one sitting say so in a boxed note.

Lab token (every lab). Each lab image in the hosted lab and in the course’s local images contains a weekly seed file at /opt/seclab/seed. Inside the lab container, type echo -n "YOURCANVASID-$(cat /opt/seclab/seed)" | sha256sum | cut -c1-16, replacing YOURCANVASID with your Canvas user ID (Account, Profile). The 16-character result is your lab token for that week. Paste it into your lab report. The instructor checks it against a key computed from the same seed. The token shows that the commands were run in the course lab environment by the account that submitted them; it does not prove who typed them or that the learner understood them, which is why each week also has a short recording.

Docker Compose. Current Docker includes Compose as docker compose (with a space). Every lab uses that form. The older docker-compose (with a hyphen) is deprecated and is often missing; if a classmate’s notes or Tolboom’s text show the hyphen form, use the space form instead.

Screen readers. Every Docker step can be done from the terminal, which works better with screen readers than the Docker Desktop window. Submitting pasted terminal text instead of screenshots is a standard option for everyone.

Windows lab (Week 10 Lab Part C only; outlined, built and timed in the pilot). One planned lab part uses Windows instead of Linux, because help desk account work happens in Windows and Microsoft 365. The same order applies: hosted first, local second.

  1. Hosted (primary). A Windows desktop in the college’s hosted lab, where you are an administrator of your own disposable Windows machine.
  2. Local alternative. Your own Windows 10 or 11 computer, in Windows PowerShell (not PowerShell 7) opened with Run as administrator. The lab creates practice accounts with fictitious names and deletes them at the end. Never do this lab on a work-owned, school-owned or shared computer. If you would rather not change accounts on your own computer, Microsoft offers a free 90-day Windows 11 Enterprise evaluation as an ISO file (registration required); install it only inside a virtual machine, never over your own Windows, because Microsoft states it cannot be uninstalled and recommends it for IT professionals.
  3. No Windows available (macOS, Chromebook, or a work computer). Use the click-path walkthrough in Canvas: a captioned instructor recording of each ticket on a Windows virtual machine, with the command output as pasted text. You still write all four ticket notes. No penalty and no explanation needed.
  4. Microsoft 365. There is no free Microsoft 365 or Entra ID sandbox for individual learners. The Microsoft 365 Developer Program’s E5 sandbox is now limited to qualifying Visual Studio subscribers, ISV Success or AI Cloud Partner Program members and Premier or Unified Support customers (Developer Program FAQ, checked 25 November 2026), and Microsoft Learn’s free Entra labs expect your own tenant. So the Microsoft 365 steps are click paths you read in Microsoft Learn and write into your ticket notes, not steps you perform.

Weekly “walk me through one decision” recording. Each lab (Weeks 8 to 13) ends with a 2-minute recording: pick one decision you made in the lab (a command you chose, a value you recorded, a fix you applied) and explain why. RSI and Course Conventions

Weekly rhythm. Modules open Monday at 12:00 a.m. The live session runs Tuesday evening for 90 minutes and is shared with Career Launch: this course uses and counts 60 minutes, and Career Launch uses and counts its 30-minute career segment. Initial discussion posts are due Thursday at 11:59 p.m. Replies, labs and assessments are due Sunday at 11:59 p.m. Week 14 uses a different schedule for the capstone (see Week 14). The instructor returns quick-check rubric scores on labs within 72 hours and full written feedback on assessments within 72 hours where possible (5 days at most), so feedback arrives before the next submission. The instructor answers messages within 24 hours on weekdays.

Section size and grading time. Plan for a section cap of 25 learners. At that size, expect about 4 to 5 hours a week to score labs with the quick-check rubric, about 6 to 8 hours for full written assessment feedback, and about 1 hour for recordings and spot checks, on top of the live session. Part 0. Command line for beginners (1.5 hours). A short Canvas mini-lesson with a captioned video and practice in the hosted lab or your own terminal. You will learn:

  1. Open a terminal. Hosted lab: click Terminal. Windows: open Ubuntu from the Start menu (the Linux terminal you installed with WSL). macOS: open Terminal. The text ending in $ or # is the prompt; it waits for a command.
  2. Where am I? Type pwd (print working directory) to see the folder you are in. A path such as /home/you/Downloads names a folder, from the top down.
  3. What is here? Type ls to list the files in the folder. Type ls -l to see details. The -l part is a flag: an option that changes what a command does.
  4. Move. Type cd Downloads to move into a folder, cd .. to go up one folder, and cd alone to go home.
  5. Read a file. Type cat filename to print a file.
  6. Save typing. Press the up arrow to bring back your last command. Press Tab to finish a file or folder name.
  7. Copy and paste. In most terminals, paste with Ctrl+Shift+V (Windows Ubuntu and Linux) or Command+V (macOS), or right-click. Copy and paste long commands from Canvas rather than retyping them.
  8. Stop something. Press Ctrl+C to stop a command that is still running.
  9. Pipe. The | symbol sends the output of one command into the next, for example ls -l | grep txt.

Common pitfalls.

  • Learners treat “two passwords” as MFA. Re-teach the three factor kinds from the NIST MFA page.
  • Learners mix up RADIUS and TACACS+. One line for the exam: RADIUS hides only the user’s password in transit and combines authentication and authorization in one reply (RFC 2865, Sections 2 and 5.2); TACACS+ hides (RFC 8907 says “obfuscates”) the whole packet body and separates authentication, authorization and accounting (RFC 8907, Sections 1 and 4.5). RFC 8907 also warns that this obfuscation is weak by modern standards, so TACACS+ traffic should run over a protected management network. Security+ materials often say TACACS+ “encrypts the whole packet”; accept that answer on the exam’s terms and teach the RFC wording.
  • CHAP and MS-CHAPv2 appear in the official outline. Teach them as legacy: MS-CHAPv2 is considered broken and should not protect new systems.
  • Learners assume forced 60- or 90-day password changes are best practice. NIST SP 800-63B-4, Section 3.1.1.2, says verifiers shall not require periodic changes, except on evidence of compromise.
  • Tolboom 7.1.2 gives an SMS code as a typical MFA example. Point out that NIST and CISA now treat SMS and app codes as phishable and prefer phishing-resistant MFA.
  • The octal permission numbers (such as 750) confuse beginners. Spend lab time on the read, write, execute letters before numbers.

Common pitfalls.

Putting a dollar figure on risk: SLE, ARO and ALE. SME-authored; approval required before release. The Security+ exam (SY0-801 5.2) asks you to calculate these. No open source assigned in this course defines them, so this box is written for the course SME to approve.

  • Single loss expectancy (SLE) is the expected cost of one occurrence: asset value x exposure factor (the share of the asset’s value lost, as a percentage).
  • Annualized rate of occurrence (ARO) is how many times a year you expect it to happen (0.5 means once every two years).
  • Annualized loss expectancy (ALE) is the expected cost per year: SLE x ARO.

Worked example (fictitious numbers). Agave Springs Family Clinic values its front-desk scheduling system, including the cost to rebuild it and lost appointments, at $40,000. A ransomware infection would cost about 25% of that (exposure factor 0.25), so SLE = $40,000 x 0.25 = $10,000. The clinic expects one such infection every two years (ARO = 0.5), so ALE = $10,000 x 0.5 = $5,000 a year. A control that costs less than about $5,000 a year and removes most of that risk is worth considering. Quantitative figures like these sit beside, not in place of, the qualitative SP 800-30r1 scores.

Choice Text (character count) Result

  • B consequence: Dana adds phishing-resistant MFA for email and admin accounts first, and a short, blame-free training. Feedback: “Correct. NIST recommends multifactor authentication, ideally phishing-resistant MFA.   • B consequence: You treat the account as compromised and open an incident ticket at 7:08 a.m. Feedback: “Correct.   • C consequence: Devon approves the next attacker prompt at 2:15 p.m., during work hours. Feedback: “Time of day does not tell a real prompt from an attack. The user should approve only prompts for sign-ins they just started, and report the rest.” (CISA)

  • B consequence: Lorraine agrees to a 15-character minimum for passwords used alone, a blocklist check, and changes only when there is evidence of compromise. Feedback: “Correct. SP 800-63B-4, Section 3.1.1.2, sets a 15-character minimum for single-factor passwords (8 when used within MFA), requires a blocklist check, and bars periodic forced changes and composition rules.” (NIST SP 800-63B-4)

  • B consequence: The clinic enables number matching now and plans FIDO security keys for admins and billing staff. Feedback: “Correct. CISA calls number matching one of the best interim mitigations when phishing-resistant MFA is not yet possible, and calls phishing-resistant MFA the gold standard. Plain-language definitions for learners. Each points to the source to read; definitions are summaries, not quotations.

Term Meaning Source
802.1X A standard that checks a device’s identity, often with a certificate, before it may use a wired or wireless network port. NSA Network Infrastructure Security Guidance, Section 2.5
AAA Authentication, authorization and accounting: proving who you are, deciding what you may do, and recording what you did. Tolboom 1.2.2
Account lockout A temporary block on signing in after too many failed attempts. It ends when an administrator unlocks the account or the lockout time runs out; it is not the same as a disabled account. Microsoft Learn, Account lockout threshold; Win32_UserAccount (Lockout, Disabled)
Access control list (ACL) A list attached to a file or network rule set that says who or what is allowed or denied. Tolboom 7.3.6
Alignment (DMARC) DMARC passes only when the domain that passed SPF or DKIM matches the domain in the From: line. RFC 7489, Section 3.1
Annualized loss expectancy (ALE) Expected loss per year from one risk: SLE x ARO. Information Security Fundamentals Week 14 box (SME approval required)
Annualized rate of occurrence (ARO) How many times a year a loss is expected to happen. Information Security Fundamentals Week 14 box (SME approval required)
Business impact analysis (BIA) Studying business functions to see how a disruption would affect them. NIST glossary; SP 800-34r1, Section 3.2
Chain of custody A record of who handled evidence, when, and what they did with it, so it can be trusted later. NIST SP 800-86, Section 3.1.2
CIA triad Confidentiality, integrity and availability, the three main goals of security. Tolboom 1.2.1
Compensating control A safeguard used instead of a recommended one that gives similar protection. NIST glossary
Confabulation Generative AI producing confident but false content. NIST AI 600-1, Section 2.2
Control category What a safeguard is made of: technical/logical, managerial/administrative, operational or physical/environmental. SY0-801 1.1; Information Security Fundamentals Week 8 table (SME approval required)

Lab token A 16-character code you compute inside the lab container from your Canvas ID and a weekly seed file, showing the lab ran in the course environment. Information Security Fundamentals Lab environment and setup

RADIUS and TACACS+ Central services that check sign-ins for network and device access. RADIUS hides only the password and combines authentication and authorization; TACACS+ hides the whole packet body and keeps authentication, authorization and accounting separate. NIST glossary; RFC 2865; RFC 8907; NSA guide, Section 4
RTO and RPO How long a system can be down (RTO) and how much recent data can be lost (RPO). NIST glossary; SP 800-34r1
Salt Random data stored with a password hash so identical passwords store differently and precomputed tables do not work. In a yescrypt line it sits between the third and fourth $ signs. Tolboom 2.5, 2.11; NIST SP 800-63B-4, Section 3.1.1.2
Security zone A part of a network grouped by trust level and separated by controls such as firewalls. NIST SP 800-41r1, Section 3.1
Shared account One sign-in used by several people, so actions cannot be traced to a person. Information Security Fundamentals Week 10 term (SME approval required)
Single loss expectancy (SLE) Expected cost of one occurrence of a loss: asset value x exposure factor. Information Security Fundamentals Week 14 box (SME approval required)
SSH key A key pair used to sign in to an SSH server without sending a password; the server keeps the public key in authorized_keys. Tolboom 7.3.7
Stale account An account that has not been used for a long time but is still active. Information Security Fundamentals Week 10 term (SME approval required)
Temporary Access Pass (TAP) A time-limited passcode an administrator issues in Microsoft Entra ID so a user can sign in and set up a new MFA method, for example after losing a phone. Microsoft Learn, Configure a Temporary Access Pass
VLAN A virtual LAN: devices grouped into their own network segment by switch settings, even when they share the same physical switches. NSA Network Infrastructure Security Guidance, Section 2.2
Zero Trust A security model that does not grant trust based on network location. NIST SP 800-207, Section 2.1
