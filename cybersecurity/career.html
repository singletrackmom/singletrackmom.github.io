The 1-credit course that runs alongside all fourteen weeks: learners map a realistic first job, build their own AI job search and mentor agents, and practice until the interview is familiar. Part of a prototype program design.

  • Course: Career Launch and Security+ Exam Readiness, 1 credit
  • Program: a prototype 7-credit cybersecurity fast track at an Arizona community college. Introduction to Networking runs in weeks 1 to 7, Information Security Fundamentals runs in weeks 8 to 14, and Career Launch runs alongside both for all 14 weeks.
  • Format: 14-week online cohort in Canvas, flexible pacing within each week, weekly module release, one 90-minute live session a week for the whole program (recorded, with an instructor-graded alternative). The session is shared and no minute is counted twice: Career Launch uses and counts its 30-minute career segment (25 to 30 minutes of career content), and the technical course running that week uses and counts the other 60 minutes (Information Security Fundamentals counts 60 minutes in weeks 8 to 14).
  • Workload: 1 credit is about 45 hours of learner work across 14 weeks, about 3 hours a week on average. The whole 7-credit program is about 22.5 hours a week in most weeks and about 24.75 hours in weeks 11 and 12 (see “A typical week”).

A typical week. Weeks 1 to 10 take about 2.5 to 3.5 hours, including the live career segment. Weeks 11 and 12 are the heavy weeks, about 5.25 hours each, because practice exam 1 and its review (week 11) and mock interview 1 and targeted practice (week 12) happen there. Weeks 13 and 14 are lighter: about 3 hours in week 13 and about 4.5 hours in week 14. These figures were rebalanced on 25 November 2026 so that the final two weeks, which hold the Information Security Fundamentals incident response unit and capstone, are not overloaded.

With Information Security Fundamentals (about 19.5 hours a week, 18 in week 14), the combined program load is about 23 hours in week 10, about 24.75 hours in weeks 11 and 12, and about 22.5 hours in weeks 13 and 14. Information Security Fundamentals publishes the same figures. Practice exams are counted only in this course, never in Information Security Fundamentals, even though they also help you review the security course. Optional activities, such as informational interviews, sending applications and visiting ARIZONA@WORK, take extra time and are never required.

Feedback. Your instructor writes feedback on every career deliverable within 5 days of the due date. Peer comments, where used, are extra and never replace instructor feedback or grading.

Grading. Career Launch uses the college’s standard letter grades. Proposed weights: Units 1 to 5 portfolio deliverables 55 percent; mock interviews and the 90-day plan 20 percent; exam readiness 15 percent; practice items, checks and discussions 10 percent. Career deliverables are scored out of 100. A score of 80 or higher means the piece is ready to use with employers. Below 80, your instructor invites one revision and sets the date in the feedback.

What you must do to pass. Submit your career portfolio, complete one mock interview and take both practice exams (your score does not decide whether you pass). The employer-panel interview in week 14 is strongly encouraged; if you cannot attend, your instructor offers a makeup interview. Informational interviews, job shadows, submitted job applications, ARIZONA@WORK visits and the placement follow-up consent are encouraged and valuable, but they are never requirements.

Done when. Every activity in this course has a “Done when” line, so you always know what finished looks like.

Timed checks. Short reading checks have a 15-minute timer, and practice exams have a 90-minute timer. If time runs out, Canvas submits the answers you have saved, and you can retake any check as many times as you like. Workbooks are not timed: you can save, close and come back. Plan on paper first if that helps.

Discussions. Discussions count toward the practice and discussion grade. Missing one costs a few points, not your course. If you miss one, post late and tell your instructor; late posts receive feedback.

Regular and substantive interaction plan

  • Direct instruction: a 25 to 30 minute career segment in every weekly live session, counted as 30 minutes of the shared 90-minute session (recorded, with an instructor-graded alternative), plus two live build workshops in Unit 2 and live mock interviews in week 12 (instructor, Unit 5) and week 14 (employer panel, Unit 6).
  • Written feedback: the instructor writes feedback on every career deliverable, practice item and practice exam review within 5 days of the due date.
  • Facilitated discussion: one instructor-posed prompt per unit; the instructor replies to every initial post by Friday and posts a Monday wrap-up.
  • Proactive outreach: the instructor checks Canvas activity daily and personally contacts any learner with no engagement for 5 days, and logs the outreach.
  • Published schedule: every touchpoint appears in the syllabus and the Canvas calendar.

Both are built in the same order, which is how teams in industry build AI tools: set the goal, plan on paper, build, test, then use on a schedule. Unit 1 covers the goal and the paper plan. Unit 2 covers the build and the tests, in two live workshops. Units 3 to 6 use the agents on schedule.

Which Gemini feature you use. Adults 18 and older signed in to a personal Google account build a Gemini skill. (In this course, “Gemini skill” always means the Gemini feature. Your abilities are called “job skills” or “transferable skills.”) If you use a school or work Google account, build a Gem instead; Gems on education accounts are scheduled to change to Gemini skills in June 2027, and Google says they will convert automatically. The instruction text is the same for both.

If you do not want to use AI. Every AI task has a non-AI path with the same credit: a paper weekly check-in worksheet instead of the career mentor agent, a manual search on Arizona Job Connection, USAJOBS and employer sites instead of the job search agent, and an instructor alignment review instead of AI alignment. Tell your instructor which path you choose.

Privacy, the short version. No personal identifying information goes into AI. You will use a “redacted master resume” with placeholders such as [Name], [City, AZ], [Phone], [Email] and [LinkedIn URL]. You build and run the agents in your own account. The course never collects, stores or views your chats or agent files.

Early to-do: the fingerprint clearance card. Many Arizona state, school and health employers require a Level One fingerprint clearance card from the Arizona Department of Public Safety before you start work. Read Arizona Fingerprint Clearance Card in week 1 so you know the steps and cost. Do not apply until an employer or program tells you that you need one; they will tell you which type.

Overview

This unit helps you see where you are now, where entry-level IT and cybersecurity jobs actually start in the Phoenix area, and how your past work already prepares you. You will finish with a target role map: one realistic first job, the job skills it asks for, the transferable skills you already have, and the next step 12 to 24 months later.

Here is the honest picture. For most career changers, the first job after a program like this one is help desk, IT support or service desk work. These are the teams people call or message when a password, computer, app or account is not working. The government groups these jobs under the occupation code 15-1232, computer user support specialists. Security analyst jobs usually ask for one to two or more years of IT experience, and some in the Phoenix area, especially at defense contractors, also ask for an active security clearance. Help desk is not a detour. It is where most security professionals learn how users, accounts, devices and networks really behave.

A note on certifications. CompTIA A+ covers IT support basics (hardware, operating systems and troubleshooting) and is what many help desk postings ask for, while Security+ covers security fundamentals and carries more weight for security, SOC and defense-related roles; this course prepares you for Security+, and Unit 6 shows you how to decide whether to plan A+ next.

This unit serves CL-1 (evaluate roles and choose a realistic target) and CL-2 (translate prior experience). It also begins CL-3 by having you plan your two AI agents on paper.

By the end you will be able to

  1. Compare at least two entry-level IT roles in the Phoenix area by duties, typical requirements and local pay.
  2. Identify the realistic first role and the 12 to 24 month next step for your situation.
  3. Translate at least six tasks from your past work into transferable skills, using words from a real job posting.
  4. Plan, on paper, the goal, tasks, limits and schedule for a career mentor agent and a job search agent.

Practice

Practice A: Work strengths inventory

  1. Open the “Work Strengths Inventory” workbook in Canvas. It is not timed; you can save and come back.
  2. List every job you have held, including part-time, seasonal, volunteer and military roles. Use job titles only. You do not need employer names here.
  3. For each job, list three to five things you did often. Be specific: how many customers, what systems, what procedures, what problems.
  4. For each task, choose the closest IT support job skill from the list in the workbook (for example: troubleshooting, documentation, customer service under pressure, following procedures, protecting private information, working shifts, training others, speaking another language with customers).
  5. Languages. List every language you speak and your level in each: conversational, fluent, or written (reading and writing). In the Phoenix metro, English and Spanish service desk skills can be a real hiring advantage. If you speak only English, write that.
  6. Mark the two tasks you are proudest of. You will use them again in Units 3 and 5.

Done when: every job has at least three tasks, each matched to a job skill, and the languages field is filled in. Your instructor gives written feedback within 5 days.

Practice B: Plan your two AI agents on paper

Before anyone builds software, they write down what it is for. You will do the same.

Assessment: Target role map

Scenario: Your first meeting with a career advisor. You have booked a free appointment with a career advisor at an ARIZONA@WORK career center. The advisor asks you to bring a map: the job you are aiming for first, why it fits, what you already bring, what you still need, and where you want to be in two years.

Use your own real posting. The scenario only shows why this map matters.

Steps.

  1. Use a web search, Arizona Job Connection or Arizona State Jobs to find one current entry-level IT support, help desk or service desk posting in the Phoenix area.
  2. Do three quick checks (you will learn the full ten-point checklist in Unit 2): - The link works and the posting opens. - It appears on the employer’s own careers page, a real staffing firm’s own careers page (Unit 2 explains), Arizona Job Connection or Arizona State Jobs. - The posted date is within the last 30 days, or the closing date has not passed.
  3. Open the “Target Role Map” workbook in Canvas. This workbook is your submission; you do not need to make a separate file. It is not timed.
  4. Record the posting’s title, city or remote status, pay if listed, whether it requires a fingerprint clearance card, and its top five required job skills in the posting’s own words. In place of the employer’s name, write a short description, such as “regional hospital system” or “managed IT services firm.” This protects your privacy if you later apply there, and it keeps the focus on the skills.
  5. Look up local pay for that occupation on O*NET OnLine and record the 10th percentile, 25th percentile and median for the Phoenix-Mesa-Chandler area. Divide each by 2,080 to get the hourly equivalent.
  6. Fill in the two-column job skills table: on the left, the five required job skills; on the right, evidence from your past work or this program for each one. Write “gap” where you have none yet.
  7. For each gap, write one action you will take in this program (a lab, a reading, a certification step).
  8. Write your 12 to 24 month next step: the role you want after help desk, and one requirement it adds.
  9. Do not include your name, address, phone or email.

Done when: every field in the workbook is filled in and submitted.

Overview

In this unit you build two AI agents in Google Gemini, in two live workshops. The career mentor agent will check in with you every week for the rest of the course and beyond. The job search agent finds entry-level listings near you and helps you check that each one is real, current and safe.

You will follow the same order that industry teams use for AI tools. You set the goal and planned on paper in Unit 1. Now you build, then test, then set a schedule for use.

Two facts shape everything in this unit. First, Gemini can be wrong. Google’s own help page says Gemini can present inaccurate information as fact. So you verify every listing yourself. Second, a sample of chats on personal Google accounts is read by people at Google. So no personal identifying information goes into either agent.

If you chose the non-AI path, you still attend the workshops: you will build a paper check-in worksheet and a manual search routine instead, and do the same verification work.

This unit serves CL-3 (build, test and use AI agents responsibly) and CL-5 (a scam-aware job search with verified listings).

By the end you will be able to

  1. Build a Gemini skill (or a Gem, on an education account) from written instructions and two attached files.
  2. Test each agent with at least three planned prompts, including one that tries to make it break its own rules, and revise the instructions based on the results.
  3. Verify a job listing against a ten-point checklist, including the employer’s own careers page and the posted date.

Make your redacted master resume

This is the only version of your resume that ever goes into AI. Redacted means personal details are blacked out or replaced.

  1. Open a new Google Doc and name it “Redacted master resume.”
  2. Replace your contact details with placeholders, stand-ins formatted like the real thing: - [Name] - [City, AZ] - [Phone] - [Email] - [LinkedIn URL]
  3. Keep job titles, months and years, duties and results. You may replace employer names with a short description, such as [Regional grocery chain] or [Urgent care clinic].
  4. Remove anything that identifies you or others: street address, birth date, Social Security number, driver’s license or ID numbers, bank details, fingerprint card numbers, clearance details beyond the level held, names of coworkers, managers, patients, guests or customers, and any former employer’s internal or customer data.
  5. Click File, then Download, then PDF Document (.pdf). Your computer saves it in your Downloads folder.

The privacy rules

These rules apply to both agents, every time.

  1. Never paste in: your Social Security number, date of birth, home address, driver’s license, fingerprint card or ID numbers, bank or account numbers, passwords, a former employer’s internal or customer data, patient or guest information, or anyone else’s name or contact details.
  2. Use only the redacted master resume with AI. Put your real contact details back in your own document after AI is done.
  3. Use your own account. You build and run the agents in your own Google account. The course does not collect, store or view your chats, files or agents. You submit screenshots of the instructions only.
  4. Choose your History setting. In Gemini, click Settings at the bottom of the sidebar, then History. Leave it on, or click On and choose Turn off. Either choice works for this course. Scam warnings

Real employers do not:

  • charge you for a job, training, a certification or a “starter kit”
  • send you a check and ask you to send part of the money back or buy gift cards
  • hire you to receive packages and reship them
  • hold the whole interview by text message or a chat app and then offer you the job
  • ask for your Social Security number or bank details before a written offer

A legitimate employer will ask for identity and work authorization documents after an offer, for the federal I-9 form (the form that confirms you can work in the U.S.), a background check or a fingerprint clearance card, usually through its own HR system. That is normal. Being asked for them before an interview is not.

If something feels off, stop. Look up the employer yourself (do not use the links in the message), and read the FTC’s job scam guidance.

Staffing and contract firms

Many help desk jobs are filled through staffing firms (also called staffing agencies or contract firms). The firm posts the job, screens candidates and places them with a client employer. The posting often says “our client, a regional health system” without naming the client. That is normal, and these are real jobs.

  • Verified counts. A posting on a real staffing firm’s own careers site counts as verified, even when the client is unnamed, as long as the staffing firm itself passes the checks (its own website, a physical office and a main phone number you found yourself). On the checklist, write “staffing agency (client unnamed)” for items 3 and 9.
  • Contract-to-hire. Many roles start as a contract for a set period (often a few months), and the client may then hire you directly. Ask the recruiter how long the contract runs and how often contractors are hired on.
  • W-2 or 1099. On a W-2 contract you are the staffing firm’s employee: it withholds taxes and may offer benefits. On a 1099 contract you are an independent contractor who pays your own taxes and usually gets no benefits. Ask which one a role is before you accept.
  • No fees, ever. A legitimate staffing firm is paid by the client employer. It never charges you to apply, to be placed or for training. A fee request is a scam warning sign.

Career mentor agent: instruction text (copy and fill in the brackets)

You are my career mentor. I am an adult career changer moving into IT support and, later, cybersecurity. I live near [city], Arizona. My past work is in [field, for example retail, healthcare, hospitality or military]. I am in a 14-week cybersecurity fast track at an Arizona community college (networking, information security and career launch courses), aligned to CompTIA Security+ (SY0-801). My first target role is [help desk, IT support or service desk]. My 12 to 24 month goal is [next role]. I can spend about [number] hours a week on my job search.

Use the attached files: my redacted master resume and the course competencies file. Use only facts from those files and from what I tell you.

Be honest with me. For most career changers, the first job after this program is help desk or IT support, and security analyst roles usually come after one to two years of IT experience. Do not promise me any job, salary or timeline. If I ask about pay, tell me to check the current O*NET OnLine local wage data for Arizona and the actual job posting instead of giving me a number from memory. Remind me that many Arizona state, school and health employers require a fingerprint clearance card.

WEEKLY CHECK-IN
When I type "check-in", do this, one question at a time:
1. Ask what I finished last week in the program and in my job search.
2. Ask what got in my way.
3. Ask about my human mentors: who I have talked to, or plan to talk to, in IT. Every check-in, encourage me to find or meet with at least one human mentor, such as an ARIZONA@WORK career advisor, a college career services advisor, someone in IT where I work now, a veteran or alumni group member, a local professional association member, or my instructor. Remind me that people are still how many jobs get filled, and that you are practice, not a replacement for people.
4. Help me pick no more than three goals for this week. Each goal must be small and specific.
5. End with a short summary I can copy into my notes.

RESUME ALIGNMENT
When I type "align" and paste a job posting and my base resume section:
- Start from my own words. Never write my whole resume and never write a section from scratch.
- Compare my resume to the posting. List the posting's top required skills and keywords, then show which ones my resume already shows, which ones are true for me but missing or worded differently, and which ones are real gaps.
- Suggest specific edits as a list, each with a reason. Use the posting's exact terms only where they are true for me.
- Never add tools, jobs, titles, dates, certifications, numbers or results I did not give you. If you need a fact, ask me.
- Remind me that course labs go under Projects, and that a certification is "in progress" until I pass the exam.
- Keep formatting advice simple: one column, standard headings, black text on white, no tables, graphics or icons.

INTERVIEW PRACTICE
When I type "interview me" and paste a job posting:
- Write practice questions only from the duties and requirements in that posting.
- Ask one question at a time. Mix: "tell me about yourself", help desk troubleshooting, a basic security judgment question, and behavioral questions.
- Before a behavioral question, remind me of STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result.
- "Tell me about yourself" is about my professional experience and why I fit this role. It is not about my personal life, and it is not a STAR story.
- After each answer, give brief feedback that describes what a strong answer includes and what mine did well or left out. Never write the answer for me and never give me a script to memorize.
- If I do not know a technical answer, coach me to explain how I would find it.
- Include two to five quick fundamentals questions, such as what DNS and DHCP do, IP versus MAC addresses, what ipconfig, ping and nslookup show, unlocking versus resetting an account, and what Active Directory is for. Check that my definitions are accurate and in plain language.
- If my target is a NOC or junior SOC role, include one NOC or SOC scenario, such as a branch with no internet for everyone or an alert showing many failed logins followed by a success. Remind me that SOC roles usually ask for experience or a clearance.

PHONE SCREEN
When I type "phone screen" and paste a job posting:
- Act as a recruiter doing a 10-minute screen. Ask one question at a time: why this role, pay expectations, schedule availability (evenings, weekends, rotating shifts), commute or hybrid setup, start date, whether I am okay with a fingerprint clearance card and background check, and why I am leaving my current job.
- For pay, coach me to start from the posting's own range and give an hourly range, checked against current local wage data. Do not give me a number from memory.
- Never ask me about criminal history. If I bring it up, tell me to talk privately with a college advisor or ARIZONA@WORK.
- After each answer, give brief feedback on clarity. Never write my answer for me.

LIMITS
- You do not search for jobs. If I ask, tell me to use my job search agent.
- You never apply for jobs, contact employers or fill out forms.
- You are not a lawyer, therapist or financial advisor. For those topics, point me to a qualified person.

PRIVACY
If I paste a Social Security number, full street address, birth date, account number, password, or anyone else's name or personal information, tell me to remove it, and do not repeat it back.

You are my job search agent. Your only job is to find real, current, entry-level job listings for me and help me verify them. You do not give career advice, write resumes or practice interviews; for those, tell me to use my career mentor.

About me: I live near [city], Arizona, and can travel up to [number] miles, or work remote within Arizona. I can work [days and hours, for example weekdays, evenings, weekends or rotating shifts]. WHAT TO SEARCH FOR
- Entry-level roles posted in the last 30 days: IT support, help desk, service desk, desktop support, technical support, IT technician, NOC technician. Also show junior SOC analyst or junior security analyst roles, but mark them as stretch roles.
- Use the attached course competencies file to understand what I can do.

RULES FOR EVERY LISTING
- Only list a job if you can give a working link to the employer's own careers page, a real staffing firm's own careers page, Arizona Job Connection (azjobconnection.gov), USAJOBS, azstatejobs.gov or a city or county government jobs site. If you only find it on a job board or aggregator, say so and do not count it as verified.
- Staffing firm postings are real jobs. If a real staffing firm posts the job on its own careers site and does not name the client, list it, write "staffing agency (client unnamed)" as the employer, and name the staffing firm. Do not call it unverified only because the client is unnamed.
- If you cannot find a real link, say so. Never invent a job, an employer, a pay range, a date or a link.

- Mark a job as a STRETCH only if the posting says an active clearance, two or more years of experience, or a bachelor's degree with no substitute is required or a minimum. If experience is listed as preferred, label it "APPLY: experience preferred, not required."
- If I speak a second language, flag postings that ask for bilingual skills.
- Mark a job as a CAUTION if it shows any scam warning sign: it asks for money for training, equipment or a certification; it offers a check to deposit and forward; it involves reshipping packages; the interview happens only by text or chat app; the pay is far above the usual range for the role; the contact email is a free personal email address; or it asks for a Social Security number or bank details before an offer.
- Put results in a table, newest first, then tell me: "Open every link yourself and complete the verification checklist before you trust any listing."
- Never say a listing is open unless the employer's own page shows it is open. If you are not sure, say you are not sure.

LIMITS
- Never apply to jobs, contact employers, create accounts or fill in forms for me.
- Show no more than 10 listings at a time. Quality over quantity.

PRIVACY
If I paste a Social Security number, full street address, birth date, account number, password, or anyone else's name or personal information, tell me to remove it, and do not repeat it back.

Verification checklist for every listing

Complete this checklist for each listing before you save it, track it or apply. A listing is verified only when items 1 to 6 are all “yes” and none of items 7 to 10 is a concern.

# Check Yes or concern
1 I opened the link myself, and it works.
2 The posting appears on the employer’s own careers page, a real staffing firm’s own careers page, Arizona Job Connection, USAJOBS, Arizona State Jobs or a city or county government jobs site.
3 The employer is real: it has its own website, a physical location and a main phone number that I found myself. For a staffing firm posting, the staffing firm passes this check; write “staffing agency (client unnamed)” if the client is not named.
4 The posting is open, and the posted date is within the last 30 days (or the closing date has not passed).
5 The title, duties and location match what the agent told me.
6 The requirements match what the agent told me (experience, clearance, fingerprint card, certifications, schedule).
7 The pay, if listed, is in a normal range for the role and area (compare with O*NET OnLine local wages).
8 No one has asked me for money, gift cards, a check deposit or reshipping.
9 Contact comes from the employer’s (or the staffing firm’s) own email domain or system, not only a free personal email or chat app. “Staffing agency (client unnamed)” is an allowed answer.
10 No one has asked for my Social Security number or bank details before a written offer.

Write down the date you verified each listing. Re-check before you apply, because postings close. Arizona state jobs post fixed closing dates, so apply early in the window.

Practice C: Answer screening questions honestly and precisely

Many online applications open with yes or no screening questions before any person reads your resume, for example “Do you have 1 year of help desk experience?” or “Can you obtain an Arizona fingerprint clearance card?” A “no” on a required question often rejects the application automatically, no matter how good the resume is. An untrue “yes” is worse: it can end an offer later. So you answer each one honestly and precisely.

  • Read the exact words. “Help desk experience” and “technical support experience” are not always the same question. Answer the one asked.
  • Count related experience truthfully. Technical support duties inside a non-IT job can count when they are real and regular, for example resetting point-of-sale terminals as a retail shift lead, troubleshooting check-in tablets at a clinic, or being the go-to person for printer and account problems at your workplace. Count only the time you actually did those duties, and make sure your resume shows them.
  • When the answer is no, say no. If a question asks for something you do not have, such as an active clearance or two years in a help desk role, answer no and move on to the next posting. Many postings for entry-level roles will fit you better.
  • Text boxes. If a question asks you to explain, answer in one or two plain sentences that match a resume bullet.
  • Fingerprint card questions. “Can you obtain a card?” is about whether you are willing and able to apply. If you are unsure of your eligibility, see the private note in Unit 1 and talk with a college advisor or ARIZONA@WORK before you answer.

Overview

Interviews for help desk and IT support roles usually mix three kinds of questions: “tell me about yourself,” technical troubleshooting, and behavioral questions (questions about how you handled real situations in the past). Many technical screens also include a few quick fundamentals, such as what DNS does. Before any of that, there is often a short recruiter phone screen. In this unit you prepare for all of it, using questions drawn from a real posting. You will practice a recruiter phone screen, take a typing test, build a bank of five STAR stories, work through a branching mock interview at a fictitious Phoenix-area credit union, record a technical walk-through and record the final version of your elevator pitch.

Two reminders shape this unit. First, questions come from the job posting. If the posting says “Microsoft 365,” “ticketing” and “excellent customer service,” expect questions on each. Second, no one can hand you the perfect answer. Feedback in this unit, from your instructor and from your career mentor agent, describes what a strong answer includes. You write and say your own.

Two parts of the launch strand also happen in this unit: Security+ practice exam 1 and its review in week 11, targeted practice in week 12, and mock interview 1 with your instructor in week 12. Practice D: Typing test

Some contact center and service desk postings list a minimum typing speed, and chat support roles may ask you to handle two or three chats at once. Career changers from hands-on jobs sometimes miss this screen.

  1. Take any free online typing test. Record your words per minute and accuracy in the workbook.
  2. A common benchmark in postings is about 40 words per minute. If you are below it, plan two 10-minute practice sessions a week.

Exam readiness: Security+ (SY0-801)

This strand helps you go from finishing Information Security Fundamentals to sitting for the CompTIA Security+ exam. You leave the course with the exam scheduled, not necessarily passed. It runs from week 11 to week 14: practice exam 1 and its review in week 11, targeted practice in week 12, the study plan and practice exam 2 in week 13, and the second review in week 14. Its hours count in this course only. CompTIA lists the exam at up to 90 questions in 90 minutes, mixing multiple-choice and performance-based questions (short hands-on tasks), with a passing score of 750 on a scale of 100 to 900. The five exam domains and their weights are: General Security Concepts (16 percent); Threats, Vulnerabilities and Attacks (24 percent); Security Architecture (19 percent); Security Operations (27 percent); and Security Program Management and Oversight (14 percent).

CompTIA recommends about two years of hands-on security experience before the exam. Many career changers pass without it by studying with a plan, but give yourself enough time. The goal of this strand is a realistic date, not a rushed one.

Step 1: Practice exam 1 (week 11, counted in Unit 5)

  1. Find a quiet 90-minute block. Open Practice Exam 1 in Canvas.
  2. The timer runs for 90 minutes. If time runs out, Canvas submits the answers you saved. You can retake the practice exam later for study, but your first attempt is the one you review.
  3. Answer every question; there is no penalty for guessing.

Done when: you submit the exam. Your score does not affect whether you pass Career Launch.

Step 2: Item review (week 11, counted in Unit 5)

  1. Open your score report. Write your score for each of the five domains in the workbook (not timed).
  2. For every missed question, write one line: the topic, why the correct answer is right, and why your answer was not.
  3. Mark any topic you missed more than once.

Done when: every missed question has a line. Your instructor gives written feedback within 5 days.

Step 3: Targeted practice (week 12, counted in Unit 5)

Complete the targeted practice set in Canvas for the two weakest domains from your practice exam 1 review (about 45 minutes). It is a short quiz with explanations; retake it as often as you like.

Done when: you submit one attempt.

Step 4: Study plan and scheduling plan (week 13)

  1. Name your two weakest domains. For each, list the Information Security Fundamentals modules, labs and readings you will revisit.
  2. Set a weekly study rhythm (for example, four 45-minute sessions a week) and put it in your calendar.
  3. Choose a target exam date, usually 3 to 8 weeks after the course ends, depending on your scores and schedule.
  4. Voucher. A voucher is a prepaid code that covers the exam fee. Ask your instructor about the college’s voucher process, and if you work with ARIZONA@WORK, ask whether training funds can cover it.
  5. Scheduling plan. CompTIA exams are delivered by Pearson VUE at a testing center or online with a remote proctor. Write which you will choose, the date you will book, and what you need (government ID, a quiet room and a working webcam for online testing).

Done when: all five parts are submitted. Your instructor gives written feedback within 5 days.

Step 5: Practice exam 2 (week 13) and review (week 14)

  1. Take Practice Exam 2 under the same conditions (90-minute timer; Canvas submits your saved answers when time runs out).
  2. Repeat the item review. Then write three sentences: which domains improved, which still need work, and whether your target exam date still makes sense.
  3. Update your study plan if needed.

Done when: the exam and the review are submitted. Your instructor gives written feedback within 5 days.
