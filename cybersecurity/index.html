Cybersecurity Fast Track

Learning Design · Case study

OverviewProgramSecurity CourseCareer LaunchWorkforce PellReview

A design study exploring what a Workforce Pell-eligible program could look like: a prototype 14-week pathway into an Arizona community college’s cybersecurity programs, with career preparation and Security+ exam readiness integrated across all fourteen weeks.

Goal

Design a prototype of a 14-week fast track built to Workforce Pell requirements into an Arizona community college’s existing cybersecurity pathway. Two existing courses from the college’s cybersecurity AAS, redesigned for an accelerated cohort, and a new 1-credit career course, proposed through curriculum review with its own competencies, 7 credits in all, run as one online cohort and package into a short certificate that a career changer can finish in a semester and stack into the degree. Workforce Pell requires 70% completion and 70% placement. Through 2028–29 any job counts; from 2029–30 completers must be working in the target or a comparable occupation. So job search, interviewing and Security+ exam readiness run through all 14 weeks instead of waiting for the end.

The project is an experiment in what a Workforce Pell-eligible program would require, from federal and state compliance to course design and placement. It has not been implemented or piloted with learners.

Audience

WhoWhat they need
LearnersWorking adults in the Phoenix area changing careers from retail, health care, hospitality or the military, many with no degree and no IT background, who need a first IT job within months, not years.
CollegeA community college with an established cybersecurity certificate and AAS, and an industry advisory committee that includes cybersecurity professionals from federal and private-sector employers, that needs a short award fitting the federal rule and Arizona’s draft state policy.
EmployersArizona employers hiring help desk, service desk and junior security operations staff, who want people who can troubleshoot, write a clear ticket and talk to a frustrated user.

Process

StepWhat happened
ResearchSynthetic experts researched the brief in parallel: a cybersecurity subject matter expert, an instructional designer, a Workforce Pell policy researcher and an IT help desk hiring manager. Each cited primary sources, from the federal final rule and Arizona’s draft policy to Phoenix wage data and official course outlines.
Program designThe first plan was 12 credits. At about 38 hours of work a week, that would push out the working adults the program is for, so it dropped to 7 credits (about 22 to 25 hours a week): Introduction to Networking in weeks 1 to 7, Information Security Fundamentals in weeks 8 to 14, and a 1-credit career and exam readiness course across all 14.
Course buildsThe security course was redesigned from its official competencies as a 7-week accelerated version, with open textbooks and government sources vetted for license, currency and published reviews where they exist, weekly interaction and Security+ mapping. In practice this redesign starts from the faculty’s existing course shell. The career course is the one new course and was built in full. The networking course is outlined, not redesigned.
Adversarial reviewThe designer and the subject matter expert attacked each draft, a synthetic learner read every unit for friction, and the hiring manager checked the career course against how help desk candidates are actually screened. Each review changed the course.
Human reviewThe review packet lists everything a human cybersecurity expert and the college’s program director must confirm before a learner sees any of it.

What to Look At

  • Information Security Fundamentals: the full seven-week course, with weekly lessons, labs, open readings and two branching scenarios.
  • Career Launch: the full 14-week career and Security+ readiness course, where learners build their own AI job search and mentor agents.
  • Program: how the three courses fit together, week by week, and how they stack into the degree.
  • Workforce Pell: the federal and Arizona rules and the evidence file a reviewer will ask for.

Technology

  • Canvas, with Canvas Studio for captioned video.
  • Articulate Rise 360 for branching scenarios, published into Canvas.
  • Google Gemini, which learners use to build their own job search and career mentor agents.
  • Open-licensed labs from the course’s open textbook, run in a hosted virtual lab environment.
  • A synthetic expert panel run through Claude, directed and reviewed at every step.

Status

Prototype. The program plan, the security course and Career Launch are drafted and reviewed by the synthetic panel. Next are the Rise scenarios, the instructor videos and review by human cybersecurity faculty.
