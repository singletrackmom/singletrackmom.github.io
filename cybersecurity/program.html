Two existing community college courses and a new 1-credit career course, 7 credits, one online cohort: how they fit together, what they cost and how they stack into the degree. Part of a prototype program design.

Hours follow the college’s credit-hour policy, which applies the federal definition (34 CFR 600.2), at about 45 hours of learner work per credit. The whole cohort meets live once a week for 90 minutes (recorded, with a graded alternative for anyone who cannot attend), and each week opens on Monday with a new module.

Why Seven Credits

The first plan packaged four courses, 12 credits, adding a Linux course in the first half. Twelve credits in 14 weeks is about 38 hours of work a week, a full-time load that only works for learners who are not working. The people this fast track is for are mostly working, so the plan dropped to 7 credits. That still sits inside the federal range for a credit-hour Workforce Pell program (at least 4 semester hours, 8 to 14 weeks of instruction), and both technical courses already appear in the college’s cybersecurity AAS. They keep their official competencies and credits; only their pacing and delivery change. The career course is new. It goes through curriculum review with its own official competencies and is added to the AAS, so all 7 credits stack and the program keeps the credit-to-clock exemption. The 12 months of operation count only while every course counts in full toward the AAS (34 CFR 668.8(k)(2); 690.94(a)(1)). If a 1-credit special projects section counts in full toward the AAS, the first cohorts can use it and the clock starts with them; switching to the approved course later is a modification the college reports. That section must meet the special projects course’s official competencies and be taught by faculty qualified for it. If no such section exists, the clock starts with the first cohort after Career Launch is in the AAS.

The trade-off is real. The security course lists a Linux course as a prerequisite and a Windows or hardware support course as a corequisite. A fast track that skips them needs a written program policy from the program director, applied to every learner in the cohort, not a case-by-case waiver. The alternative is to keep the Linux course and accept the heavier load.

Why a Career Course

Workforce Pell limits a program’s length and also holds the college accountable for outcomes. At least 70% of learners must finish within 150% of the published length, and at least 70% must be employed in the second quarter after they leave, measured in administrative data such as state wage records; from 2029–30 only completers working in the target or a comparable occupation count. A program that teaches the technical content well but leaves learners to find jobs on their own is betting its eligibility on something it does not teach.

Why a separate credit course

  • The technical courses have no room. Their official competencies already fill their credit hours. Adding resume, interview and exam preparation to them would push out required security and networking content.
  • Its outcomes are different. Career Launch has its own competencies: evaluating entry-level roles and pay, translating prior experience, building and verifying AI job search agents, producing screen-ready materials, interviewing and preparing for Security+. None of these belongs to an existing course, which is why it needs its own approval.
  • Required work counts; optional work does not. The US Department of Education counts toward program length all instruction required to complete the program as approved and offered, and coursework that is not required cannot count toward a learner’s Pell eligibility. A required credit course counts; an optional workshop does not, and it would reach the learners who need it least and would leave placement to chance.
  • Credit makes it accountable. A credit course has a named instructor, graded deliverables, written feedback and regular interaction, the same distance education standard as the technical courses, and its results show up in the program’s completion and placement data.
  • It stays small. One credit is about 45 hours across 14 weeks, about 3 hours a week and about $97 in tuition. It keeps the program at 7 credits, well inside the federal range, and adds the least cost to the value-added earnings test.

What employers get

  • Candidates who have practiced the screens employers use: knockout questions, a recruiter phone screen with an honest pay answer, technical quick checks and a typing test.
  • Readable ticket notes written to a standard: who, what was requested, how identity was verified, what was done, the result and when to escalate.
  • A mock interview scored by an employer panel on technical fundamentals, troubleshooting, security judgment and customer service tone.
  • Referrals through an instructor who knows each completer’s work.
  • Honest credentials: completers leave with Security+ scheduled, not necessarily passed, and their resumes list only what they have done.

Learners build their own AI job search and career mentor agents in it, so the job search continues after the program ends, when the placement measure is actually taken.

Weeks 11 and 12 are the heaviest, about 25 hours, because the first practice exam and the first mock interview land there. They were moved out of weeks 13 and 14 so they don’t collide with the security course capstone. The learner schedule says so on day one, and a pilot with real novices sets the final estimates.

Week 14 is the last content week of the security course (governance, risk, compliance and AI risks), plus the capstone, oral defenses, the employer panel, the practice exam 2 review and every makeup. Nothing is scheduled for the cohort after week 14, because the US Department of Education counts any week with scheduled work or instructor support as a week of instruction and the limit is fewer than 15. An individual learner with an incomplete may finish within 21 weeks; this cannot become the norm.

The fast track skips the Windows corequisite, and Windows account work is most of the day-one help desk job. So the security course’s identity week outlines a help desk lab in Windows and Microsoft 365: four tickets (an account lockout, a lost MFA device, a group request that needs approval and a password reset for a caller who cannot be verified) and a standard for writing ticket notes. The lab is outlined in this prototype and gets built and timed in the pilot. It does not replace a full Windows course; Active Directory domains and Group Policy come later in the AAS.

Security+ Alignment

The security course’s official competencies cover most of the Security+ ground (authentication, access control, cryptography, PKI, threats, hardening, remote access, wireless, infrastructure, risk and policy), but the outline does not name the exam. So the career course carries the exam work: a map from each security course week to the CompTIA Security+ V8 (SY0-801) objectives, two timed practice exams, a study plan and a scheduled exam date. SY0-801 launched November 17, 2026, and the older SY0-701 is scheduled to retire in 2027 (confirm the date with CompTIA), so this cohort prepares for V8. The mapping is on the security course page.

How It Stacks

Earn and learnIn the final semesters of the AAS, learners can look for paid security operations internships, an earn-and-learn step after the fast track, not part of it.
TransferThe AAS connects to university degrees in cybersecurity and applied computing through existing transfer agreements.

Target Jobs

The honest first job is help desk. Computer user support specialists (SOC 15-1232) in the Phoenix area earn a median of about $62,310, and about $45,050 at the 10th percentile, per O*NET local wage data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Network support (15-1231), network administration (15-1244) and information security analyst (15-1212) are the 12 to 24 month next steps, and the course says so plainly instead of promising a security analyst job in 14 weeks. Some Arizona employers, including state agency help desks, also require a fingerprint clearance card, which the career course covers.

Recent Phoenix-area help desk postings start around $18 to $25 an hour, which the career course uses; the 10th, 25th and median figures above are about $21.66, $24.10 and $29.96 an hour.

The program does not cover every day-one help desk task. Outlook, Teams and OneDrive support, printers, remote support tools, hardware and imaging, and ticket priority and service levels are learned on the job or in later AAS courses, and the career course teaches learners to say so honestly in interviews.

Cost

At typical Arizona community college in-state tuition of about $97 a credit, 7 credits is about $679 before fees, a Security+ voucher and lab access. Open textbooks keep book costs near zero. Published tuition and fees may not exceed the program’s value-added earnings, which the US Department of Education calculates; until then Arizona applies an interim estimate.
